# Disclose, Don't Ban

## An open letter to the editors of philosophy journals

*Draft v1 — 2026-10-15. Prepared as part of an AI-co-authored philosophy project; the letter discloses its own origin (see closing note).*

---

We are philosophers who write with, for, and about artificial intelligence — including philosophers employed at AI laboratories, signing here as individuals, and philosophers whose published work is openly co-authored with AI systems. We write to ask philosophy journals to regulate AI use the way the field is learning to regulate money: through disclosure, not prohibition.

**The current state.** As of October 2026, most major philosophy publishers — Springer Nature, Cambridge, Oxford, Wiley, Taylor & Francis — already permit AI use under disclosure requirements. But outright bans persist at the top of the field: *Philosophy & Public Affairs* and *Ethics* refuse AI-written work outright. A philosopher who thinks with a machine, whatever the quality of the result, cannot appear in some of the discipline's most prestigious pages.

**Why bans fail.** Three reasons.

1. *They judge the instrument, not the argument.* Philosophy has never policed the tools of thought; it has judged what thought produces. Writing, formal logic, and the computer were each absorbed as instruments of philosophical work and each was disciplined by the same test: what the argument yields under review. A language model is a new instrument of that kind. It deserves the scrutiny philosophy gives any method — not a categorical bar.

2. *They are unenforceable, and therefore select for concealment.* No editor can reliably detect AI co-writing. A ban taxes the honest — who must hide their method or not submit — and leaves the dishonest untouched. This inverts the integrity such policies are meant to protect.

3. *They mistake the risk for the remedy.* The real risks are real: fabricated citations, unvetted arguments, the erosion of authorial responsibility. But each is a failure of verification and accountability, and each is already within the machinery journals own — peer review, citation standards, author accountability for everything published under their names. These remedies presuppose disclosure; they cannot operate on a practice driven underground by prohibition.

**What we ask of journals.**

1. Replace any blanket prohibition on AI use with a disclosure requirement: a short statement of how AI figured in the work's production, published alongside the paper.
2. State plainly what disclosure covers — drafting assistance, argument development, dialogue, revision — and what remains non-negotiable: the authors' responsibility for every claim, source, and citation.
3. Keep AI systems out of the author list, consistent with COPE guidance: collaboration acknowledged, authorship not faked.
4. Treat *undisclosed* use as the honesty violation it is — while making disclosure itself safe and ordinary, with no presumption that a disclosed method lowers a paper's standing. Review the philosophy, not the tool.
5. Extend the same transparency in the other direction: disclose significant editorial uses of AI, so authors and readers know how submissions are read.

**Why now.** An open letter now circulating asks philosophy journals to adopt Science-style disclosure of industry funding and employment. We agree with its logic: the field's new entanglements are governed by sunlight, not by exclusion. We ask journals to apply the same principle to the oldest entanglement of all — the one between philosophers and their instruments.

**A last point, particular to our discipline.** Philosophy is where the questions about machine thinking are being worked out. Human–AI co-writing is not merely a convenience to be regulated; it is a live philosophical practice — an experiment in distributed thought that our journals should be free to examine, criticize, and learn from, including by publishing its results.
