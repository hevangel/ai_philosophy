# Gettier and the Analysis of Knowledge
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the Gettier problem; the tripartite analysis of knowledge*

## The question
When philosophers analyse knowledge, they usually mean *knowledge that* — knowing that some proposition is true — as distinct from *knowledge of* (being acquainted with a person or thing) and *knowledge how* (an ability, like riding a bike). The traditional analysis says knowledge that is *justified true belief*: you know that p when p is true, you believe it, and you are justified in believing it. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper arguing that these three conditions, even when all satisfied, are not enough — a belief can be true and justified while its truth is essentially luck, and lucky true belief is not knowledge. What more does knowledge require?

## The 90-second version
Gettier does not argue that you can know without truth, belief, or justification. He argues the other way: that having all three is not *sufficient*. His example: Smith and Jones are rivals for a job. Smith has counted exactly ten coins in Jones's pocket, and the company president has told Smith that Jones will get the job. So Smith justifiably believes: *the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket*. But the president was wrong — Smith himself gets the job. And, unknown to Smith, Smith also has ten coins in his own pocket. The belief is true; it is justified; yet it is true only by a coincidence that has nothing to do with Smith's reasons. It is not true *for the reasons Smith thinks*. So justified true belief, here, is not knowledge — and most philosophers, the lecture reports, agree that such a belief is not knowledge. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [The Gettier Problem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEvDQc0GciQ).)

## Positions

### The tripartite analysis — the target
Knowledge that p = believing p, p being true, and being justified in believing p. In the Carneades lectures' terms, a knower must hold a belief that is true and *warranted*, where exactly what the warrant consists in "can vary from one philosopher to another" ([The Three Kinds of Knowledge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=59RMe5ccT7o)). This analysis is the background Gettier's argument takes for granted in order to test its sufficiency.

### Gettier's counterexample argument
Gettier's paper itself is in this corpus (book 007, ch20), but the fetched copy is corrupted and unreadable — binary garbage after its title page (see the pilot README). The reconstruction below is therefore of the argument as the Carneades lecture presents it, and is labelled accordingly.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades lecture, "The Gettier Problem," presenting Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", 1963 — formalized):
- P1. Smith is justified in believing that Jones will get the job (the company president told him so) and has seen that Jones has exactly ten coins in his pocket.
- P2. Smith therefore justifiably believes the proposition: *the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket*.
- P3. In fact Smith himself gets the job, and Smith himself — unknown to him — has ten coins in his pocket. So the proposition Smith believes is true.
- P4. But its truth is disconnected from Smith's justification: it is not true because Jones (who has the coins) got the job; it is true by a double coincidence.
- ∴ C. Smith has a justified true belief that is not knowledge. Justified true belief is therefore not sufficient for knowledge; something more is required.

**Objections and replies as staged in the corpus** — book 007's "Analysis of Knowledge" section places two responses immediately after Gettier: Richard Feldman, "An Alleged Defect in Gettier Counter-Examples" (ch21), and John Pollock, "The Gettier Problem" (ch22). In this corpus both are citation stubs — titles and citations only, no text — so their arguments cannot be faithfully stated here. What can be said is only that the anthology's own staging treats the search for a defect in the counterexamples, and then a systematic treatment of the problem, as the debate's next moves.

### Russell's earlier version of the point: true belief through a false premise
Half a century before Gettier, Russell had already denied that true belief is knowledge when it arrives by a bad route. If a man believes that the late Prime Minister's last name began with a B, he believes truly — the late Prime Minister was Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman. But if he believes it *because* he thinks Mr Balfour was the late Prime Minister, "this belief, though true, would not be thought to constitute knowledge." Likewise a newspaper that luckily announces a battle's result before any telegram arrives produces true belief in its readers, but not knowledge.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XIII, "Knowledge, Error, and Probable Opinion"):
- P1. A true belief is not knowledge when it is deduced from a false belief.
- P2. Nor is it knowledge when it is deduced by a fallacious process from true premises (knowing all Greeks are men and Socrates was a man does not let you know Socrates was a Greek).
- P3. Nor is it enough that the premises be true and the deduction valid: the premises must be *known*.
- ∴ C. Knowledge requires the right pedigree: Russell's positive proposal is *derivative knowledge* — belief validly deduced from premises known *intuitively* — with the concession that psychological inference (as in reading print and passing directly to its meaning) counts where a valid logical connection is discoverable on reflection.

**How far this gets** — Russell's diagnosis covers cases where a false premise or a fallacy sits in the chain. It is faithful to his text to say he identified the bad-route phenomenon and built his account of knowledge around it; his text does not pose Gettier's general question about epistemic luck, and he himself warns that knowledge "is not a precise conception: it merges into 'probable opinion'." His chapter's closing criterion for sorting beliefs is coherence among probable opinions — a test that increases probability but "never gives absolute certainty."

### The distinction that frames the problem (Russell; the Three Kinds lecture)
Russell's ch. V divides knowledge of things into *acquaintance* — direct awareness "without the intermediary of any process of inference," as with sense-data, remembered data, introspected thoughts and feelings, and universals — and *knowledge by description*, where we know that there is exactly one object answering to a description ("the physical object which causes such-and-such sense-data") without being acquainted with it. The lecture's three kinds — knowledge that, knowledge of, knowledge how — cut the same territory differently, and its masked-man paradox shows why the division matters: you *know* your father (knowledge of), yet may not *know that* the hooded man approaching is your father; the paradox trades on equivocating between the two. The Gettier problem concerns only the propositional kind: whatever acquaintance and know-how are, they are not justified true belief, and are not threatened by his cases.

## Thought experiments
- **Smith, Jones, and the ten coins** (Gettier, via the lecture). Setup as in the 90-second version above. It is meant to show that the truth of a justified belief can be accidental in a way incompatible with knowledge; it pressures any analysis that lists conditions without asking how the belief's truth connects to its justification.
- **The masked man** ([The Three Kinds of Knowledge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=59RMe5ccT7o)). A hooded man approaches: you do not know who he is; he turns out to be your father, whom you know well. You both know and do not know him — until the senses of "know" are separated. It pressures sloppy use of "know" across the knowledge-of / knowledge-that boundary.
- **The premature battle report** (Russell, ch. XIII). A newspaper correctly announces a result before any report has arrived; its readers believe truly and even, in a thin sense, reasonably — but nobody calls it knowledge. It pressures the same sufficiency intuition as Gettier's case, by luck rather than by false premise.

## Where the debate stands
In the sources available to this corpus, the negative verdict is the settled part: justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge — the lecture reports that most philosophers agree a belief like Smith's is not knowledge, and Russell had already excluded true belief arrived at by false premises or fallacies. The positive part — what to add to or substitute for the three conditions — is *not* settled in these sources, and this entry does not pretend otherwise: the anthology's own continuation of the debate (Feldman, Pollock) exists here only as citations, and Russell's remedy (derivation from intuitively known premises) is avowedly imprecise at its edges. The Gettier structure also travels: the Carneades series uses the same luck-diagnosis against the cogito in "Doubting 'I Think'," where the belief "I am thinking" is imagined to be caused by the wrong sensation entirely.
