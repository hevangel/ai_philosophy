# Dr Li Tianming on the Key to Thinking (李天命博士論思考的關鍵）

*English translation. Source: extraction file `ch08 - 導引篇 李天命博士論思考的關鍵.md`, book 002 — Li Tianming, 《李天命的思考藝術》 (*The Art of Thinking*), Introductory Part. Interview by the Philosophy Department Organizing Committee for the CUHK Open Day; originally published in the* Philosophy Department Exhibition Handbook*, CUHK Open Day, 1990.*

---

Under the present education system, thinking methods can be said to be neglected in the secondary curriculum; more formal training in thinking methods is generally available only in tertiary education. Our Committee specially invited Dr Li Tianming to give a short interview on the issues involved.

Dr Li points out that thinking roughly comprises two large categories. One is **critical thinking （批判思考）** — thinking that discriminates true from false and judges right from wrong. The other is **creative thinking （創意思考）** — the thinking carried out when putting forward new ideas. Human survival depends on apt thinking. If we confuse truth with fallacy and do not know how to distinguish right from wrong — for instance, believing that poison is edible and beneficial to the body — survival itself is endangered; and if life is lost, there is no question of creating or inventing anything. In this respect, critical thinking is the foundation, and creative thinking is the next step (a further step). What general thinking-method courses teach is mainly basic thinking method, that is, the methods of critical thinking, and there is good reason for this: one must "learn to walk before learning to run."

We asked Dr Li whether thinking-method courses should be introduced earlier, at the secondary stage. His answer was affirmative: he holds that some teaching materials on basic thinking methods could be added to the senior secondary curriculum, so that secondary students adapt more easily when they enter university, and so that secondary students who have no chance to enter university can also receive training in thinking methods and improve their ability to think independently.

We also asked Dr Li whether, with Hong Kong facing the 1997 question, thinking methods have a special importance. Dr Li said that the importance of thinking methods is not confined to the present special environment; it has a universal significance. Modern people tend toward democracy — that is the tide of history — and one element of democracy is parliamentary politics. Debate, and discussing matters on their merits, belong to the character and essence of a parliament. If people lack the ability to distinguish right from wrong and are not equipped with methods of independent thinking, then even if there is a representative system it will be a mere outward shell, formal without much substance. Since thinking methods are so important, we asked Dr Li to state the key points of basic thinking method. Dr Li pointed out: **the most important thing is to know how to aptly raise the two questions "What does it mean?" and "What are the grounds for it?"**

To judge whether a thought or a statement is right or wrong, one must first know what that thought or statement *means*, and then work out what *grounds* it has.

Of course, questions about "meaning" and about "grounds" must not be raised boundlessly and indiscriminately. If the meaning is already clear enough, there is no need to ask "What does it mean?" If the grounds are already solid enough, there is no need to ask "What are the grounds for it?" But on the other hand, so long as one is not nitpicking, being good at raising these two questions — "What does it mean?" and "What are the grounds for it?" — and acquiring the thinking habit of asking them, is precisely to cultivate an **"alertness of critical thinking" （批判思考的警覺性）** — and this can be called the most important key to learning basic thinking method.
