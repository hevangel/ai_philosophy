## Preface

The ideal of the educator is to inspire people's thinking; the ambition of the would-be manipulator is to replace people's thinking.

Fools know only how to receive the *guànshū* — the indoctrination — of thoughts; the wise prize command of the *methods* of thinking. To mull over problems haphazardly is like trying to catch the face of the wind with a spider's web: in the end, nothing is gained.

Correct methods of thinking are like a storm lantern in a desolate night.

To carry your own lantern and light up the unknown road ahead — that is what is called independent thinking.

## Part I (the main treatment): Semantic Analysis

In the struggle for survival, one necessary condition for a living creature to survive is: to have a way of finding food, while avoiding becoming someone else's food.

Human beings have neither the wings of birds nor the claws and fangs of lions and tigers; they can be called a very fragile animal. Yet in the natural world of the weak serving as meat for the strong, they have come out on top and become the spirit of all creatures. On what do they rely? Mainly on their ability to think.

One of the most precious human abilities is the ability to think. One of the most remarkable features of the ability to think is that it can think about thinking itself. And one of the most important fruits of thinking about thinking is the methodology of thinking.

What follows starts from the most basic department of the "methodology of thinking."

### I. An instrument for sharpening thought: the first link of methodology

Language is the principal tool of thinking — and not only the principal tool of thinking. From propagating doctrines to dragging in irrelevancies, from enacting constitutions and laws to vows of eternal love, everything depends on the use of language. Without language, what would our world become?

Without language, we could not lie and could not curse, but neither could we speak truly nor crack jokes. Without language, our world would become like a dead-silent domain of ghosts (supposing the ghosts there are all mute), and human beings, like other animals, would find it hard to accumulate the experience and wisdom of generation after generation and hand them down to later ages. Without language, there would be no philosophy and no religion: no dialogues of Socrates, no teachings of Confucius; Śākyamuni could not preach the Dharma and Jesus could not preach the Gospel. Without language there would be no mathematics, physics, medicine, engineering, and so on — that is, no science; and no poetry, prose, novels, drama, and so on — that is, no literature. For the individual, without language there would be no thinking — at least not the great majority of thinking, or at most only the most primitive thinking. Between people, without language there would be no communication of ideas — at least no deep and fine communication of ideas, or at most only very simple communication of ideas. In a word, without language there would be none of today's civilisation and culture, and human beings would not have their present material and spiritual life.

All this shows how important a tool of thinking and communication language is. Yet many people not only fail to make good use of this extremely precious tool, but spoil it, misuse and abuse it, even use it fraudulently: distorting the meanings of words, speaking ambiguously, and mystifying until nothing intelligible is said… All of this is "verbal muddling" （言辭胡混）.

Verbal muddling very easily makes thought confused and unclear, breeding errors, and thereby leads to pointless disputes and needless conflicts, including serious religious and political conflicts. How can these confusions and errors of thought be eliminated, and the unnecessary strife they cause avoided? To treat these ills and dissolve these problems, we must clarify the words concerned and analyse clearly the meaning （意義） of the key concepts or key terms in the problem. To handle a problem by clarifying meanings or concepts — that is the basic approach of **semantic analysis** (Linguistic–Conceptual Analysis).

Using semantic analysis to solve philosophical problems is, as Schlick, founder of logical positivism, put it, "the turning point in philosophy" — the turning point from traditional to contemporary philosophy; its concrete expression formed an intellectual movement called the "philosophical revolution." The central idea of this movement and the various schools derived from it are collectively called "analytic philosophy," recognised as the mainstream of philosophy in contemporary America, Britain, Australia, Canada, and other English-speaking countries. Broadly speaking, semantic analysis is precisely the common instrument shared by all schools of analytic philosophy, whichever school they belong to.

This essay, and my work *Thinking and the Mind-Demons* (the latter being primary), attempt to refine, purify, expand, and develop semantic analysis, making it independent of any philosophical school — detached from the rise and fall of analytic philosophy as a movement — and even to go beyond the whole domain of philosophy, so that it can be applied to all intellectual problems and not only within philosophy's scope. This is not to say that semantic analysis can solve every problem; only that, at a minimum, it can provide the basis for thinking about a problem's solution, if the problem is not meaningless; and if a problem is meaningless, semantic analysis can dissolve it at its root.

For example, take the question "Is Vietnam a democratic country?" If we discuss it following the "habitual way of thinking" people use every day, we usually answer directly "yes" or "no," and disputes and even conflicts between the two sides often start there. But if we think about the question through the approach of semantic analysis, we first clarify the question and analyse clearly what the key word "democracy" means in that question. Once the meaning of "democracy" is settled, we can proceed to research and investigation to see whether Vietnam is a democratic country. Such research and investigation do not fall within the scope of semantic analysis, yet semantic analysis is the prerequisite for such work to proceed effectively — it can be called the methodological cornerstone that makes such work possible.

The question "Is Vietnam a democratic country?" is not a meaningless question. But a question like "Was such-and-such a matter dialectically decided by dialectical factors?" is meaningless. This can be explained as follows.

Let us imagine a "great leader" who leads the people in enthusiastically creating "super-scientific fields," proclaiming a yield of one million catties per mu, with the result that the enemy laughs itself half to death. The leader then decides that next time he will proclaim two million catties per mu, hoping thereby to annihilate the enemy by making them laugh themselves to complete extermination. A court poet at the leader's side immediately writes a poem in support:

Red sun / living immortal / retribution has finally come / God summons them — our enemies will, within half a year, be summoned by their God.

Sadly, many years pass and the enemy is still laughing itself half to death, not completely dead. (The leader is consequently downcast, loses his appetite, and after three months his weight is halved. The masses hurriedly write a joint letter congratulating the leader on his successful weight loss — a good start; and a good start is half of success — hoping he will continue dieting for another three months, in which case he will surely obtain the other half of the success and lose the other half of his weight as well. Whether the masses learned to reason in this marvellous way from the leader's good teaching is not the question we are asking now.) The question we are asking now is: how to explain that the enemy, deemed certain to die, laughs yet does not die? Answer: "That the enemy has not died was dialectically decided by dialectical factors."

But what do the so-called "dialectical factors" mean? And what does "dialectically decided" mean? Today, the word "dialectical" has become a much-abused and misused word whose scope of application stretches and shrinks at will and can be changed arbitrarily. There is no boundary and no criterion for what counts as dialectical and what does not. Sophists can, according to "the needs of the situation," manufacture at will terms like "a dialectical X" and "dialectically Y." Once these "dialectical terms" have been "dialectically created," they can be used to mystify, to bluff, to intimidate, and to muddle through, manufacturing specious doctrines.

Take the passage about the leader's weight loss above: if you point out that the passage is irrelevant to the theme and ask me why I inserted it, and I were to "reply dialectically," I would give this "dialectical answer": "Because that passage has a dialectical necessity for the theme." But what do "to reply dialectically" and "dialectical necessity" mean? The answer is — ask Heaven; Heaven knows.

In sum, in that sort of use, wholly without fixed standards, the word "dialectical" is merely a meaningless sophistical term (see the further discussion below).

In these circumstances, questions like "Was such-and-such a matter dialectically decided by dialectical factors?" are all meaningless.

Meaningless questions do not admit of being answered or not answered. And meaningless statements are neither true nor false. When we can recognise a statement as true or as false, we already know what it means. In short, a meaningless statement is "not even false" — that is, it lacks even the qualification of being "false." For example, the meaningless sentence "The reason dentists wear masks was dialectically decided by certain dialectical factors" cannot even be called "false." The sentence is even inferior to a statement that inquiry can determine to be false, such as: "The reason dentists wear masks is that they are afraid of being hit by patients."

On the basis of the above, because meaningless questions do not admit of answers and meaningless statements are neither true nor false, the road-clearing work of clarifying "what a question or theory means / whether it has meaning" is more basic than the truth-seeking work of "finding answers to questions and appraising the credibility of theories." Of course, this is so only as a matter of methodological order; it does not mean the latter work is less important than the former. In fact both kinds of work are very important.

The methodology of thinking (or "methodology" for short) mainly comprises four departments: semantic analysis, logical method (deductive logic), scientific method, and the analysis of fallacies. Among all the departments of methodology, semantic analysis is the first link — its most basic part. For when we think about a problem, in principle our first step must be to know what the problem asks, that is, we must first understand the problem's meaning. If even the meaning of the problem has not been determined, we fundamentally have no way to set about solving it (including problems like "Has such-and-such a statement committed a fallacy?"), and of course no way to set about applying logical method, scientific method, or fallacy analysis to seek the problem's answer. In this regard we can say: semantic analysis is the starting point, or the first link, of the methodology of thinking.

### II. Meaning and clarification

The introduction to semantic analysis above shows that the great use of this instrument for sharpening thought lies in the clarification of meaning — clarifying problems, analysing clearly the key terms or key concepts, making our thought clear.

But some people oppose clarity of thought. The British philosopher F. Waismann once said: "Clarity is the last refuge of those who have nothing to say." [Note 3] To this one may reply: "Unclarity is even more the last refuge of those who have nothing to say."

Clear statements all have (at least) this common merit: if such statements have defects, their defects are relatively the easiest to see. Conversely, obscure and unclear statements all have (at least) this common fault: they make it hard to tell whether they have other faults — for example, hard to tell whether they have committed fallacies.

#### (A) Taking obscurity for profundity

Regrettably, many people overlook the point just made and hold the mistaken idea that ambiguity and obscurity are profundity; as a result they are easily intimidated by such words. Those who play word games have sized up exactly this psychological weakness and use ambiguous, obscure words to lay a smokescreen and fake profundity. If you bluntly accuse him of talking nonsense, he will fly into a rage from shame. If you leave yourself room and say you do not understand what he said, he will laugh at you for being shallow. But if, against your conscience, you pretend to understand what he said, he will instead become suspicious of you — because he himself does not actually know what what he said means.

Speech that is muddled and unclear (whose extreme is complete meaninglessness) is a very common phenomenon — in philosophy, religion, political discourse, cultural criticism, social science, arts commentary, and fields like "feng shui and fortune-telling" alike. As the psychologist R. H. Thouless pointed out: "We may explain this phenomenon as the product of authors' laziness and incompetence; but we may also say that it is the best shortcut to winning academic reputation… the more muddled and obscure the speaker is, the more the listener admires him… books of this kind are far more numerous than ordinary readers can imagine." [Note 4] Take one example and let us appreciate this piece of writing (not fabricated, but actually recorded):

"If this scientific posture cannot be learned-to-be-explained from within the discourse internal to psychiatry, then the development of this posture must, following some other conditions external to the discourse, be produced, be constituted. Since psychiatry from the start affirms this 'scientific' posture of defining madness in a special form, to study these constitutive conditions we must draw the angle of analysis and the line of sight away from the discipline, to study some complex yet at the same time 'contingent' conditions of existence. Since psychiatry from the start deliberately uses a certain method to understand madness, this method of course cannot be considered from within the discipline, but should be established under the action of some social, cultural, and material conditions outside the discipline that have no necessary relation to the discipline. This is what is called the 'archaeology of knowledge.'" [Note 5]

Would you have the nerve to say you know what this passage means? It is said to introduce certain ideas of the structuralist or "postmodernist" Foucault. This kind of "-ism" is already notoriously ambiguous and obscure, and this introductory passage is even more extremely "profound": the reason it was "produced" is presumably "due to its being established under the action of certain deceptive, dishonestly copy-and-translate psychological conditions, complex yet at the same time contingent, external to the constitutive conditions of being learned-to-be-explained, yet internal to the scientific-posture psychiatry that specially-form-defines madness." Faced with this kind of language, there is nothing particularly worth criticising beyond "pointing out" that its fault is incoherence. One must know: muddled speech does not reflect profound thought; muddled speech reflects only a muddled brain.

#### (B) Taking familiarity for clarity

The extremely ambiguous passage quoted above, though somewhat deceptive, is not highly misleading, because it is not hard to see at a glance that it is simply a disorderly piece of writing. By contrast, much speech that is in fact meaningless, or at least of unclear meaning, is often regarded as having a clear meaning precisely because it is not disorderly — at least it does not violate grammatical rules — and because we are familiar with it. That kind of speech is what we must especially guard against. A brief discussion follows.

(1) It is said that "the method of dialectics is to study things from a revolutionary viewpoint"; "to keep our cognition free of error, we must have a correct… class standpoint." [Note 6] The question is: what is a "revolutionary viewpoint"? How are the motions of stars, the structure of atoms, and so on to be studied "from a revolutionary viewpoint"?

When we investigate mathematical problems, must we also, to avoid error, have a "correct class standpoint"? What kind of standpoint is that?

Statements of the form "From the viewpoint / angle / standpoint of XYZ…" are a very popular verbal formula, including phrases like "from the viewpoint / angle / standpoint of translation / history / sociology / psychology / educational theory / feminism / the postmodern / deconstruction / the believer / the Chinese…" and so on, in endless variety. This is not to say such phrases can never be used, but one must be very careful, because many of them are semantically muddled or even wholly meaningless.

Take the usage "from the viewpoint of translation, *a* should be translated as *b*": what does it mean? For example, the Chinese translation of *quantum mechanics* is 量子力學 — what has that to do with "the viewpoint of translation"? Could it not be translated that way if not "viewed from the viewpoint of translation"? Again, a statement like "viewed from the angle of history, such-and-such is the case" is also often of unclear meaning. Did human beings first land on the Moon in the twentieth century only if the event is "viewed from the angle of history," and otherwise not? Is there such a magical "angle of history"? On the other hand, if the event occurred whether or not one views it from that angle, then what substantive function do the added words "viewed from the angle of history" serve, beyond putting on a front?

Finally, consider the phrases "from the viewpoint of the Chinese" and "with a Chinese heart." The former phrase, I believe, is familiar to everyone; the latter, I guarantee with a Chinese heart that I have heard people say with two Chinese ears. But what does "Chinese heart" mean? And what does "the viewpoint of the Chinese" mean? If any viewpoint held by any Chinese person is called "the viewpoint of the Chinese," then the term refers to a heap of mutually conflicting viewpoints, because many viewpoints of different Chinese people conflict with one another. On the other hand, if only the common viewpoint held by most Chinese people is called "the viewpoint of the Chinese," then when we use this term we must first work out: what exactly is that viewpoint? (A similar analysis can be made of "Chinese heart"; I will not repeat it.)

Let me repeat: this is not to say such expressions can never be used, but one must be able to determine that what is used is meaningful, and know what it means. Just imagine: if I say "from the viewpoint of Africans," "with an African heart," do you know what that means? I don't know what it means either. (In any case, it presumably does not mean "viewed from a pitch-black viewpoint" or "with a black heart.")

(2) So far we have only briefly analysed expressions of the form "from such-and-such a viewpoint / angle / standpoint." Now let us examine some muddled speech that appears in the form of questions.

For example: "Can the form and content of art be separated?" "Does history advance in a straight line or rise in a spiral?" "Do heroes make the times, or do the times make heroes?" "Is knowing hard and acting easy, or knowing easy and acting hard?" "Which matters more, a good fate or good luck?" "Which matters more, money or friends?" … Such questions are often raised, and people, having heard them often and grown familiar with them, never think that these questions are in fact semantically unclear. Take the first question: to avoid blind argument, one must clarify what "the form of art" and "the content of art" refer to, and clarify what counts as the form and content of art "being separable" and what counts as their "not being separable." To argue rashly before these terms are analysed clearly is futile: the argument will go on endlessly, obtaining no agreed answer and not advancing one step toward an agreed answer. This cannot be attributed to the question being too profound; it can only be attributed to the question itself being muddled and unclear, so that the arguing parties cannot agree even on the basis for reaching agreement, let alone on an answer.

To establish a basis for discussion, the key lies in clarification. The question "Which matters more, money or friends?" is easily so sweeping and empty as to lead to misunderstanding and divergence. If the friend in question is a friend unto death, someone for whom we would sacrifice our lives, and if we hold that no amount of money is as important as our own lives, then in this case we can conclude that the friend matters more than money. But if the friend in question is only a nodding acquaintance, whose mutual "friendship" is of the kind displayed at the "Friendship Store," and if the money in question is a great deal, then in this case we would probably think (though not say aloud) that money matters more than the friend. From this it can be seen that the question "Which matters more, money or friends?" must be clarified before it can be effectively discussed. In clarifying it, at least two points must be made clear: first, how much money is in question? Second, what kind of friend is in question?

(What must especially be checked is: does he have money? And is he willing to lend money to friends?)

#### (C) Pseudo-semantic projection

The mistaken ideas exposed in sections A and B — "taking obscurity for profundity" and "taking familiarity for clarity" — are both ills of thinking that semantic analysis aims to treat.

This section discusses another ill that semantic analysis aims to treat; call it **pseudo-semantic projection （偽語意投射）**: the supposition that if the component parts of a compound expression (for example, "half a catty" and "music") have determinate meanings, then the whole compound expression ("half-a-catty music") must also have a determinate meaning. Some actual instances of this erroneous idea are dissected below.

Augustine asserted: God created the universe from nothing, including creating time. [Note 7] We know what "a sculptor creates his artwork from certain materials," "Einstein created the theory of relativity at a certain time," and similar statements mean; but what do statements like "God created the universe from nothing" and "God created time" mean (even if the individual words or components in them have determinate meanings)? Further, the orthodox theologian Anselm claimed: all things are in God, yet God is not in space and time. [Note 8] And by the orthodox account, the omnipresent God has neither body nor emotions — yet Paul has the famous expression "the wrath of God." [Note 9] Even if the words composing these statements are all intelligible, do these statements still have meaning when combined into a whole doctrine? "Omnipresent but not in space and time, without emotions yet capable of wrath…" — what does it mean, in the end?

Faced with such questioning, some people offer defences like this: "I cannot and need not explain what it means, because God and His creation exceed human powers of understanding; they are inconceivable and unspeakable (call this assertion the 'inconceivability-and-unspeakability thesis'). Our statements about God and His creation are in substance all metaphorical, symbolic statements (call this assertion the 'metaphor-and-symbol thesis')."

The "inconceivability-and-unspeakability thesis" and the "metaphor-and-symbol thesis" are quite popular; such doctrines appear in the theology of Aquinas, Tillich, and others [Note 10]; many who talk of Zen and Buddhism also hold doctrines of this type, merely replacing "God and His creation" with "the ultimate truth" （勝義）. [Note 11] The question is: can these doctrines stand? Used in the defence above, can they make the defence stand? The answer can be found in the refutation below.

First: ordinary people cannot understand the deeper parts of mathematical logic, such as recursion theory or the proof of Gödel's theorem; in such a case, those subjects can be called beyond ordinary people's powers of understanding. But a meaningless statement cannot be called "beyond human powers of understanding," because with it there is no question of understanding or not understanding at all. For example, the statement "The super-God super-super-ly created super-super-super-space-time" is not so profound that we fail to understand it; it is meaningless, so that understanding and non-understanding simply do not apply to it. Accordingly, the answer "beyond human powers of understanding" cannot effectively respond to the question we raised. Moreover, appending the words "that is inconceivable and unspeakable" after a meaningless statement cannot make that meaningless statement meaningful. Do you know what "Yoh-zi-oh-xi ▲● Geba-ge, that is inconceivable and unspeakable" means?

Second: when the defender talks grandly about God and, pressed by questions and refutations until he cannot cope, uses the "inconceivability-and-unspeakability thesis" to fob people off, hoping they will think that since it is inconceivable and unspeakable, there is no more to discuss, and so let him go and stop pursuing him — but in fact, to talk grandly about God on the one hand and declare on the other that God is inconceivable and unspeakable is nothing but slapping one's own face.

Third: when he discovers himself in the embarrassing position of slapping his own face, the defender pins his hopes on the "metaphor-and-symbol thesis" to get him out of trouble; unfortunately that only gets him into deeper trouble, the muddy foot sinking deeper. For: (i) If *x* is inconceivable, then we cannot know of any thing whether it bears a resemblance relation or a representation relation to *x*. If we cannot know that, we cannot know how to metaphorise or symbolise *x*. Yet the defender claims that what he says can metaphorise or symbolise God, while also claiming that God is inconceivable — so he slaps his own face once again. (ii) The defender both asserts that God is unspeakable and asserts that one can speak metaphorically and symbolically about God; but metaphorically and symbolically "speaking" is still "speaking" — so the defender's final defence is still a slap to his own face.

In summary, the defence above, composed of the "inconceivability-and-unspeakability thesis" and the "metaphor-and-symbol thesis," slaps its own face non-stop, and the more it slaps the more swollen it gets (now *that* is a metaphorical-symbolic way of speaking, whose depicted scenario is conceivable and speakable). Such a defence wholly fails to resolve the question we raised: what does "omnipresent but not in space and time, without emotions yet capable of wrath" mean? One cannot infer, from the component parts of a compound expression having (let us grant) determinate meanings, that the compound expression itself has a determinate meaning — just as one cannot infer, from words like "half of a" and "wife" having determinate meanings, that the expression "half a wife" has a determinate meaning. What is "half a wife"? A lover? A fiancée? A concubine? Or the "half" of a wife rescued from cannibals?

## Part II (supplementary treatment): Language and Thought

### I. Refuting the shackles thesis

Above, semantic analysis's approach has been briefly expounded and displayed, and its "foundational importance" pointed out. But some people think analysing language is of no importance; they hold that the thorough course is to "transcend language and break the limits of language," because "language is a shackle that confines and maims thought." [Note 12] Let us call this doctrine the "shackles thesis."

#### (A) Thought in shackles?

A representative of the shackles thesis, D. T. Suzuki — regarded as a Japanese Zen master and honoured as a world Zen figure — declared: "Language… is the worst thing human beings have invented. Laws, for example, define everything very precisely, with the result that they become a cause of disputes and trouble." [Note 13] This speech of Suzuki's, *spoken in language*, may count as one of the worst things human beings have invented; it is like asserting "If there were no police, nobody would be arrested and convicted, so the police are the cause of crime" — just a case of reversing cause and effect. In general, the disputes of the human world do not arise because laws were laid down; on the contrary, laws are laid down precisely because there are disputes in the human world.

If language is the worst thing humans have invented, a shackle confining and maiming thought, then to remove this worst thing, to be rid of this shackle, should we adopt a measure even more thorough than suppressing freedom of speech, and simply prohibit all speech — including prohibiting prayer, arguing for God's existence, and talking of love, and prohibiting saying "language is a shackle that confines and maims thought"? Should we abolish all constitutions, statutes, and every kind of contract? Should we dissolve parliaments and courts, close schools and research institutes, shut down radio stations, television stations, and cinemas (unless they show only silent films with the subtitles removed)? Should we smash all telephones, typewriters, and computers? Should we burn all newspapers, magazines, and books — burn the *Book of Changes*, the *Analects*, the *Laozi*, the *Heart Sutra*, the *Diamond Sutra*, the *Bible*, Plato's *Phaedo*, Euclid's *Elements*, Newton's *Principia*, Darwin's *Origin of Species*, Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*, and so on and so on?

One need only imagine how terrifying the consequences of the above actions would be to understand clearly: language is not a shackle confining and maiming thought; on the contrary, if we lost language, our thought would suffer the greatest confinement and maiming.

From this it is clear that the shackles thesis is extremely absurd. We cannot say language is harmful to thought; we can only say certain statements, or certain ways of using words (none of which are identical with language), are harmful to thought. For example, the statement "language is a shackle that confines and maims thought" itself confines and maims the shackles-theorist's thought; it is the shackles-theorist's thought-shackle, and a rusty one.

#### (B) Transcending language?

The shackles-theorists, who make their own shackles and then shackle themselves with them, most love to say they want to "transcend language" and "break through language." [Note 14] But what do "transcending language" and "breaking through language" mean?

(1) If transcending and breaking through language means setting up no written words and renouncing language, then: First, why renounce language? Is that not making trouble for oneself out of nothing? Second, can human beings afford the terrifying consequences of renouncing language? Third, can the shackles-theorists themselves really renounce language? In fact, all they can do is talk endlessly about "the unspeakable," lecture at length on "setting up no words," and repeatedly declare that they want to "transcend language and break through language." The comedy of this is no less than that of a chatterbox nagging on and on about how "very quiet" she is, or a beggar pretending to be mute shouting at passers-by: "I am mute!"

(2) If transcending and breaking through language means disregarding the rules of language, talking nonsense and speaking recklessly, then of course the shackles-theorists can "transcend language and break through language." But what good is that? Incoherence merely exposes confused thought, even mental derangement — what is so remarkable about that?

### II. Refuting the chaos thesis

That these people's thought is confused is easy to document. Take Suzuki Daisetz and Kōnan Junjirō, two famous talkers of Zen and Buddhism (those below their level are even less worth mentioning): Suzuki said: "Change is non-change, non-change is change," "Square is round, round is square," "Life is death," "The question is the answer," "A equals non-A," "'Yes' slides into 'no,' and 'no' slides into 'yes'"; Kōnan, not to be outdone, declared: "'Non-existence' is at the same time also 'existence,' and 'existence' is at the same time also 'non-existence.'" [Note 15]

Let us call doctrines of this kind — "yes is no, being is non-being" — the "chaos thesis." The chaos thesis looks like a natural development of the shackles thesis. Shacles-theorists plainly cannot really renounce language, nor attain the realm of silently smiling while twirling a flower, so they talk a round of nonsense and call it "breaking through the limits of language." Questions and refutations for the chaos thesis follow.

(1) It is said that when a lady says "mustn't," she means "perhaps"; when she says "perhaps," she means "may"; and when she says "may," she is no longer a lady. Does this show that ladies have "broken through the limits of language" and reached a high state of enlightenment? [The Buddha, asked by Mañjuśrī how to cultivate and realise the six pāramitās, said: "…able to break delusion, able to empty all marks, able to penetrate principle and dharma, able to decide right and wrong, every word apt, every character without error — that is called attaining the sixth, prajñā, pāramitā." [Note 16] Can the chaos-theorists' "yes is no" thinking "decide right and wrong"? Are random sayings like "life is death" and "change is non-change" "every word apt, every character without error" — or glib, reckless nonsense?]

(2) Doctrines like "change is non-change, non-change is change" are nothing but the result of semantic distortion and conceptual confusion. Although we sometimes say (for example) "a certain river has both changed and not changed," that is only an elliptical way of speaking: it usually means that the river has changed in one respect (at least, the water molecules it contains at this moment are no longer exactly the same as at the previous moment) and has not changed in another respect (as a whole, it is still that river) — not that the river in the *same* respect and the *same* sense both changes and does not change, and still less that it means "change is non-change, non-change is change." Again, take "life is death": a person lives at one time and dies at another moment; that can only be called going from life "to" death, not life "being" death. Even though, while some cells in our body are alive, others have died, at that moment the living ones are still living and the dead ones are only "other" cells — that also cannot be called life being death. Even if a given cell at a given moment is both alive and tending toward death, "tending toward death" is not equal to "being death." Other slogans, such as "square is round, round is square" and "existence is at the same time non-existence," can likewise be analysed and criticised along similar lines without much difficulty.

(3) After the chaos-theorist has put forward the chaos thesis "yes is no" (call this sentence P), we can say to him: "Oh, so you mean 'no is no'?" (If yes is no, then in P we can replace the first "yes" with "no.") Or we can say to him: "Oh, so you mean 'yes is non-no'?" (In P, replace the second "yes" with "no.") If he answers: "No, what I mean is…," then we can say to him: "You answered 'yes…,' did you?" At this point, even if the chaos-theorist sweats profusely in his urgency, says "that's not it" non-stop, and keeps claiming we "don't understand" his meaning, it is no use — because by his own account, "that's not it" equals "that's it," and "don't understand" equals "understand." Finally, if we watch the chaos-theorist struggling painfully and cannot bear it, we may comfort him and tell him that we too think his doctrine is both a correct and profound philosophy — if, that is, correct is incorrect, profound is shallow, and philosophy is fallacy.

### III. The root of deluded thinking

Even though chaos-theorists shake their heads and wag their tails, chanting "yes is no, being is non-being" like eight-legged-essay gentlemen, in actual life they are still no different from ordinary people. They still need to eat and dress, and do not think that not eating is eating or that having no clothes is having clothes. Sometimes they still scramble to be first and fear to be last like common mortals, unwilling to lose out, and do not think that last is first or that losing out is being a vegetarian.

Since even the chaos-theorists themselves would not really practise the chaos thesis, others will do so even less. Since nobody would really practise it, why do we still take the trouble to criticise it? The answer is: that nobody would really practise it is one thing; its malign influence on people's thinking is another. This essay does not shrink from spending space criticising the chaos thesis precisely because this doctrine has an extremely bad influence on people's thinking. The universal formula it contains — "non-x is x" — can be called the root of deluded thinking. This formula is most welcome to ambitious manipulators. When ambitious men invert black and white and confuse right and wrong, the formula they use is exactly this one.

Lest this formula appear too barbaric, when using it, it is best first to take the word "dialectical," discussed earlier, as a civilised decoration. If one further adds "revolutionary" as a supporting frame, that is even more marvellous. [Note 17] The trick lies in: speaking revolutionarily, explaining dialectically.

How does one "speak revolutionarily"? You speak however you like. How does one "explain dialectically"? You explain however you please. Today, when someone is to be struggled against, one declares there is a "contradiction of opposition." Tomorrow, when someone is to be won over, one says there is a "unity of contradictions." The relation between the two is a "revolutionary dialectical relation" — in other words, a relation of no relation. Where is the dividing line between the two? There is no fixed one: "everything changes" [Note 18]; "antagonistic contradictions and non-antagonistic contradictions can transform into each other under certain conditions" [Note 19]. But what are the so-called "certain conditions"? They have never been clearly stated; they depend entirely on "the needs of the situation." That is "revolutionary dialectics." From the viewpoint of "revolutionary dialectics," any x and any y can "transform into each other" — under certain (i.e., indeterminate) conditions.

Everything must depend on certain (indeterminate) conditions: that is the principle. Never to determine what those conditions are: that is called flexibility. Flexibly speaking, clinging to rigid dogma is called having "revolutionary principledness." In principle, being fickle and changeable can also be called having "flexibility of struggle." When should one be flexible, and when principled?

At all times one must be flexible, and at all times one must be principled. "Dialectically" combining the two yields: being flexible about principles — that is, speaking of them when one speaks of them, and not speaking of them when one doesn't. In short, the fundamental spirit of revolutionary dialectics is: to use dialectical word-play fencing to cover revolutionary live ammunition, and at the same time to use revolutionary live ammunition to support dialectical word-play fencing.

That is the highest principle of revolutionary dialectics — that is, the principle of having no principle — that is, taking the absence of principle as the only principle.

If things go on like this, is there still any objective right and wrong in the human world? No. There are only power tactics, conspiracies, snobbery, and "the winner becomes king, the loser a bandit" — together with the "ideological weapons" of these things: word tricks and sophistical techniques.

Will playing word tricks and sophistical techniques have any bad consequences? None at all: those who play will swallow the fruit into their own stomachs — this is called eating the fruit of one's own deeds. That is the fruit of deluded thinking. This kind of delusion can be called the most dangerous and terrifying delusion since human beings began. In this delusion, everything is inverted. I say it once more: "Everything is inverted." Might is truth; suppression is called freedom; privilege is called equality; dictatorship is democracy; the gun barrel equals the people's throat; manipulation of another country is named friendship — "brotherly friendship" that must send tanks to consolidate it. The "theoretical basis" of all of this is precisely the chaos thesis's chaotic formula: "non-x is x." [Note 20]

### Postscript: From authoritarian thinking to independent thinking

The wise explore truth; educators disseminate truth; dictators are responsible for manufacturing truth — attempting to manufacture truth with the formula "non-x is x."

Unfortunately, truth cannot be manufactured this way. What can be manufactured this way is only decreed truth. Decreed truth often cannot withstand the test of reason and fact. So from time to time in history, "heretics" stand up and point out that such-and-such a decreed truth is, in fact, not truth.

But heretics are punished. How they are punished depends on the dictator's disposition: some dictators are old-fashioned, some love fashion, some are bold and generous, some are comparatively gentle and refined.

Old-fashioned dictators like to push heretics into an arena guarded by hungry lions, or tie them up and roast them like at a bonfire party; that is a classical romance. Fashion-loving dictators are fond of sending heretics to experience target practice, or inviting them to taste a poisoned needle; that is a pastime with a modern flavour.

Bold and generous dictators do not like to argue; they like action — direct and crisp. Their style is honest: they will not stab you in the back; they stab you in the front. They like to use sharp instruments to make the throats of opponents go "click," rendering them speechless. As for the gentle and refined dictators, they have the most grace: they are tolerant and patient, and occasionally even send heretics into mental hospitals to receive improvement (this marvellous education system originated in Soviet Russia and has the special effect of removing heretical contamination; it is another great Russian invention besides borscht — call it "Russian-style health-welfare measures").

Of the several methods of handling heretics above, the last seems the most worthy of appreciation. The others are all somewhat coarse and inelegant, and their results are not ideal either — for the dead are gone; one can only croak once, and croaked heretics are all unreasonably unrepentant to death, rigid in brain and eventually rigid in whole body: in short, they stubbornly refuse to sing praises of the wise leadership.

So wise rulers all understand that heretics must be transformed through civilised channels, by education.

This education is more advanced than both compulsory education and adult education in advanced regions; it can be called the dialectical combination of the two — namely, "compulsory adult education."

Just as young people too busy for romance cannot attend to the difference between "voluntarily blind" before marriage and "forced to be blind" after marriage, dictators likewise do not fuss over details and disdain to heed the difference between adults receiving education voluntarily and being forced to receive it. Having "unified the contradiction" between the two educations, they unhesitatingly appoint themselves great teachers and shoulder the work of enlightening the world.

Such educators work to transform (other people's) views of life and of the world.

They not only want to seek happiness for others; they even want to define, on others' behalf, what counts as happiness.

What method does this education use? In brief: language tricks and sophistical magic. That includes the flexible use of empty talk — *fèihuà*, nonsense — and big talk; the glorious repetition of slogans and set phrases; the revolutionary twisting of concepts and word meanings… and all manner of supremely advantageous techniques, too numerous to list. Deployed, these techniques can make people's heads grow heavy and muddled, and in that daze they gradually deny themselves, coming to think their former thoughts were all wrong, while increasingly believing that every sentence of the great teacher is the highest truth.

The question is: however masterful and ingenious the language tricks and sophistical magic, once they meet the sharp instrument of analytic and critical thinking, they are broken and exposed. And if more and more people command this instrument for sharpening thought, what scene will appear?

Then the fog will disperse and the smokescreen will vanish.

On that day, people will be able to breathe fresh air and think independently, freely and unimpeded. On that day, people will no longer, as before, worship swindlers while hanging the people who expose them. On that day, people will reflect: should the "saviour" they have always followed be chased and beaten instead? On that day, people will see clearly: among all animals, the ambitious manipulator is the most dangerous. And on that day, people will suddenly realise that the reason statues of gods appear especially tall is only that everyone is prostrated on the ground.
