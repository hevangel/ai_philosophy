The final value of human life lies in the capacity for awakening and thinking, not in mere survival. — Aristotle

## Preface

The philosopher Pascal has a famous saying: "Man is but a reed, the most fragile in nature, but he is a thinking reed."

This "thinking reed," if it thinks a little about thinking, will not find it hard to see the importance of thinking.

For the individual: to lack knowledge is merely to be ignorant; to be clumsy at thinking is to be stupid. For society as a whole: only when more and more people can think independently do the conditions for freedom, democracy, and the rule of law have a chance to arise and a solid guarantee. Majestic constitutional articles alone are useless — the constitutions of autocratic states can be written just as majestically; what matters is that they can actually be implemented. But to hope that those who hold power will automatically implement a constitution that restricts them is no guarantee at all; what matters is the people's supervision. Yet for ignorant people to carry out supervision is unrealistic; what matters is the awakening of the masses. That depends on a general raising of people's ability to think independently — on everyone being able to discern right from wrong, true from false.

The ability to discern right from wrong is especially necessary in this age of propaganda.

Have you ever seen anyone marching with a placard reading: "Firmly believe 1 + 1 = 2! Believers shall be saved!" Or striding along, head held high, shouting slogans: "Uphold relativity! Uphold quantum theory! Uphold mathematical logic! Uphold the above three upholds!"?

I believe you have never seen such a magnificent spectacle, right? In general, the more reliable a doctrine, the less it needs propaganda slogans to support it; the more a doctrine needs propaganda slogans to support it, the less reliable it is. But many doctrines closely concerning the masses are supported by propaganda slogans. These things are originally like seven-coloured balloons blown full: one needle-prick and they burst into wind. Sadly, ordinary people do not have that "needle." So ambitious manipulators can conquer the masses without firing a shot, using propaganda slogans alone — like hunters who need not fire a gun, hunting hearts exclusively.

To see through such "hunters" — or, more broadly, to identify right from wrong — a naked brain without equipment, "thinking independently" on its own, is not enough. We must possess sharp instruments of thought for discerning right from wrong: a set of analytic and critical conceptual tools as the methodological cornerstone of independent thinking.

The main purpose of this essay is to provide exactly such a set of tools, hoping to help remove the blindfolds that cover thought's line of sight. Once the blindfold is removed, people can walk by themselves, without others leading them by the hand — and still less will they be led by the nose.

## Introduction: A practically effective framework for the critique of linguistic harms

If we advertised for a nursemaid to look after children and received this application letter: "Dear Sir: I am originally of the Gaoshan (mountain aboriginal) people, educated in the city, hardworking and enduring, adaptable, I eat anything, and I am most fond of children" — before deciding whether to hire this nursemaid, we would seemingly first need to clarify what "most fond of children" means here: does it mean "most dotes on children," or "most likes to eat children"? Similarly, when we receive a leaflet notifying us that "believe in the triune God and you can go to Heaven; do not believe and you must go to Hell," before deciding whether to believe, we seemingly also need first to clarify what "triune" means. If it means "both Father and Son and Spirit"… "being one's own father and also one's own son" — what does that mean? If the Son is the Son's Father, then the Son's Father is "the Son's Father's Father," but this "Son's Father's Father" happens to be the Son himself, because "the Son's Father's Father" equals "the Son's Father" equals "the Son himself." But what does this whole head-spinning heap of talk, arising from "both Father and Son," actually mean?

When such questions are met, a common response is to lecture people: "Don't ask, just believe! Don't analyse with reason; believe first and then you will understand!"

But that is putting the cart before the horse. It is not that I don't want to believe; I just don't know what you are asking me to believe. If I don't even know what "x" means, then there is "no way to start believing" — belief and non-belief in x simply do not arise. To speak of believing x would be nothing but self-deception. Suppose someone asks you to believe in "the six-in-one God, both Father and Son and Grandson and Essence and Spirit and Soul," and when you ask what it means he says "Don't analyse with reason; believe first and then you will understand" — do you think such an answer stands up?

Don't sign before you've seen clearly: that is common sense. There's no believing before you know what something means: that is an unshakeable commonplace truth. But ordinary people have no training in semantic analysis, and lack the thinking habit of "clarifying key terms of unclear meaning." There are exceptions, but they are rare. For instance, if you go on a date with an old secondary-school flame but tell your wife you are going to an alumni gathering, your wife may order you to "clarify" what the so-called "alumni gathering" means. But that can only count as a special case. In general, people only notice the things they are thinking about and overlook the language used to think — just as people ordinarily only notice the things they see and forget the eyes they see with.

That is why many people only know how to talk, talk, talk, and do not know to pause and reflect on what what they are saying actually means. Pointless arguments constantly arise from this. "Is such-and-such a place a cultural desert?" "I ate a bad egg and my stomach hurts; is the bad egg the internal cause or the external cause of the stomach-ache?" "Is there equality in Cambodian society?" — argue about such questions without clarification and the result is predictable: there is no result [Note 1]. Take "Is there equality in Cambodian society?": A thinks there is; the equality in his mind is egalitarianism (or "people's egalitarianism") — national income divided half-and-half between the bureaucrats and the people, the people further dividing their half equally. B thinks Cambodia has no equality; the equality in his mind is equality of opportunity — "everyone has an equal opportunity to prove that people's abilities are indeed unequal." In these circumstances, unless "equality" is first clarified, the argument cannot be rationally resolved — or can only be handed to the Cambodian Public Security Bureau to instruct the Cambodian People's Court to adjudicate. (I bet A wins.)

The dispute between A and B is in fact a "verbal dispute" — a dispute caused by factors like the ambiguity and vagueness of terms. L. Abbot, editor-in-chief of *Outlook* magazine, said his father held that nine out of ten disputes in philosophy and theology are verbal disputes, while Abbot himself held that the remaining one-tenth are also verbal disputes. If father and son Abbot argued about this, that would probably become a verbal dispute too. Philosophers have been likened to blind men looking in a black room for a black cat that doesn't exist; theologians, to people who actually find the cat. For philosophers and theologians fond of verbal disputes, that satire should act as a sobering dose. But verbal disputes are in fact rife not only in philosophy and theology; they are common in other fields too. In politics, for example, people dispute over "thoughts" or "-isms" whose meanings are still unclear, and end up slaughtering one another; such tragedies are staged from time to time, rivalling those staged in religion.

The most fundamental route to preventing such human tragedies is to eliminate verbal disputes. The most effective method of eliminating verbal disputes is to use semantic analysis, the sharp instrument of thinking [Note 2]. The primary task of semantic analysis is to criticise the various language-and-concept ills harmful to apt thinking (call them "linguistic harms," 語害 [Note 3]). This essay builds a practically effective framework for semantic analysis [Note 4] with which to criticise the three most common categories of linguistic harm. Able to recognise the various linguistic harms, we will not easily be fooled by muddled speech, and in thinking and discussing will not become like a blind bird or an out-of-control aircraft, or like a reckless person who boards whatever bus comes along, only to discover on reaching the terminus that he boarded the wrong bus.

## Category I of linguistic harm: Semantic ambiguity

Do universals exist really and independently of individual things, or within individual things, or are they merely empty names? This question caused philosophy's well-known "dispute between realism and nominalism." It was a long-drawn-out dispute in scholastic theology, lasting many centuries, later escalating into intense opposition between two camps of universities: on one side, the University of Paris banned the books of Ockham, the nominalists' representative, and rigidly required its teachers to swear to teach realism; on the other side, the Universities of Prague, Vienna, Heidelberg, and others insisted on teaching nominalism. The two camps confronted each other for over a hundred years.

The question is: did this tangled question finally get a definite answer? The answer is: there was no definite answer. Is that because the question is too profound? It is not because the question is too profound, but because the question itself is unclear in meaning. The biggest problem is failing to see that the so-called problem is in fact no problem at all. Such a problem is like a "contest" with no fixed rules: dispute it for another thousand years and there will still be no definite result, because statements like "universals exist within individual things" are fundamentally muddled and unclear. And if "universals exist within individual things" is muddled and unclear, then "universals do not exist within individual things" is also muddled and unclear.

Let us collectively call words, phrases, statements, questions, and so on that (in a given usage) are unclear in meaning and muddled "(in that usage) semantically ambiguous speech." Examples will now exhibit the nature of such speech.

### I. Exhibiting examples

#### (A) "Mission" and "fate"

People often talk about mission and fate, but few reflect on what they actually mean. It is not that the words are wholly meaningless; it is that their meanings are not clear. Look first at "mission": here are a few lines of "poetry" to display the word's ambiguity:

Having completed its historical mission / the outdated nuclear bomb is dismantled / like a fruit plucked down. / Having completed its era's mission / the new nuclear bomb blooms in the night sky / like brilliant fireworks.

Whether or not it explodes, a nuclear bomb can be said to have completed (or "victoriously completed") its mission. Under what circumstances could it not be so described? There is no fixed standard. This shows that "mission" is here a semantically ambiguous word. Ambitious manipulators like to exploit the ambiguity of this word to incite the masses — for example: "To establish the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere is the glorious mission of our Yamato people." (And is exposing how manipulators exploit the word "mission" my historical mission and my era's mission?) Now look at "fate": to say that someone "spent his life fleeing fate and in the end could not escape fate — that was his fate" — what does that mean? We sometimes use the word as a synonym (a slightly literary synonym) for "what befalls one" （遭遇）. Driving your girlfriend for a spin and discovering on a slope that the brakes have failed, a logic-talking person would say to his girlfriend, "We shall have a shared lot [what befalls us in common]"; a poem-writing person would say, "We shall have a shared fate"; a person who both talks logic and writes poems might say: "If we have a shared lot, we have a shared fate, and vice versa." When "fate" is used as a synonym for "what befalls one," the word cannot be called ambiguous: its meaning is just "what befalls one." But the word has another usage of unclear meaning, for example: "Our fate controls what befalls us." Here "fate" cannot be interpreted as "what befalls us," or the sentence would amount to "What befalls us controls what befalls us." But if it cannot be so interpreted, how should it be interpreted? There is no clear answer. The "fate" in such speech is inherently semantically ambiguous. Oppositions like "there are / are no living things on the Moon," "the Moon's position can / cannot change" can be resolved, but oppositions like "people have / do not have fate," "fate can / cannot be changed" can never be resolved. Above, two stanzas of poetry displayed the ambiguity of "mission"; below, two epitaphs display the ambiguity of "fate":

Russell: Here lies Russell; he was fated to have four wives. He realised his fate.

Li Tianming: Here lies Li Tianming; he was fated to have four wives. Sadly, he did not realise his fate.

From these two epitaphs one can see the ambiguity of the word "fate." With any man you can exploit this ambiguity and say he was "fated to have four wives." If (like Russell) he in fact had four wives, you can congratulate him on realising his fate. If (unlike me, unfortunately) he in fact did not have four wives, you can still comfort him by saying he merely failed to realise his fate. (But I prefer pretty face-saving talk; I like to say: Russell submitted to fate; I successfully challenged fate.)

#### (B) "Dialectics"

What is the negation of knowing? Not knowing.

The word "negation" originally has a clear logical use: the negation of a concept or proposition can be defined by "non-F" [Note 6]. By this use, we know the negation of "knowing" is "not knowing." But "negation" also has an ambiguous "dialectical use." In that use, we do not know whether the negation of "knowing" is "not knowing." Let us briefly analyse the ambiguity of this "dialectical negation."

According to dialectics, all things contain "internal contradictions": "Inside any thing there are two aspects, affirmation and negation." [Note 7] "One divides into two; this is a universal phenomenon; this is dialectics." [Note 8] On the basis of these assertions, Engels's beard, for example, being a thing, should contain an internal contradiction and exhibit the universal phenomenon of "one dividing into two"; inside it there must exist an affirmative aspect and a negative aspect. But what is the internal contradiction of Engels's beard, what is the affirmative aspect inside his beard, and what is the negative aspect inside his beard? Nobody knows.

The "law of the negation of the negation," called one of the three laws of materialist dialectics, asserts: the development of all things passes through the process "affirmation — negation — negation of the negation." [Note 9] In terms of two frequently cited classic examples, going from "a" to "−a" to "a²" is a series of negations: "−a" is the negation of "a," and "a²" is the negation of "−a." Again: sow a seed; the sprout that grows is the negation of the seed; the new seeds later produced are in turn the negation of the sprout. The question is: can you, through such "explanations," understand what the so-called "negation" means? "Multiplying by −1," "multiplying by −a," "a seed sprouting," "producing new seeds" — when all of these are uniformly called "negation," what meaning-content does the word "negation" still have? Nobody knows.

"Materialist dialectics holds that when new things negate old things, they do not negate everything, but eliminate the negative factors in the old things and critically inherit and develop the positive factors in the old things." [Note 10] On this basis: is the negation of knowing a deeper knowing, or not knowing? Still nobody knows. In this magical use of the word "negation," is a razor the negation of a beard? Is a beard the negation of a chin? Is a razor the "negation of the negation" of a chin? Still nobody knows.

### II. Terminological camouflage

Suppose I say: addition is subtraction, because [here a formula in mathematical logic followed, its glyphs partly lost in the extraction]. Can this reason stand? Most people do not understand mathematical logic (and ordinarily have no need to), so they cannot judge whether the reason stands. But with a little knowledge of the deeper parts of mathematical logic, one can see at a glance that such a reason is absurd, because that formula merely gives the definition of "+," so the whole assertion is like saying: "Addition is subtraction, because addition is addition." [Note 11]

From this it can be seen that terminology (especially symbolic terminology) very easily creates a smokescreen or "camouflage effect." Semantic ambiguity often relies on terminological smokescreens (call them "terminological camouflage," 術語迷彩） to conceal itself. The American philosopher B. Blanshard held that one of the hardest things in the world is to "write clearly." To speak obscurely and unintelligibly, what difficulty is there? Randomly cobbling together a heap of mysterious terminology is as easy as turning over a palm. Yet some people always feel terminology has a mysterious attraction — "there must be some profound doctrine inside." In fact, playing terminological tricks is like laying a layer of duckweed over a pool of sewage: it makes it hard to tell the water's depth. Deliberate mystification, dressed in the garb of seemingly profound terminology: that is fashionable depth.

Terminology of course has its important uses, but for this essay's purposes we must attend to the fraudulent use of terminology. Academic research usually needs to introduce terminology; muddling through usually needs to play with terminology. Terminology is the tool of scholarship and the mask of muddling. One of the muddler's tricks is: when he has a theory, he talks theory; when he has data, he talks data; and when he has nothing at all, he uses terminology to bluff.

#### (A) Numerical camouflage

Bluffing with terminology: itinerant fortune-tellers are good at it, and certain social scientists who could be called "social fortune-tellers" are even better. Two itinerant fortune-tellers will produce three different predictions; three social fortune-tellers will produce six different predictions. Tomorrow they can all explain to you why yesterday's predictions were not fulfilled today. The magic weapon such "explanations" rely on is often just a heap of bluffing terminology.

Statistical terminology is often needed in the social sciences. Good use of statistical terminology is necessary for the social sciences; playing with statistical terminology is deceptive to ordinary people. For example, never publishing what the base number is and merely declaring "the economy has grown by thirty percent" is a semantically ambiguous formulation. When the first pimple grows on our face, our "pimple growth" is (one could say) infinite. When the second pimple grows, our "pimple growth" is one hundred percent. When the third pimple grows, "pimple growth" falls to fifty percent. From this it is clear that concealing the base while merely announcing a growth of such-and-such a percent smacks of playing with statistics. As far as I know, the deception contained in such tricks (including the sentence I am now uttering) has a "deceptiveness growth" of 77.88 percent per year.

#### (B) Camouflage in art criticism

Traditional arts critics mostly liked metaphors and were good at description — for instance, Liu Kezhuang's *Houcun Shihua* on Meng Haoran's poetry: "Like ripples beginning on Lake Dongting, tree leaves slightly falling." Many of today's arts critics, by contrast, like to show off terminological flourishes and excel at unintelligibility, for example: "causing the consociate original-nature of universals participating in nature's operation to change in quality and in appearance" [Note 12].

Sighing that you cannot understand what it means? No need to sigh: nobody can understand what meaningless word-salads mean. (Some people equate meaninglessness with profundity, and treat any criticism of ambiguous speech as a failure to understand. But how do *they* understand it? They have never given a clear explanation; the moment someone raises a criticism, they only say the other person doesn't understand.)

The "causing the consociate…" passage above comes from an article discussing literary aesthetics. Now appreciate another (series of) paper(s) exploring the aesthetics of painting: "Today, the only thing that can save physics is probably metaphysical-aesthetic dialectics… Aesthetics workers are the prophets of humanity… enabling today's Chinese, by the utmost of aesthetic method… to face the West without fear"; "The best development of human civilisation from now on… its most essential grasp probably still lies in China's indigenous aesthetic method."

[Note 13] So aesthetics (especially China's indigenous aesthetics) is great beyond compare. Within that series of "aesthetics papers," what great content does this incomparably great aesthetics, or "metaphysical-aesthetic dialectics," have? Only a heap of incomparably ambiguous terminology, for example: "Fan Kuan, because his feeling is melancholy, emphasises the limit-contrastivity of dialectical structure"; "The process of a person's life is nothing but the methodical exhaustion of all the large and small real feelings passing through, and of form-objects." [Note 14] Besides verbal terminology as profound as "the limit-contrastivity of dialectical structure" and "the methodical exhaustion of form-objects," there is also profound symbolic terminology: "If we let [a symbol partly lost in the extraction] stand for everything painting seeks to pursue, and let Af stand for a highly humanistically integrated form, then the manner in which Af stands for [that symbol] can be simplified into the following formula: Af --> [symbol]." [Note 15]

What is this stuff? Anyone who knows a little mathematical logic, seeing these symbols, will probably smile knowingly: it is like a small child who both reveres symbols he doesn't understand and scribbles and chants them at random. Some people are no longer children but still harbour a psychology of symbol-worship: seeing the large sets of symbols in logic, mathematics, and so on that they cannot understand, they grow reverent, supposing them mysterious beyond measure, and so (attempt to) copy the gourd from the model — when they should simply say "eat rice," they will not say "eat rice" simply, but insist on going in circles and using symbols to "stand for" it. Symbolic terminology has very important functions; in logic, mathematics, physics, computer science, and similar subjects it is even indispensable. But in aesthetics there is generally no need to use it. The symbols in the quoted passage are a random pile, useless except for muddling. The symbol systems set up by logic, mathematics, and so on are established with definite norms, through strict procedures of introduction — not by casually extracting a letter or two from the English alphabet, vaguely "standing for" something, and adding an arrow of unclear reference. What does the "-->" in the passage mean? A logical "conditional relation"? A mathematical "tendency"? A chemical reaction? A traffic sign? Directions to the toilets?… [Note 16]

### III. From floating to incoherence

The discussion above of terminological smokescreens or camouflage aims to reveal that terminology is easily used to conceal semantic ambiguity. Note that between semantic ambiguity and semantic clarity there is only a difference of degree, not a clean-cut distinction. But the absence of a sharp boundary does not mean there is no approximate boundary. Day and night, for example, have no obvious dividing line, yet they are not undifferentiated. (Even if copying one book is called plagiarism, copying ten books is called reference, and copying a hundred books is called creation, so that plagiarism and creation differ only in degree, one cannot infer that there is no distinction between them.)

#### (A) Semantic floating （語意虛浮）

Semantic floating is a milder degree of semantic ambiguity. "Semantic floating," as used here, means "being vague and evasive, without bounds and without fixed standards." Such speech can also be called "floating words and empty talk." The characteristics of such speech are set out below.

(1) Quite a few people (including the author) like to give "young" a new definition every year. In fact the word "young" has no sharply drawn line of use. Even so, we cannot say "young" is a wholly boundless word, stretchable at will. Eighteen can definitely be called young; eighty cannot count as young [Note 17]. Again, consider "child": some parents, wanting their children to help with housework, say "You are no longer a child; you should do some housework"; when they don't want their children to date, they say the opposite: "You are still a child; don't date so early." This shows the scope of application of "child" has no very clear demarcation.

But we also cannot call it boundless. Four years old is definitely a child — a four-year-old who can date is called an old-man-child, or a genius; forty years old is definitely not a child — a forty-year-old who still cannot date is called an idiot, or a sage [Note 18].

From this it can be seen that words like "young" and "child" are not wholly without standards. By contrast, "counter-revolutionary," for example, is a word without standards.

What is called the most revolutionary behaviour today can be called counter-revolutionary tomorrow [Note 19]. Again, the phrase "in accordance with (or contrary to) God's will," in the usage of some religious people, is likewise mere floating words and empty talk without standards — how would you test whether a statement like "Pray and you will receive, if it accords with God's will" is true?

(2) We often need to use elastic words like "often," "about," "kind," "quite a few," and so on; it cannot be said that whoever uses elastic words is speaking semantically floating speech. For example: "Quite a few people hold this doctrine, but the doctrine contains a fallacy, because…" — the main point of such a statement is to indicate that a certain doctrine contains a fallacy, not to report how many people, or which people, hold it. In this case we do not consider the statement semantically floating. But if the crux at the time hangs on indicating how many people, or which people, hold the doctrine, and the speaker says only "quite a few," then we will consider his statement semantically floating. In general, to determine whether a statement counts as semantically floating, one must consider whether the statement is apt and proportionate to its context.

(3) This shows that whether a kind of speech is floating words and empty talk often depends on the context involved. For example, the formulation "harming the fundamental interests of the state and the nation" could, if there were comparatively concrete articles and detailed rules to expound it, or classic precedents in a legal tradition to serve as reference, be interpreted to a fairly clear degree. But if such a basis of interpretation is lacking, or has been "severed," then the formulation is nothing but floating words and empty talk. (The "concepts" expressed by such floating words and empty talk are exactly the "fluid concepts" that Frankfurt School figures like Marcuse used as magic weapons.)

Using floating words and empty talk (or "fluid concepts") to frame constitutions and laws easily produces the strange situation in which "people are at once lawless and struck dumb with fear." Lawless, because the legal articles are too elastic, with no clear norms to bound them. Struck dumb with fear, also because the legal articles are too elastic: people do not know what to do, offend at every move, the more they say and do the more mistakes they make; say nothing and do nothing and make no mistakes — best to be passive and idle, until in the end it is a pool of dead water.

(4) Generally, semantically floating doctrines are neither correct nor incorrect; they do not rise to the level of being correct-or-incorrect, incorrect-or-not (many people do not know this extremely important distinction). Such a doctrine cannot be definitely applied. A useful theory can guide practice, enabling people to avoid mistakes as far as possible, without blind groping. Landing on the Moon, for instance, relied on the definite application of scientific theory. Although the astronaut "walked a unique road, with no precedent to draw on," he did not need to "grope about" in pitch-dark space. Space flight is not space groping; astronauts do not grope their way to the Moon. If we adopt a doctrine to guide practice and in fact can only rely on ourselves to grope, that shows the doctrine is useless. Some people confuse floatingness with universality and equate floating doctrines with universal truths. When they discover such a "truth" cannot be applied in practice, they regard it as the inevitable phenomenon occurring when "universal truth is combined with concrete circumstances." In fact this is a serious misunderstanding. If a doctrine cannot possibly be applied, that is no different from proving that the doctrine is not a universal truth — because a universal truth is a universal truth precisely in that it applies universally to all relevant concrete circumstances.

Once a doctrine of the above kind is enthroned as the whole society's "guiding thought," it is very likely to lead to a behavioural mentality of appealing to power rather than to reason, manifested as: not asking about objective right and wrong, only asking about the latest situation; everyone must "keep up closely with the situation," and everyone fears "falling behind the situation." The reason is that whoever has power has the power of interpretation, and whoever has the power of interpretation has the "highest truth"; in the end, practice is not the criterion for testing truth — live ammunition is the criterion for testing truth [Note 20].

[MARGINAL NOTE: In such a situation, when one faction styles itself orthodox, what countermeasures do other factions have? As far as semantically floating doctrines are concerned, whoever gains power is orthodox and whoever loses power is heretical; that is natural and proper, and nobody has strong grounds to explain why their interpretation of the doctrine is the only correct one. That being so, to willingly honour the other side as orthodox is the worst strategy. What then are the middle and best strategies? To set oneself up as orthodox is the middle strategy. To let that "orthodoxy" exist in name only, or simply to transcend that "orthodoxy," is the best strategy. As the French writer Maurois said: "Some party leaders are prepared to sacrifice the country for a doctrine or an -ism, but the true leader says: 'To hell with the -ism; saving the country is what matters.'"]

#### (B) Semantic incoherence （語意錯亂）

When semantic ambiguity reaches the extreme of complete meaninglessness — of incoherence and unintelligibility — let us call it "semantic incoherence."

Why is incoherent speech "produced"? Perhaps because the producer wants to fake profundity; perhaps because during the production process he did not think — has no habit of "thinking"; perhaps because disordered thought vented in disordered speech produces a disordered pleasure. Whatever the cause of its "production," even with the greatest sympathy we ultimately cannot understand such speech. Sympathetic understanding ends here, exhausted. Repeated chanting can at most produce in you a hazy "illusion of understanding" of it. The illusion of understanding is not understanding. From the highest heaven to the deepest underworld, nowhere (including in such speech) will you discover any meaning in it. Such speech is usually good for nothing — except to serve as an example: an example demonstrating something that is good for nothing except serving as an example.

From a series of articles called *Philosophical Diary* one can pick up a great pile of such examples at random. Here is a small sample:

"…precisely because it is truly illusory, it is a kind of reality… Morality is a 'possible impossibility' in human exis[tence]… an illusory reality… If, in terms of 'here,' 'there' may be a kind of 'real' illusion, otherwise we would fall into the illusory reality of 'here'… Precisely because impossible, it is man's only possibility… I must smash the a = a in expression, because only in morality is there a true a = a. And smashing the formal a = a in expression is restoring true morality." "Incompleteness is relation. Expression is incompleteness. Existence is not. But existence in expression is still incompleteness, is relation, is the relation to all relations… All numbers can be reduced to 1, 2, 3… Any thing is 1. Any thing's self-negation is 2. But a thing's self cannot be expressed; negation cannot be deduced; therefore any thing or 1 must begin from 3." "Degeneration is a kind of impossibility that twists possibility into impossibility… Only by making the impossible impossible, and then, with a sameness of reality, presenting a positively possible impossibility. In fact this is man's only possibility, i.e., approximate possibility" [Note 21].

Brief comments: (1) One need not swallow a whole rotten egg to know it is rotten, nor read the whole passage above to know it is unreadable. (2) To suppose that readers are all abnormal is itself an abnormal supposition. (3) Faking profundity serves precisely to expose shallowness. (4) Finally, one might as well spit out a round of nonsense to "express" the same (though nonsense, it is probably still more intelligible than the passage quoted) — Precisely because it is truly hypocritical, it is a kind of sincerity. ## Category II of linguistic harm: Empty verbiage

Above we discussed speech without meaning; now we discuss speech without information content.

### I. Tautologies and information content

Compare the two sentences below:

P: There are fish in the water of West Lake.

Q: There are fish in the water of West Lake, or there are no fish.

Both sentences are true [Note 22]. To determine P's truth, we must examine the relevant empirical facts. A sentence like P, "whose truth can only be determined by examining empirical facts," is called an "empirical truth." To determine Q's truth, by contrast, we need not research or investigate empirical facts; we need only understand Q's meaning (that is, know the usage of the terms concerned) to be able to determine that Q is true. A sentence like Q, "whose truth can be determined merely by understanding its meaning," is called a "tautology" （重言句） [Note 23].

For example, assertions like "All bachelors are unmarried" and "All dogs are canines" resemble Q: all are tautologies. To determine that "all bachelors are unmarried" is true, there is no need to invite the bachelors over for an inspection; we need only know the usage of the terms concerned — for example, know that the term "bachelor" （王老五） refers to unmarried men — to determine the statement is true. Likewise, to determine the truth of "all dogs are canines," there is no need to risk "being bitten" examining every kind of dog — including tiger dogs, shar-peis, silly dogs, and mad dogs, but not hot dogs — we need only understand the meaning of the terms concerned to determine it is a true statement. Others, such as "A triangle has three angles," "All objects occupy space," "If you attend my funeral, I probably cannot attend yours"… a little analysis shows all are tautologies.

Although tautologies and empirical truths are both true, their ways of being true differ. Take P and Q as illustrations. P is in fact true, but not necessarily true, because although there are in fact fish in West Lake's water, there could possibly be none (we cannot deny the logical possibility "there are no fish in West Lake's water"). Q, by contrast, is necessarily true — it holds in all possible circumstances — because whether or not there are fish in West Lake's water, "there are fish in West Lake's water or there are no fish" is true.

Note that although P lacks necessity, it has information content. By "having information content" is meant describing the state of empirical things [Note 24]. Q, conversely, though it has necessity, has no information content — it makes no description of the state of empirical things: precisely because Q holds in all possible circumstances, it describes no particular circumstance: from "there are fish in West Lake's water or there are no fish" we cannot infer whether there are, in the end, fish in West Lake's water or not.

Broadly speaking, empirical truths have no necessity [Note 25], but have a "compensation": the compensation is "having information content." Tautologies, conversely, have necessity, but must pay a "price": the price is "having no information content." (Note: "having no information content" is not equal to "having no meaning." Q, for example, has a fairly clear meaning, yet has no information content.)

Is tautologies' lack of information content a defect? (a) If a tautology is not put forward as a statement of empirical fact, then "lacking information content" is not its defect. (b) If a tautology *is* put forward as a statement of empirical fact, then "lacking information content" constitutes its defect. Points (a) and (b) are explained below.

(a) In pure mathematics, many propositions or theorems can be proved to be tautologies [Note 26]. For example, when "2" is defined as "1 + 1," "1 + 1 = 2" is a tautology. This tautology amounts to saying: "1 + 1 = 1 + 1." Such tautologies in pure mathematics are not put forward as statements of empirical fact; empirical facts can neither confirm nor refute such sentences. Even if one sheep "plus" one wolf equals one wolf, that cruel empirical fact cannot refute "1 + 1 = 2." Conversely, even if two 20-year-old lovers could be "substituted" for one 40-year-old wife, that encouraging affair could not confirm "20 + 20 = 40." Mathematical propositions of this kind can be used as tools of calculation rather than to describe empirical things. That being so, although such tautologies describe nothing about empirical things, that clearly cannot count as a defect [Note 27].

(b) But when a tautology masquerades as a statement of fact, "lacking information content" becomes a serious defect. Suppose a weather reporter announced on television: "Tomorrow it will rain or it will not rain; there may be a typhoon and there may not be a typhoon." Or a sociologist published his research results: "Based on long observation, it is known that the mothers of all juvenile delinquents are women. As to the sex of their fathers, further research awaits another research grant." Such a weather report and such research results would probably be fiercely attacked. But why attack them? Is what they say false? "Tomorrow it will rain or it will not rain," "the mothers of all juvenile delinquents are women," and so on are not false; they are true, indeed necessarily true — but these statements have no information content, yet are put forward as statements of fact. That is where their fault lies.

Let us call tautologies masquerading as statements of fact "**empty propositions**" （空廢命題）.

The various forms of empty propositions are analysed below.

### II. On empty propositions

#### (A) Empty talk used as grounds

"People who were never born never advocated birth control; all who advocate birth control are people who have been born — from which it can be seen that humans really are selfish animals who disregard others' lives and deaths." The grounds or reasons this argument relies on (the two sentences before "from which it can be seen") have no information content; it is not hard to see they are tautologies. In fact nobody would really use such tautologies as reasons to argue that "humans are selfish animals who disregard others' lives and deaths." Interestingly, "I am I" is also a tautology, but is often used as a reason: as a reason for not obeying regulations and for self-centredness. Similarly, "business is business" is often used as an excuse for "business need not heed business ethics." And the tautology "what is to happen will in the end happen" is often used as grounds for "there is no need to be proactive, no need to take the initiative to do anything." Yet such reasons, excuses, and grounds fundamentally do not stand. For instance, advising someone not to see a doctor when ill and not to dodge an oncoming car, because "what is to happen will in the end happen" — such grounds are obviously invalid. But when people utter such tautologies, they often act as if they had made a major discovery about the empirical facts of human life, creating an illusion that those sentences express some profound doctrine. In fact, when these tautologies masquerade as statements of fact, they are nothing but empty talk — empty propositions [Note 28]. These empty propositions have no more information content than the sentence "If your parents have no children, then you will have no children either," because: all empty propositions have equal information content, namely "zero information content"; all empty propositions make equal descriptions of empirical facts, namely they describe nothing at all.

#### (B) Concealed emptiness

Some empty propositions are more concealed than those with the form "x is x" [Note 29]; their "emptiness" takes a little more analysis to expose. Examples follow.

(1) Some people like to "criticise" language, logic, and science. Little do they know that criticising language already requires using language; any criticism, to be reasonable, must not violate logic; and to transmit the content of a criticism through modern media one must apply technology — that is, use the fruits of science. Setting that aside, we ask: what reason is there for criticising language, logic, and science? Critics like to offer this reason: "Language, logic, and science all have their limitations, their definite limits." But what does this statement mean?

Under questioning, critics often give explanations like: "Language is just language; language is not equal to the real world." "Logic is just logic; logic is not equal to human life." "Science is just science; science is not everything." The fault of such talk lies in: First, shooting at no target — who ever said language equals the real world (who would think "venturing into the world" equals "venturing into language," or that a "world war" equals a "language war")? Who ever said logic equals human life? Who ever said science is everything? Second, empty verbiage — what is said is just an empty proposition. To use such an empty proposition to "criticise" language, logic, and science is no different from accusing a thing of being merely the thing it "is" and not the thing it "is not," or accusing a method of being able to handle only the problems it can handle and not the problems it cannot handle. Such accusations are wholly invalid — like accusing human memory of "having its definite limitations": it can only remember the things it can remember and cannot remember the things it cannot remember — especially, it cannot remember future things.

(2) A certain philosophical viewpoint called "reflection theory" holds: "Concepts and the whole process of cognition are reflections of the objective world." [Note 30] Some reflection theorists go further and declare: every thought, every link of culture, bears a deep "imprint of class" and reflects social relations or class interests; even "myths are also reflections of objective social relations, only they are distorted reflections" [Note 31]. The question is: what counts as a "distorted reflection"? The word "reflection" is a very elastic word. A small child who gets zero on an exam, while being spanked by his mother, argues that his result "reflects a failed education system"; whether this defence stands is hard to determine, because the word "reflection" has great elasticity. But however great its elasticity, according to the word's customary use we cannot call just any concept of thought a reflection of some x. Yet some reflection theorists call every concept of thought a reflection of some x, because the reflection they speak of includes their so-called "distorted reflection," and their so-called "distorted reflection" can in turn equal what is ordinarily called "not reflecting." For example, people ordinarily think the concept "Pegasus" is not a reflection of the objective world, but those reflection theorists hold that all concepts "are reflections of the objective world" [Note 32]. But if "reflection" includes "distorted reflection," and the latter can equal "not reflecting," then when reflection theorists declare "every thought reflects class interests," what they have actually said is only the empty proposition "every thought reflects or does not reflect class interests."

#### (C) Forced definitions that manufacture emptiness （強定成空）

The last form of "empty verbiage" to be demolished is the use of forced definitions to manufacture empty propositions in an attempt to defend the doctrine one holds. For ease of exposing this sophistical technique, call it "forcing a definition to manufacture emptiness."

Theorists of innate goodness who hold that "all humans are innately good" (the lifeline of Confucian theory), when met with the counterexample "Mr X is human but not innately good," if they respond with "since he is not innately good, he cannot be called human," are forcing the word "human" to be defined as including the meaning "innately good" [Note 33]. That done, "all humans are innately good" becomes like "all mothers are female" — an empty proposition. That is one instance of "forcing a definition to manufacture emptiness." Again, a proponent of "talent will always meet its opportunity," who claims that if talent meets no opportunity, that shows what was possessed was not real talent, gives another instance: to rescue the fragility of the "talent-always-meets-opportunity theory," he covertly gives "possessing talent" a forced definition, making the original assertion an empty proposition.

Using forced definitions to argue a case is a widely adopted sophistical technique. I read this doctrine in a newspaper: "Chinese mothers put family affection first and romantic love second. Otherwise they are not Chinese mothers." [Note 34] This doctrine covertly defines "Chinese mother" as necessarily satisfying the condition "family affection first, romantic love second." The technique is of the same kind as that used in the following statements: (i) "There are no dishonest Christians; anyone dishonest cannot be called a Christian." (ii) "All Iranians are patriotic; anyone unpatriotic cannot be called an Iranian." (iii) "Every Nazi Party member is incorruptible; otherwise he is not a real Nazi Party member — at most a degenerated Nazi Party member, or a fake Nazi Party member who has infiltrated the Party."

That forced definitions can seemingly justify any statement shows precisely that this is a sophistical technique. Consider Oscar Wilde's famous saying:

"Men marry because they are tired of life; women marry because they are curious; in the end both are disappointed." Suppose this statement is refuted by counterexamples. In this case, if one insists on defending Wilde with a forced definition, one could say: those who do not marry from tiredness of life are not men; those who do not marry from curiosity are not women; and those who marry and are not disappointed are not human.

#### (D) A new *Romeo and Juliet*

Many empty forms of speech have been dissected above. In cognitive discussion, using empty propositions to engage in sophistry and muddling is of course an improper practice that should be criticised; but what if one uses empty propositions for play and joking, or to convey an implication beyond the words? Whether that is improper cannot be generalised; it depends on the context concerned [Note 35].

I have lately wanted to reopen the case of the cause of Romeo and Juliet's deaths; the research results are expressed in the form of a one-act play, in which Romeo's lines all look like empty propositions.

Whether these empty propositions should be criticised is best left to the reader's own judgement —

Juliet: "Romeo, Romeo, tonight I shall wait for you on the balcony. Will you come or not?"

Romeo: "If I come, I come."

Juliet: "Romeo, Romeo, I love red roses best. What about you?"

Romeo: "Red roses are red."

Juliet: "Romeo, Romeo, think: if your lips touched my face, what would happen?"

Romeo: "If my lips touched your face, then your face would touch my lips."

Juliet (petulantly): "You really are a bore. Let us meet again in ten years!"

Romeo (delighted): "In ten years you will be ten years older than now — if you live to that day."

Result: Juliet died of anger, and Romeo died of happiness.

## Category III of linguistic harm: Conceptual slippage

It is not rare for different interpretations of the same term to cause misunderstandings. Take the term "this kind of thing" itself: in the following story it causes a misunderstanding. It is said that a student at a village primary school arrived late and explained to the teacher: he was late because he had to take a boar to mate. The teacher asked: "Couldn't your father do this kind of thing?" The student answered: "No — it has to be the boar!"

The last category of linguistic harm this essay explores arises from giving different interpretations to a term.

### I. Conceptual confusion

If a term in a given context admits at least two different interpretations, then the term in that context is "ambiguous" [in the sense of being equivocal, 模稜兩可]. Suppose while shooting you accidentally hit a rich merchant, and his son, watching nearby, shouts: "Good!" If that short sentence in that situation can be interpreted both as rejoicing that the event happened and as praising your marksmanship, the sentence in that situation has the property of ambiguity.

Let us call the use of this ambiguity to mislead and muddle, or the specious speech caused by ambiguity, "**conceptual confusion**" [Note 36]. Example: "I asked to borrow a hundred dollars from you; you lent me only fifty, so you still owe me fifty. But since you lent me fifty, I also owe you fifty. Since each owes the other fifty, we're even — neither owes the other anything." This is a fallacious piece of conceptual-confusion reasoning, in which the word "owe" （欠） is ambiguous between (i) being in debt, not yet repaid; and (ii) lacking, falling short.

The example just given of conceptual confusion is fictional; now let us analyse some real cases.

#### (A) Sameness and communication

The British philosopher John Wisdom once asked: can two people do the same thing? The initial reaction is generally affirmative: of course two people can do the same thing — for example, if we each touch our own nose, is that not doing the same thing? But we can seemingly say that does not count as doing the same thing, because you touch your nose once and I touch my nose once: we touch different noses, so it is not doing the same thing. But if we touch the same nose — for example, we both touch your nose once — is that still not doing the same thing? It can be said to be still different, because when you touch your nose, you touch "your own" nose, and when I touch your nose, I touch "someone else's" nose; the result is that what we do remains different things.

In the discussion above, the word "same" （相同） is in fact ambiguous: it can be interpreted as "identical" （同一） or as "similar" or "of the same kind." Under the first interpretation, the sentence "No two people can do the same (i.e., identical) thing" is a tautology: necessarily true but wholly without information content. Merely by understanding the meaning of words like "identical," one can determine that the things done by any two people A and B can never be identical, because the one is "the thing A did" and the other "the thing B did," and A and B must be two different people — otherwise they could not be called "two people," A and B. Now look at the second interpretation: under it, "No two people can do the same (i.e., similar or same-kind) thing" has information content, but the information given is *false*, because, for example, when you touch your nose and I touch my nose, what we do is not the "identical" thing, but it is a "same-kind" or "similar" thing: the kind of thing called "touching one's own nose" [Note 37].

The above analysis can be transferred to criticise this doctrine: "Between people there can in fact be no communication, because no two people have the same experiences." This doctrine commits the fault of conceptual confusion: its grounds, "no two people have the same experiences," are like "no two people can do the same thing" — ambiguous. If "same" is interpreted as "identical," the grounds lack information content; but if interpreted as "similar" or "same-kind," the grounds give false information.

#### (B) Mathematics and experience

Many small children have met examination questions like this: "There are three birds on a tree. You shoot one dead. How many are left?" (Call this question D.) If the child answers "two are left," it's over — not "the answer is over" but "the child is done for" — because by the school's rule, the correct answer is "zero." The reason is said to be: the dead bird fell down, and the two [surviving] birds were frightened by the gunshot and flew away.

But stipulating that question D has a unique "correct answer" in this way is a product of conceptual confusion; it in fact lacks sufficient grounds and stifles children's flexibility of thought. D can be called an ambiguous question. If D is interpreted as asking "3 − 1 = ?", what is asked is a mathematical question [Note 38], whose answer is obviously "2." On the other hand, if D is interpreted as asking "After one of the three birds on the tree is shot dead, how many birds *in fact* remain on the tree?", what is asked is a question about empirical fact, and this question has no definite answer. After the shot, it is possible that no birds remain on the tree; possible that one, two, or even all three birds remain. For example —

Answer one: "0." The dead bird fell down; the live birds flew away.

Answer two: "1." The dead bird fell down; one bird flew away; another bird remained on the tree, because it was a deaf bird, or a silly bird, or neither deaf nor silly but stunned by the gunshot and unable to fly.

Answer three: "2." The dead bird fell down; [the other two] remained on the tree, because both were deaf birds, or silly birds, or stunned birds, or one deaf and one silly, or one silly and one stunned, or one stunned and one deaf.

Answer four: "3." [All] remained on the tree; the dead bird was caught by the branches and also remained on the tree.

Also: consider the possibility of "more than 3."

Note: it can be said that a dead bird is also a bird, just as a dead fish is also a fish. The fish eaten in a restaurant, when placed on the table, are usually dead; we cannot on that account call the manager over and scold him, saying his restaurant is not giving us fish to eat.

### II. Conceptual distortion

In this essay, the term "conceptual slippage" （概念滑轉） is used to cover both conceptual confusion and conceptual distortion. The "conceptual confusion" discussed above involves different interpretations of the same term, but those interpretations all conform to the word's meaning — to the term's customary use.

The "conceptual distortion" to be discussed below also involves different interpretations of the same term, but some of those interpretations *violate* the word's meaning: the term has no usage from which such an interpretation could be derived. To illustrate: the phrase "looking for a chief accountant" can be interpreted as (i) seeking to hire a chief accountant, or (ii) searching for a (runaway) chief accountant; both interpretations roughly conform to the word's meaning. By contrast, interpreting "physician" （內科醫生， literally "internal-medicine doctor") as "a doctor who specialises in treating henpeckedness" （懼內， "fearing what's inside [the home]"), or interpreting "seeing first is quickest" （先睹為快） as "peeking at the answers before an exam makes you answer faster," violates the words' meanings.

Let us call thought-confusion caused by (or by exploiting) interpretations that violate a word's meaning "**conceptual distortion**." Two types of conceptual distortion are dissected below.

#### (A) Taking words at face value (reading meanings off the surface, 望文生義）

Many people, as students, played the game of "taking words at face value": peeking at a neighbour's seat in the examination hall is called "scientific observation"; whispering to each other is called a "questionnaire survey." Naughty students, to show they were "heroic" and "had guts," would even, when the teacher spot-checked their explanations of idioms, deliberately distort and force interpretations, taking words at face value for fun. The teacher asks: "Chen Agou, what does 'The wife must first humiliate herself, and only then will others humiliate her' （夫人必自侮而後人侮之， a Mencius line in which 夫人 means "that person") mean?" Chen Agou: "Teacher, the sentence means: that wife must have humiliated herself first, and only then would her descendants （後人， "later people") humiliate her." The whole class roared with laughter; Chen Agou was made to stand as punishment and kept back after class.

Such face-value games are ordinarily harmless; everyone knows they are deliberate. But on other occasions, taking words at face value often leads to the distortion of concepts. For example:

(1) Some people call criticising a religion or its doctrines "infringing religious freedom" (or "infringing freedom of belief"). That is taking words at face value and distorting the concept of "religious freedom." Verbal criticism is not equal to prohibition in action. Prohibiting people from converting to a religion or conducting lawful religious activities [Note 39] certainly counts as infringing religious freedom; but criticising a religion or its doctrines cannot be called "infringing religious freedom." Believers have the right to propagate certain views and persuade people to believe them; why do others have no right to criticise those views and persuade people not to believe them? When theists assert that theism is correct, they equivalently assert that atheists' views are wrong. Why do atheists have no right to criticise theists' views as wrong? Everyone has the right to declare "all people are sinful," and everyone also has the right to declare that this is nonsense.

You have the right to say that others — themselves, their parents, siblings, ancestors eighteen generations back, and descendants eighteen generations forward — are all sinful; do others have no right to say that is sheer nonsense?

(2) The Han Confucian Dong Zhongshu has a famous saying: "Rectify the righteousness and do not scheme for profit; illuminate the Way and do not reckon merit." Today, the word "utility/merit" （功利） has become a derogatory term, and "utilitarianism" （功利主義） has been distorted into "a snobbish ideology of seeking profit only." In fact "utilitarianism" is the translation of the English *utilitarianism*. Roughly speaking, this philosophy takes "benefiting or advantaging the greatest happiness of the greatest number" as the criterion of good action — how is that a snobbish ideology of seeking profit only? Yet the distortion of the concept of "utilitarianism" has now sunk deeply into people's hearts; one can see how successful the first face-value readers were. It is truly worth drinking a toast: "Cheers — let us all perish together!"

#### (B) Calling a deer a horse （指鹿為馬）

In a society where concepts can be twisted at will, any promise, contract, or legal article becomes worthless: people, after breaking faith and violating agreements, will still say, as a matter of course and with eloquent justification, that they are keeping faith — they have merely "given the promises or articles concerned a completely new interpretation."

Such tampering with word meanings relies constantly on doctrines like "concerning concepts or terms, each person *may* have their own understanding and interpretation." But this doctrine is extremely absurd. If one insists on interpreting "Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism" （儒釋道） as "Christianity," even if someone says that is his own understanding and interpretation, such understanding and interpretation is still "not permissible." It is like a monkey jumping and banging at random on a piano: that cannot count as performing Beethoven's *Moonlight Sonata*, and cannot be called "giving every note of the *Moonlight Sonata* a unique interpretation."

The above point should not be hard to understand; regrettably, many people have an unconscious tendency of thought: to suppose that even if two things are poles apart, utterly different, once they are crowned with the same name, they must be "two species belonging to the same genus" [Note 40]. For example, two exactly opposite economies, if they are collectively called by the name "planned economy," will be thought to belong to the planned-economy genus as two of its species — even if one is a planned economy in which "all economic activities are within the plan" and the other is a planned economy in which "everything except the economy is within the plan."

This strange notion of "opposites of the same genus" paves the road for ambitious manipulators to invert black and white. In an environment where black and white are inverted: everyone having nothing except the privileged class can be named "public ownership"; the poorest and most helpless people can be called "bourgeois elements"; even having one's head chopped off by mistake can be called "happiness" — with no head, one will not grow pimples, will not get wrinkles, and will not suffer meningitis or lovesickness; is that not happiness? In short, in a world of distorted concepts and inverted black and white, a horse is a deer — a kind of deer without antlers; a deer is a horse — a kind of horse with antlers. A chicken's tail is a chicken's head — a kind of chicken head shaped like a chicken's tail; a chicken's head is a chicken's tail — a kind of chicken tail shaped like a chicken's head.

And so language today has been abused to a terrifying degree. What clearly is not x is called "alternative x" or "another kind of x," or even glorified as "real x." Many words, as long as they sound stirring, will be taken by ambitious manipulators to refer to the things they hope others will accept. "Freedom," "equality," "democracy," and so on are stirring words of this kind, and so are taken to refer to even the least free, least equal, least democratic systems or social conditions. They call these "another kind of freedom," "real equality," "the highest-grade democracy." What is "another kind of freedom"? At midnight, when nobody is whispering privately, the secret police can freely enter and leave your home and invite you away for a "private whisper" — that is "another kind of freedom." What is "real equality"? Ordinary folk are all without distinction; everyone's life is the same — like dogs; that is "real equality." As for democracy: a speaker on stage speaks ten thousand sentences equal to never having spoken half a sentence, nobody in the audience is listening, and before he finishes everyone raises their hands in opposition — that is only low-grade democracy. A speaker speaks half a sentence outweighing ten thousand, nobody in the audience dares show they are not listening, and the moment he finishes everyone raises their hands in approval — that is high-grade democracy. The lowest-grade democracy relies on counting heads to vote.

The highest-grade democracy can be divided into two types: the first is the educational type, which relies on clamping heads to guide the vote; the second is the economic type, which relies on smashing heads to save the vote.

But in fact all of this is nothing but the verbal trickery of "conceptual smuggling" （概念偷龍轉鳳）. The reality is: even if you call a grass-cutter an "alternative barber," a grass-cutter is still a grass-cutter; even if you call epilepsy "genuine soul-dancing," epilepsy is still epilepsy; even if you name a nuclear bomb a "high-grade duck egg," an "American duck egg," or a "Russian duck egg," is a nuclear bomb not still a nuclear bomb? What matters is recognising the substance that pretty words refer to. If everyone had this training, ambitious manipulators' attempts to "confuse substance by names" could not succeed. Let us remember Voltaire's observation: the Holy Roman Empire, despite its name, was in reality neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire.

## Conclusion: On closed systems

The three categories of linguistic harm have been analysed above. In general, these harms have one common feature: "**evadability of refutation**" — being able to evade being disconfirmed.

Look first at the first category, semantic ambiguity. Ambiguous speech generally has no truth or falsity to speak of: muddled, unclear speech can neither be identified as true nor as false. Since a statement without truth or falsity lacks even the qualification of being "false," it of course cannot be confirmed as false — in other words, it lacks "falsifiability." Look next at the second category, empty verbiage. As stated above, empty propositions are not based on empirical facts; empirical facts can neither support nor overturn such propositions, so whatever the state of the factual world, empty propositions will not be refuted. Finally, examine the third category, conceptual slippage. Speaking ambiguously, shifting unsettledly between different interpretations, is obviously a technique for evading refutation; and as for distorting concepts — forcibly altering a term's meaning for fear of being refuted — that is even more obviously a trick for evading refutation.

Let us call statements that use conceptual slippage to make themselves irrefutable [Note 41], or statements that are in themselves irrefutable ambiguous speech or empty propositions, "**closed statements**," and a system composed of closed statements a "**closed system**" [Note 42]. Such statements and the systems they compose can be called closed because, lacking falsifiability, they cannot be tested by factual evidence, and so are insulated from the objective facts or the real world — a kind of self-locking, self-secluding discourse "uninvolved with the world."

Examples will now explain this character of closed statements and closed systems.

### I. Pseudo-explanations

#### (A) The divine-will theory

After the famous great plague and the Great Fire of London occurred in England, the House of Commons formed a committee to investigate the causes of the disasters and found that they originated from God's will, because God had been angered. The committee finally determined that it was mainly the works of the atheist Hobbes that had angered God, and so ordered his books banned from publication in England. This remedial measure seemed very effective, because from then on England never again had such a great plague or Great Fire of London [Note 43].

Some people hold that everything that happens issues from God's will. For example: why was Eve tempted by the serpent? "That was God's will." Why did Judas betray Jesus? "That was God's will." Why did Pope John Paul II survive an assassination attempt? "That was God's will." Why did over thirty thousand people gather in a church during the Lisbon earthquake and all perish together? "That was God's will." Why do some people believe there is a God's will and some never believe it? "That, of course, is also God's will."

For convenience, call such invocations of "God's will" the "divine-will theory" （神旨說）. Some people exploit the divine-will theory to do as they please; others can never determine whether their so-called God's will is genuine, because the divine-will theory is precisely a closed system lacking falsifiability: no objective fact can be used to test its truth or falsity.

In a word, the divine-will theory can be likened to an insulated system unconnected with the factual world.

Concerning such insulated systems, the following points deserve attention:

First: people originally, because they did not understand why things are as they are, used "God's will" to explain them; but such an explanation not only cannot reduce people's perplexity, it may deepen it — namely, not understanding why God's will is thus rather than otherwise [Note 44].

Second: when we explain the occurrence of things by "God's will," if someone else declares that a "super-God's will" gives a deeper explanation — claiming it is the super-God's will that makes God's will thus — then in this situation it is hard for us to produce strong grounds that both support our own account and refute theirs.

Third: even setting the two points above aside, it is not hard to see that the divine-will theory is, cognitively, an ineffective explanation. Such an explanation can play only the role of a "hindsight Zhuge Liang" (a wise-after-the-event). For instance, after an earthquake, if St Peter's Church still stands undamaged, one says that is God's will. If the church has collapsed, afterwards one likewise attributes the event to God's will. But beforehand, there is no way to rely on "God's will" to predict whether that church will collapse. In short, the divine-will theory can only be used to "fire the cannon after the horse has bolted" — to make hindsight explanations.

The points above show that the divine-will theory is nothing but a kind of **pseudo-explanation**. Such explanations have no substance of explanation, only the appearance of explanation — an appearance manufactured with words like "because" and "therefore." Their explanation of things is as cheap as the explanation "x is thus because x is x" — for example: "Why did Napoleon lose at Waterloo?" "Oh, because Napoleon was Napoleon. If Napoleon had not been Napoleon but Wellington, he would not have lost at Waterloo!"

#### (B) The class-imprint theory

The divine-will theory is only one example of a closed system in the religious field; in other fields there are many similar examples. The theory, mentioned above, about "class imprints" or "reflecting class interests" (call it the "imprint theory," 烙印說） likewise constitutes a closed system. The imprint theory has been criticised above; only a few words here. A person imprinted by the imprint theory is like someone wearing a pair of "class analysis" glasses on the bridge of his nose.

Looking out through the frames, all social phenomena seem to receive a deep (in fact pseudo-) explanation: "All are stamped with the imprint of class, all reflect class interests — either reflect them faithfully, or reflect them distortedly." The result is that everything that happens in the human world is taken as "yet another instance" of the imprint theory's correctness — either a faithful instance, or a distorted instance?

### II. The truth-illusion of the self-appointed messengers of truth

Why can closed systems like the divine-will theory and the imprint theory, which seem to encompass everything, not be confirmed as false? It is nothing but the characteristics of linguistic harms that make them so [Note 45]. But many people cannot see through this mechanism; they instead suppose that the "irrefutability" of such systems shows precisely that they are absolute truth [Note 46]. This misunderstanding can be called the "illusion of truth." People with this illusion, once they think they have found an absolute truth that holds in all the world and works for all ages, easily tend to regard themselves as messengers of truth, and further produce a fanatical emotion of acting on Heaven's behalf. This messenger-of-truth mentality is extremely dangerous and terrifying; let me talk loosely about it as follows.

#### (A) Arrogant and narrowly exclusive

The (self-appointed, hereafter) messenger of truth is always correct, because he represents truth. His words should be prominently quoted in every discipline, because he represents truth. He is humanity's teacher; everyone else must listen to his instruction and be his good student, because he represents truth. He is qualified to conduct "thought inspections" and "thought reform" on others, because he represents truth. He has the authority to stipulate what others may say, what books they may read, what films they may watch, what music they may listen to, and even whom they may marry… because he represents truth. He knows that any view different from his own is wrong and must be prohibited, because he has already found the truth.

Regrettably, although the messenger of truth has "the heart to encompass the four seas and swallow the eight wastes," there is not only one messenger of truth in the world. There are messengers of truth of this school and messengers of truth of that school. Different messengers of truth hold different truths and enthusiastically peddle them to people — all free of charge. Each salesman likes to call the opposing side's absolute truth dogma, and his own dogma absolute truth. Such a way of peddling seems somewhat contrary to commercial ethics, so conflicts constantly arise. The parties to the conflicts accuse, hate, and loathe each other. They detest the other side and find it extremely ugly. They see, on the other side, their own projection.

#### (B) Blind fantasy, chasing wind and shadows

That messengers of truth mutually exclude each other is perhaps because God (it is said) is only one, and they all compete to play God. God created Heaven; God's impersonators want to bring Heaven to the human world, or build a human heaven on the spot — a utopia. When they cannot find utopia on the map, they draw one on themselves.

They want to plan the blueprint of human society according to their "absolute truth." Sadly, such blueprints are often impractical, because the "absolute truth" they rest on is nothing but a closed system detached from the real world. Using such a blueprint to guide practice naturally produces dangerous movements, like a blind man riding a blind horse.

A blind man riding a blind horse may be thrilling and fun, especially when hitting a nail. The question is: having hit a nail, will the messenger of truth give up his closed system? Of course not. Since what he holds is absolute truth, why let go? In the messenger of truth's eyes, giving up absolute truth is unimaginable. In fact, there is not much they can imagine.

But however that may be, the problem remains there: how to explain practical failures and frustrations? One common method is to shift blame — for example, attributing it to the poisonous legacy of the ancients, the contamination of outsiders, and especially the corrosion of enemies: "It must be that ill-intentioned, vicious, hidden enemies are sabotaging us; we must heighten our vigilance, escalate the struggle, and annihilate all the enemies." So says the believer in a closed system.

This mentality of chasing wind and shadows, suspicious of gods and ghosts, once it infects a group, makes the group grave and humourless, with abnormal tendencies to collective neurasthenia, collective oversensitivity, collective nervous tension, even collective insanity [Note 47]. At such a time, almost everyone under Heaven has become an enemy: there are ferocious enemies, smiling enemies, distant enemies, enemies at one's side, external enemies, internal enemies, visible enemies, invisible enemies, yesterday's enemies, today's enemies, tomorrow's enemies, and finally enemies who are not enemies.

#### (C) The saviour mentality of acting on Heaven's behalf

Besides the characteristics described in sections A and B, messengers of truth have a most dangerous characteristic: they want to act on Heaven's behalf; they want to be saviours of the world.

Those who think they have found absolute truth having such ambitions seems natural. In the process of "acting on Heaven's behalf," the messenger of truth represents Heaven, represents the Way — that is, represents absolute truth. When elated to the point of forgetting himself, he may even regard himself as a god. Such a messenger of truth naturally does not like others to have freedom of speech and independent thinking. Since someone (himself) has already found the absolute truth, what do you still need to discuss or think about? Just believe, and you will be saved. Just follow the messenger-of-truth saviour, and you can enter Heaven — earthly or spiritual heaven.

To take Heaven or utopia as the ideal of acting for Heaven, and to take all living beings as the means of acting for Heaven — that is the typical mentality of those who act on Heaven's behalf. As means, living beings appear insignificant compared with the ideal of acting for Heaven. So although the original intention of acting was for living beings, living beings have now become tools for acting. Once people are not treated as people but as means and tools, it becomes easy to rationalise — to "give a dignified justification to" — any ugly practice in the name of an end or ideal. For example, cruelty and violence, stopping at nothing to achieve an end, yet still self-righteously decorating it with pretty excuses: glorified as "all for the needs of realising the ideal." The result: though the ideal held up is a utopia or Heaven, the reality produced is a human hell. Yet those who act on Heaven's behalf are unmoved by this. They have wills of steel and the virtue of endurance; they are best at enduring other people's suffering.

All the above situations need especial vigilance in the two fields of religion and politics, which affect very many people. Open the history books and it is not hard to find: the wars, slaughters, persecutions, atrocities, and devastation caused in order to "act on Heaven's behalf" are shocking to behold. The barbarity, cruelty, and irrationality of these events make one feel both terror and deep sorrow. Here we must truly reflect on a question: what lesson, in the end, have these history-book human tragedies that fill one with boundless sorrow taught humanity? One of the greatest lessons these tragedies teach us is this: from saviour of the world to enslaver of the world is only a hair's breadth; from messenger of truth to forger of tragedy is only half a step.
