# A Conversation with Dr Li Tianming on Methods of Thinking (與李天命博士談思考方法）

*English translation. Source: extraction file `ch07 - 導引篇 與李天命博士談思考方法.md`, book 002 — Li Tianming, 《李天命的思考藝術》 (*The Art of Thinking*), Introductory Part. Interview by the Editorial Board of* Student Affairs*, The Chinese University of Hong Kong; originally published in* Student Affairs*, issue 16, 1 September 1983.*

---

Hong Kong's primary and secondary education is often criticised as "spoon-feeding" （填鴨式， literally "force-feeding the duck"). But Dr Li Tianming, lecturer in the Philosophy Department of the Chinese University, points out that if "force-feeding" means memorising a lot of books, then this force-feeding is actually quite nutritious. In the years when he received his secondary education, students memorised far more articles, in both Chinese and English, than they do now. Although they may not have understood them while memorising, this later proved greatly beneficial to their ability to express themselves in both languages. Conversely, if "force-feeding" means a lack of opportunities to think and to explore independently, then it is indeed a bad phenomenon in education. For example, students who memorise by rote the notes given by teachers, or the "standard answers" to questions teachers guess will appear in that year's public or university entrance examinations, have their interest in learning stifled and their development constrained: on the one hand the student's attention is forced to concentrate solely on examination-related topics, restricting their interest in learning; on the other hand, in answering every kind of question they can only take the teacher's "standard answers" as their guide, limiting their personal development and their opportunities for independent thinking. In fact, the effects of these conditions are already visible in many university students. For example, when students answer examination questions, they seriously lack organisational ability; most scripts are not clear enough in either wording or line of thought, and teachers can only do their best to guess what the student means to say. This is directly connected with students' lack of training in independent thinking and in organising language during their secondary years.

## I. Training the ability to express oneself

Dr Li points out that whether one's ability to express oneself is good or bad depends mainly on two aspects: language and thinking. To train a good ability of expression, on the language side there is an ancient and effective method: memorise, or read intensively, many good articles. Young people today spend too much time on television, radio, and newspapers; good articles seldom appear in those places. As for thinking: to have correct methods of thinking, one usually needs to receive basic course training. Under Hong Kong's present education system, there are almost no thinking-method training courses in primary and secondary schools, and few such arrangements at tertiary level. This seems to reflect a very strange assumption on the part of the education authorities: the assumption that thinking, unlike Chinese, English, mathematics, and science, does not need to be learned and trained. But in fact many ways of thinking are incorrect, and correct methods of thinking often have to be learned before they can be had. Dr Li suggests that it would be best to set up some elementary thinking-method courses in secondary schools, teaching the shallower principles, and that universities should also make them compulsory. For thought and education are especially closely related. Propaganda aims to think *in place of* its target audience, hoping the other party will accept completely without thinking; education aims to inspire those being educated to think, hoping students will decide for themselves, after their own thinking, whether to accept. In these circumstances, teaching students correct methods of thinking is obviously very important.

## II. Principles of thinking method

As to the content of thinking methods, Dr Li says that the discipline of thinking methods （思考方法學） mainly comprises the following items: **semantic analysis （語理分析）, logical method, scientific method, and the analysis of fallacies （謬誤剖析）**. The essential task of semantic analysis is to clarify problems, thoughts, and concepts. The primary function of logical method is to audit whether arguments are correct. Scientific method aims to provide a set of procedures or norms by which empirical knowledge can be obtained or appraised. And the analysis of fallacies is an extension and supplement of the three items above: its role is to define and classify the erroneous ways of thinking commonly encountered, so that they are easy to identify when met. Among these four items, **semantic analysis is the most basic**: its use enables us to work out what the problem we are thinking about is actually asking. If, in a discussion, even the problem is not clear, then the whole discussion fundamentally cannot proceed effectively. **The analysis of fallacies, by contrast, is often the most practical**: it enables us, in discussion and thinking, to avoid errors of thought. For example, some people, in discussion, make personal attacks, appeal to irrelevant authorities, or say things unrelated to the topic in order to evade the problem — all of these violate the correct principles of thinking and discussion.

## III. Independent thinking and personal ideals

How, then, should we think about personal ideals or goals in life? Dr Li points out that at the secondary stage, students spend most of their time continually receiving all kinds of information, with fewer opportunities for reflection. University education works differently: there are more opportunities for discussion in class and for exchange with fellow students outside class, making it very suitable for thinking about questions such as one's direction in life. But we should know that the nature of such questions differs from that of scientific questions. Scientific questions have objective, or nearly objective, answers, whereas questions of ideals and the meaning of life cannot have their answers proved by evidence (this does not, of course, mean that they are arbitrary or groundless; our views on these questions are all influenced by factors such as personal experience, background, and learning). At the same time, these are questions that nobody else can answer in your place. In science we can divide labour and cooperate: for example, the principles of physics that architecture uses have already been experimentally proved by physicists, and the architect does not need to verify them again himself. But answers to questions such as the meaning of life must be thought out by ourselves. One goal of university education is precisely to help students establish their own principles and ideals.

Finally, Dr Li emphasises that to establish goals and principles, the most important thing is to possess the ability to think independently. (This does not mean being deliberately eccentric, intentionally opposing tradition, or contriving to be different from others; it means cultivating in oneself command of a set of correct thinking methods.) For example, society has many "social games" that follow established rules — for instance, Hong Kong people generally measure the degree of success by the quantity of money — but these rules are not necessarily right. We should examine them through our own thinking and reflection, and only then decide whether to accept or reject them, or even set up other ideals and standards. Therefore, what matters most is still being able to use correct thinking methods to think independently.
