## Introduction: Thinking methods and worked examples of critical disputation

The principle of rescuing a drowning person is: get the person out of the water, then get the water out of the person.

Knowing this "drowning-rescue principle" is not equal to being able actually to do the work of rescuing a drowning person. Likewise, knowing the methods of thinking is not equal to being able concretely to apply those methods to handle problems, still less to being able to apply them nimbly and exquisitely. To improve one's ability to apply thinking methods, a very effective route is to learn through worked examples of critical disputation. The aim of this essay is to provide (hopefully) exactly such examples.

Of the responses provoked by *God Does Not Exist?!* and *Critical Thinking and Religion*, Dr Leung Yancheng's *A Review of Li Tianming's "Critical Thinking and Religion"* (abbreviated *Review*) attracted the most attention; next come Mr Lam Shui-kei's article *Surprised that Li Tianming Failed to Hit the Mark with a Single Arrow* (abbreviated *Surprised*) and Mr Cheng Chi-shing's book *Religion and Critical Reflection — A Response to Dr Li Tianming's "Critical Thinking and Religion"* (abbreviated *Religion*) [Note 1]. My work *From the Journey of the Bloody Road to the Journey to the Kingdom of Heaven* takes the *Review* as critical material in its main text, in order to construct an anti-sophistry system, and mentions *Surprised* and *Religion* only in a footnote. Below I briefly analyse and criticise *Surprised* and *Religion*, hoping to provide worked examples of critical disputation.

## I. Grandiloquent talk

The affirmative side of the "September Debate" (whose record was published under the title *God Does Not Exist?!*) was Michael Horner （韓那）; I was the negative side. The author of *Surprised* says he himself "is a devout Catholic, but… from the record of the September Debate, Horner's arguments are full of holes and should have been broken with one blow. But Li Tianming also failed to seize the opponent's weaknesses and deliver a fatal blow — a great pity." He therefore declared he would "take up the knife himself and settle Horner's fallacies." Can this righteous, kin-slaying feat of *Surprised* — "taking up the knife himself" — succeed?

### (A) Pseudo-refutation

*Surprised* says: "To prove God exists, Horner listed six great 'evidences'… If these six great aspects 'each had' a god presiding over it, how could Horner justify himself? If Horner admits these 'six' do not necessarily have anything in common, are not necessarily the same one, then every subsequent assertion is wasted effort." [Note 2]

Suppose Horner rebutted thus:

"In my argumentation, the word 'God' is used to refer to the unique Jehovah spoken of by Christianity. One cannot infer, from the word 'God' appearing in different arguments I put forward, that the word refers to different gods in those arguments — just as one cannot infer, from the name 'Li Tianming' appearing in many different sentences in *Surprised*, that the name refers to many different Li Tianmings in *Surprised*.

"To refute my disputation, you must actually point out what is wrong with the six arguments I put forward (for example, whether they are semantically ambiguous, whether they commit logical fallacies); you cannot merely say 'if these six great aspects "each had" a god presiding…' or 'if Horner admits these "six" are not necessarily the same one…'. If there were different gods in different aspects — if there were many different gods — then of course my disputation could not stand. But my disputation did give arguments that can prove: only the unique God spoken of by Christianity exists. In short, *Surprised*'s merely advancing its 'if…' and 'suppose…' talk and treating that as having refuted my disputation is nothing but a pseudo-refutation." [Note 3]

I wonder how the author of *Surprised* would respond to the above rebuttal?

### (B) Pseudo-proof

Standing on the negative side, I originally had no need to defend Horner, the affirmative; but since this essay's aim is to provide worked examples of critical disputation, the passage above also happened to point out the cracks *Surprised* itself exposed while criticising Horner. Now I dissect *Surprised*'s other cracks as follows.

After claiming to have exposed "the fatal wound in Horner's assertions," *Surprised* proceeds to publish its own proof of "God exists." *Surprised* says: "Understand God from the angle of 'love.' This is the whole of Christianity's most important 'reality' concerning God. It is the most important clear evidence of God's existence."

What does the so-called "understanding God from the angle of 'love'" mean? If I say "understand Marilyn Monroe from the angle of 'love'," do you know what that means? Charitably understood, "understanding God from the angle of 'love'" roughly means: "God loves the people of the world; we should understand it thus."

The question is: does that prove God exists? Since ancient times, many philosophers and theologians have racked their brains and exhausted their ingenuity trying to prove God exists, but none has won general recognition of success. Now *Surprised*, with one sentence — "understand God from the angle of 'love'" — proves God exists: that is indeed no small matter. Sadly: First, understanding is not equal to proof. Understanding God from the angle of "love" is not equal to presenting the most important clear evidence of God's existence — just as understanding the devil from the angle of "hate" is not equal to presenting the most important clear evidence of the devil's existence.

Second, if there is no God, there is no question of God loving the people of the world. "God exists" is a precondition of "God loves the people of the world"; or rather, "God loves the people of the world" presupposes "God exists." When we are discussing whether God exists, "God loves the people of the world" is a thesis awaiting proof, not a thesis already proved; to use "God loves the people of the world" to prove God exists is to use a thesis awaiting proof as if it were a thesis already proved — and that commits the logical fallacy called "begging the question" （乞求論點） or "stealing the thesis" （竊取論點）.

### (C) Slapping one's own face

*Surprised* says: "As a Catholic, I completely endorse 'every point' of Horner's. But endorsing is not proving. For friends without faith, these six great points put together are undoubtedly self-contradictory."

People lacking logical common sense sometimes merely vaguely "feel" that a statement or a state of affairs is amiss or "problematic" (without being able to specify what is amiss, what the problem is) and then declare that the statement or state of affairs "contains a contradiction" — for instance:

"Warm-as-fire enthusiasm wins fur coats more easily than cold-as-frost aloofness — a real contradiction!" In fact, in logic, "contradiction" or "self-contradiction" has a strict definition [Note 4]. The relation between a statement (or proposition, concept) and its negation is called "being mutually contradictory." Simultaneously asserting two mutually contradictory statements is called "self-contradiction" [Note 5].

For example, asserting "This is a stone and (in the same sense) is not a stone" is self-contradiction. But *Surprised* fundamentally cannot point out what self-contradiction there is when Horner's "six great points are put together."

Incidentally, the phrase "for friends without faith… undoubtedly self-contradictory" is undoubtedly ignorant talk. Whether there is self-contradiction has nothing whatever to do with "for whom." For example, the assertion "Li Bai is a poet" contains no self-contradiction; then, no matter for whom, the assertion contains no self-contradiction. And the assertion "Li Bai is a poet and is not a poet" contains self-contradiction; then, no matter for whom, the assertion contains self-contradiction [Note 6].

The above analysis shows that *Surprised* does not understand what self-contradiction is; the analysis below will show that *Surprised* is quite good at slapping its own face.

Example one. *Surprised* at one moment says "Horner's arguments are full of holes," and at another says "the six points [arguments] Horner listed completely stand"; on the one hand it says Horner's "six great propositions have no problem in themselves" and "nobody can refute them," and on the other hand says "putting the six points [propositions] side by side is the fatal wound in Horner's assertions."

If the so-called six great propositions "cannot be refuted by anyone, have no problem in themselves, and completely stand," then those propositions "put side by side" still completely stand, because, by a simple logical rule [the formula is partly lost in the extraction], any set of individually standing propositions, put together, still stands (true statements do not become untrue by being put together — a point easy to understand even without studying logic). Since, "put side by side," they still stand, how can that be a "fatal wound"?

Example two. *Surprised* holds that since Horner's six propositions all speak of "God," he must prove that the God spoken of is the same one. Merely because Horner gave no proof — merely because of this — *Surprised* calls that "Horner's fallacy," "fallacious doctrine," calls it "self-contradictory" and "full of holes," and says it is "trapping himself with no escape" and will even "bring calamity to the human world." Such accusations probably exceed the powers of human understanding. Interestingly, *Surprised* itself also speaks of "God" many times (more than six), and likewise gives no proof that the God spoken of is the same one. Following *Surprised*'s own line of thought, we could say this is *Surprised*'s fallacy and fallacious doctrine, is self-contradictory and full of holes, is trapping itself with no escape, and will even bring calamity to the human world.

Conclusion: *Surprised*'s so-called "taking up the knife himself to settle Horner's fallacies" is only overconfident grandiloquent talk.

## II. Fallacies and factual errors

*Surprised* says "Horner's arguments are full of holes"; *Religion*, however, says "Horner has already presented sufficient and rich arguments" (p. 28). I too think Horner's arguments are full of holes, but do not think *Surprised* can "settle Horner's fallacies," for the reasons analysed above. Since I agree that "Horner's arguments are full of holes," that implies I disagree with *Religion*'s "Horner has already presented sufficient and rich arguments." Below I dissect the various errors *Religion* commits.

The errors of the human world include (though are not only) two large categories. One category is called "fallacies" （謬誤）; the other can be called "factual errors" （訛誤）. Roughly defined: errors in ways of thinking, let us call "fallacies" [Note 7]. For example: "Pursuing someone of the opposite sex is like patiently angling; since the hook will tear the fish's lips, pursuing someone of the opposite sex will tear lips."

That erroneous inference commits the fallacy of "strained analogy" （牽強比附） (analogising a country to a mother often leads to this fallacy). Others, such as self-contradiction, hasty generalisation (taking the part for the whole), begging the question, and shifting the thesis, can all be classed as fallacies.

On the other hand, errors of knowledge, let us call "factual errors" [Note 8].

For example, thinking the Earth is flat, thinking nitric acid is sulphuric acid, thinking the knack of catching cobras lies in "breaking their spectacles" — such errors of knowledge are not called "fallacies" in logic; by the definition just given, such errors can be called "factual errors."

I now exhibit the fallacies and factual errors *Religion* commits, as follows.

### (A) Various fallacies

"Horner has finally been judged the loser!" *Religion* sighs. A debater who "campaigned north and south" and had never lost before — "in Hong Kong, for the first time ever, he lost!" *Religion* sighs again. "Dr Li was voted an overwhelming victory," but, *Religion* holds, in fact "in content Horner held an absolute overwhelming advantage." That being so, "why did he [Horner] lose?"

That is the question *Religion* sets out to explore. The result of the exploration: the key lies in "the techniques and tactics in the 'September Debate'"; the negative side won by these, and the audience "appreciated technique more than content." In fact "Li Tianming could neither attack in advancing nor defend in retreating. The component of critical thinking was very weak, very weak." Conversely, "Horner's replies were grounded and evidenced; his six arguments all stand unshaken" (pp. 14, 33, 34).

Below I examine whether the assertions *Religion* makes are grounded and evidenced, and whether they can stand unshaken.

#### (1) The fallacy of appeal to ignorance

Analysing the "September Debate," *Religion* errs from the very start. *Religion* says: "The affirmative's position is: believing God exists is more reasonable. The method Horner used to maintain his position was to prove God exists. If the negative side cannot break his arguments, God's existence has obtained proof" (p. 2).

This statement commits the logical fallacy called "appeal to ignorance" （訴諸無知）. An erroneous argument does not become a correct argument because others cannot break it. One side of a debate may be unable, through ignorance, to break the other side's argument. If one infers, from a statement not having been proved erroneous (not having been broken), that the statement is correct, one commits the fallacy of appeal to ignorance [Note 9]. For example, if we debate an ignorant small child with specious arguments, telling him: "Pigs have two ears; you also have two ears; so you are a pig." Or: "Your grandmother worships God, so God exists, because if there were no God, how could your grandmother worship Him?" Suppose the ignorant child cannot break these arguments, and we infer from this that these arguments stand — then we commit the fallacy of appeal to ignorance. Similarly, even if the negative side cannot break Horner's arguments, inferring from that that "God's existence has obtained proof" commits exactly the fallacy of appeal to ignorance.

#### (2) The self-defeating fallacy

If a statement, logically or in effect, has the character of "damaging and disabling itself," or "one part of itself conflicting with another part of itself," the statement commits the fallacy of "self-defeat" （自我推翻）. A notice on a wall saying "No Posting" is suspect of self-defeat. In *Religion*, self-defeating fallacies are not hard to find. Its author on the one hand says: "I am not here championing Horner" (p. 32), but on the other hand says: "Horner's replies were grounded and evidenced; his six arguments all stand unshaken" and "in content Horner held an absolute overwhelming advantage" (pp. 33, 34). The question is: if this does not count as "championing Horner," what would? Would he have to "embrace him to defend him" for it to count [Note 10]?

#### (3) The fallacy of false-value transmission

According to logic, an argument is valid （對確） if and only if it satisfies the following condition: **if the premises are true, then the conclusion must also be true** [Note 11]. From this definition it follows that in a valid argument, the truth of the premises can guarantee the truth of the conclusion. But note: the falsity of the premises does not guarantee the falsity of the conclusion. For example: [an example given earlier in the author's works, whose text is partly compressed in the extraction:] this argument's premises are false, yet its conclusion is true.

To infer "the conclusion is false" from "the premises are false" (as if one supposed the premises' "false value" is transmissible, transferable to the conclusion) — such erroneous inference, let us call the "**fallacy of false-value transmission**" （假值傳遞的謬誤）. Many people's statements like "The major premise has been overthrown, so the conclusion has also been overthrown" and "The theory's foundation has collapsed, therefore the theory's thesis has also collapsed" all commit the fallacy of false-value transmission [Note 12].

This fallacy can be called the fallacy that people who talk about logic should least commit. Its being a fallacy concerns only the ABC of logic — the elementary stage of elementary logic. Remarkably, *Religion*, under a heading that discusses logic (discussing "critical thinking and the syllogism"), says: "Because the major premise is not true, the conclusion is also not true" (p. 14). That statement commits exactly the "fallacy people who talk about logic should least commit" — the fallacy of false-value transmission.

[Incidentally: a book called *How to Correct Logical Errors* (Zhejiang Education Publishing House, 1984 edition) declares: "If an inference's premises are not true, its conclusion necessarily is not true" (p. 77). One need only read a famous introductory logic book, like those by W. Salmon or I. M. Copi, or simply look up the "Chopin is a monkey" example mentioned earlier, to know how to correct this logical error of *How to Correct Logical Errors* [Note 13].]

### (B) Various factual errors

The fallacies of *Religion* have been partly dissected above; below I point out part of its factual errors.

#### (1) Factual errors about the syllogism

Making a mistake while reasoning with a syllogism is an error in a way of thinking: a fallacy. Making erroneous statements about the nature or function of the syllogism is an error of knowledge: a factual error.

*Religion* says: "Whether presenting or defending one's grounds, or counter-attacking the other side's grounds, if there is a thought component present, the simplest and clearest form of expression is syllogistic reasoning" (p. 14). In logic, this is an obviously amateurish statement.

First, the syllogism is an argument form with a certain type of logical structure [Note 14]; an argument is composed of premises (grounds) and a conclusion (thesis); what the syllogism "expresses" is not the grounds but the argument. Second, the phrase "…if there is a thought component present" is unintelligible. What is an argument or ground with "a thought component present," and what is one without? What has whether an argument or ground has "a thought component present" to do with syllogistic reasoning?

Amateurish utterances of this kind are too numerous in *Religion* to list. Setting that aside and understanding charitably, the quoted passage wants to say: whether establishing a position by argument or rebutting by argument, "the simplest and clearest form of expression is syllogistic reasoning." But that statement is still factually erroneous. Through mathematical logic, it is easy to prove that infinitely many valid arguments cannot be expressed by syllogisms. A famous example is: "All circles are figures; therefore, anyone who draws a circle draws a figure." This argument cannot be expressed by a syllogism — a result that greatly troubled traditional logicians who knew only syllogisms and not mathematical logic. Expressed by mathematical logic, however, it is easy: [the formalisation, in quantifier notation, is partly lost in the extraction].

One should know that the syllogism occupies only a tiny position in modern logic. The recognised system of modern logic is called "standard first-order logic"; one of its two great departments is "first-order quantification logic," which contains "monadic quantification logic" and "polyadic quantification logic." The syllogism is only a small item within the monadic quantification logic within first-order quantification logic within standard first-order logic within modern logic [Note 15]. There are large numbers of valid arguments that cannot be brought under the syllogism; the syllogism fundamentally cannot serve as those arguments' form of expression. That being so, still less is it their "simplest and clearest form of expression."

#### (2) Factual errors produced by misinterpretation

As stated above, the relation between a proposition and its negation is called "mutual contradiction." For example, the negation of "area A is greater than area B" is "area A is not greater than area B"; these two propositions are mutually contradictory. Two mutually contradictory propositions cannot both be true and cannot both be false: if this one is true the other is false; if this one is false the other is true. On the other hand, if two propositions cannot both be true but can both be false, the relation between them is called "contrary opposition" （相反對立）.

For example, "area A is greater than area B" and "area B is greater than area A" are contrary opposites: they cannot both be true, but can both be false — when A and B are equal, both propositions are false.

In the "September Debate," the affirmative's thesis was "believing God exists is more reasonable" (or expressed as "theism is more reasonable than atheism"), and the negative's thesis was "believing God exists is not more reasonable" (or "theism is not more reasonable than atheism"). By the definitions above, the logical relation between the affirmative and negative sides is "mutual contradiction," not "contrary opposition." ("Theism is more reasonable than atheism" and "atheism is more reasonable than theism" are the two propositions that are contrary opposites.) But *Religion* says: "Horner maintains 'believing God exists is more reasonable,' Li Tianming maintains 'believing God exists is not more reasonable'; these two claims belong to contrary opposition" (p. 16). That statement is a factual error [Note 16].

*Religion*'s factual errors are numerous; there is no need to record them all. I point out only one more category of a similar nature.

*Religion* says: "Li Tianming denies God's existence" (p. 22). That statement distorts the negative side's position in the "September Debate." Denying God's existence is denying theism, but the negative side in the "September Debate" did not deny theism; it only denied "theism is more reasonable than atheism."

*Religion* also says: "Although Li Tianming calls his position not 'denying' God's existence but only 'agnosticism,' nevertheless, from Li's position, agnosticism and atheism can be said to be no different — indeed, to go even further" (ibid.).

This passage hides more than one factual error. First, from the debate record it can be seen that the negative side never "called its position… only 'agnosticism'" [Note 17]. Second, atheism asserts that there is no God; agnosticism asserts that human beings cannot know whether there is a God or not; the two are very different — on what basis can one say "from Li's position, agnosticism and atheism can be said to be no different…"?

To summarise: *Religion*, in analysing the "September Debate," committed the fallacy of appeal to ignorance from the very start. It then tried to "reorganise with syllogisms" the two sides' disputation, while its understanding of the syllogism contained factual errors. When it came to analysing the logical relation between the two sides' theses, it wrongly interpreted "mutual contradiction" as "contrary opposition."

Finally, *Religion* even distorted the negative side's basic position, claiming the negative "denies God's existence," whereas the negative repeatedly declared that it does not deny theism but only denies "theism is more reasonable than atheism." (The above lists only a "drop in the ocean" of *Religion*'s errors — see below and the notes.)

## III. The crux of debate

In the process of rebutting "theism is more reasonable than atheism," the negative side of the "September Debate" put forward two key points: first, the affirmative's so-called explanations are only seeming explanations; second, the statement "the omnipotent, omnipresent… God" is ambiguous and suspect. I elaborate below.

### (A) Seeming explanations

Some causal explanations can be verified as false [Note 18]; such explanations belong to "erroneous explanations." For example: "Because the laxative can cure coughs, that man stopped coughing after taking it." This erroneous explanation can be verified as false. (That the man stopped coughing after taking the laxative may be because his stomach was weak and he "dared not" cough; in any case it is not "because the laxative can cure coughs.")

Erroneous explanations can be verified as false, but some causal explanations do not even possess verifiability. For example, "because there is God's blessing in the laxative, that man stopped coughing after taking it" — such an explanation lacks verifiability. A causal explanation lacking verifiability is only a "seeming explanation" （貌似解釋） (a pseudo-explanation) [Note 19].

When scientists explain the macroscopic world by the microscopic world, the explanations they establish are verifiable; their inference from the existence of the explanandum (the macroscopic world) to the existence of the explanans (the microscopic world) conforms to the norms of methodology [Note 20].

Conversely, some people use God to explain things, attempting to infer from the existence of the things explained to God's existence; but such inferences do not conform to the norms of methodology, because the explanations concerned have no verifiability at all [Note 21].

In the debate, the negative side pointed out that the affirmative's six arguments all [suffer from the flaw described above — characters partly corrupted in the extraction]. Horner replied: "Another of Dr Li's key points is what an explanation is. I have some difficulty understanding this key point of his… He erects a straw man, knocks it down, and then says he has knocked down my argument" [Note 22]. *Surprised* says: "the six great propositions [arguments?] have no problem in themselves." *Religion* says: "Horner defended watertightly; the six arguments were throughout powerful arguments" (p. 17). Such replies and comments expose a lack of methodological training. Every argument the affirmative put forward uses God to explain things (or what the affirmative took to be facts), and then infers God's existence. The negative side pointed out that such inferences are wholly invalid, because the explanations are nothing but seeming explanations lacking verifiability — like explaining rain by "God's will" — with no predictive power, able only to act as "hindsight cannons" [Note 23]. That is a rebuttal striking the vital point; how can it be called "attacking a straw man"?

In brief: we ordinarily explain a knocking sound by "someone is knocking at the door"; such an explanation has verifiability. But if we explain the knocking sound by "an undetectable demon manipulating people's wills, making them knock," such an explanation has no verifiability. Similarly, Horner's explaining things by "God exists" is nothing but a seeming explanation lacking verifiability [Note 24].

### (B) "Specifying" is not "clarifying"

Every argument of Horner's contains the concept "(the omnipotent, omnipresent…) God"; the negative side pointed out that this concept is ambiguous and suspect and needs clarifying. Horner replied: "I gather that he [the negative side] completely accepts that we are together debating the God of Christians, so I assumed he knows… the Christians' God is omnipotent…" [Note 25]. *Religion* agrees: "He [Horner] does not need to analyse and fix the concept of God, because everyone knows what the God in the debate refers to" (p. 33). *Surprised* likewise says: "Horner entered the battle in his capacity as a Christian missionary; the God in his mind is definitely the God as Christians conceive Him. On this point there is really no need for further specification" [Note 26].

Such replies show that these discussants fundamentally do not understand where the "breaking power" of the negative side's question lies: they think that if the affirmative merely indicates or "specifies" that the God spoken of is the God Christianity speaks of, the negative's question is dissolved. But in fact the negative never asked the affirmative to "specify" which religion's god the God spoken of is (who does not know that "Horner entered the battle in his capacity as a Christian missionary"?); the negative only questioned what the concept or word "God" that the affirmative used *means*. The "specification" described above is not equal to the "clarification" the negative spoke of. Even if it is specified that the God spoken of is the omnipresent, omnipotent God Christianity speaks of, that does not equal clarifying what "omnipresent" and "omnipotent" mean. (Does the meaning of "God is omnipresent" include "God is inside our ears and large intestines"? Does the meaning of "God is omnipotent" include "God can create something that cannot be destroyed"?)

Suppose a "Super-God Religion" declared it had proved that there exists a "Super-God" who is omnivomitous, omnisuper, both super-vomiting and super-super. Others ask for clarification of what "omnivomitous," "omnisuper," "super-super," and such terms mean; its apologist says: "I enter the battle in my capacity as apologist of the Super-God Religion; the god I speak of is of course the Super-God; on this point there is really no need for further specification." Does such a reply stand?

## IV. From omnipotence to maximal power

The negative side of the "September Debate" mentioned in its rebuttal the problem of "God and the stone": can God create a stone that He Himself cannot lift? This problem aims to question the concept of "omnipotence" (being able to do anything). For the negative side's position, this problem was originally not the crux, since other examples could at any time express the same question. But because this problem triggered many tangled responses, I now give a concise analysis, clearing-up, and guidance.

### (A) The anti-omnipotence argument

Call the view that the concept "omnipotence" contains no logical contradiction, and that there exists an omnipotent God, "omnipotence theory." Conversely, call the view that the concept "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction, so that there cannot exist an omnipotent God, "anti-omnipotence theory." I suggest that anti-omnipotence theorists can put forward the following "anti-omnipotence argument" to establish their position:

Whatever x may be, if x can create a stone that x itself cannot lift, then x is not omnipotent, because such a stone would not be something x can lift; on the other hand, if x cannot create a stone that x itself cannot lift, then x is also not omnipotent, because it cannot create such a stone.

Accordingly, whether or not x can create a stone it cannot lift itself, x is not omnipotent. Since "can/cannot" exhausts all the relevant possibilities, "x is omnipotent" cannot hold in any possible circumstance — that is, "x is omnipotent" is logically impossible. And since logical impossibility is exactly logical contradiction (because "having no logical contradiction" is what is called "logically possible"), "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction.

Since "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction, there cannot exist an omnipotent God (omnipotence theorists themselves agree: no existent can have a logically contradictory attribute [Note 27]). In short, even if God exists, He cannot be omnipotent [Note 28].

### (B) The omnipotence-theorists' erroneous replies

Horner, the *Review*, *Surprised*, and *Religion* all hold omnipotence theory. Their replies to anti-omnipotence theory are all erroneous —

#### (1) Creating the world, therefore omnipotent?

*Religion* says: "A God who can create even the whole universe is naturally omnipotent" (p. 27). This idea, to people lacking training in thinking, may look very "natural," but it in fact commits the logical fallacy called "non-sequitur" （推論失效）. Even if there is a God who created this universe, it still cannot be inferred from this that God can also create another kind of universe. In terms of modal logic's "possible worlds" concept: even if God created the actual world, one cannot infer from this that God can also create other possible worlds. Accordingly, attempting to infer "omnipotent" from "can create the whole universe" commits the fallacy of non-sequitur.

Moreover, anti-omnipotence theorists can mount a stronger rebuttal: the preceding section has proved that "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction, from which it follows that no x can possibly be omnipotent; and since no x can possibly be omnipotent, even if there exists a God who can create even the whole universe, He cannot be omnipotent.

#### (2) Self-limitation does not diminish omnipotence?

*Surprised* affirms that God can create a stone He cannot lift, but holds that this does not show God has something He cannot do: "First God creates a stone; then God makes this stone unable to be lifted by Himself… To say it clearly: God uses His creative power (making the stone) to limit Himself (being unable to lift the stone)… Saying God can make a stone He cannot lift does not deny God's power."

That is a defence that evades the problem, because the problem does not lie in "*why*" He cannot lift such a stone, but in "*whether He can*" lift such a stone. Saying "God makes this stone unable to be lifted by Himself" and "God uses His creative power to limit Himself" only answers the question "why can He not lift it"; but what is relevant to the issue is the question "can He lift it or not." *Surprised* has already affirmed that it is a stone God cannot lift; it cannot then deny that God has something He cannot do. Regardless of why He cannot lift it — whatever the reason ("external cause" or "internal cause," being limited or limiting Himself) — being unable to lift it is being unable to lift it. Since He cannot lift it, that is having something He cannot do, not being able to do anything.

#### (3) Logically impossible?

*Surprised* affirms that God can create a stone He cannot lift, and holds that this "being unable to lift" does not diminish omnipotence. Conversely, Horner, the *Review*, and *Religion* affirm that God cannot create a stone He cannot lift, and at the same time hold that this "cannot" does not diminish omnipotence. The omnipotence-theorists' two mutually conflicting doctrines belong to "contrary opposition": they cannot both be true, but can both be false. After deep analysis, it can be revealed that both doctrines are wrong. The preceding subsection dissected the errors of *Surprised* (the first doctrine); below I analyse the errors of the *Review*, *Religion*, and the like (the second doctrine).

Can God create a stone He cannot lift? Horner's answer to this problem is: "God cannot do logically impossible things" [Note 29], meaning that the problem is like asking God to do a logically impossible thing — unreasonable troublemaking. Likewise, the *Review* holds that a stone God cannot lift is a "logically contradictory state of affairs, for example a 'round square,'" so that the "God and the stone" problem amounts to asking "Can God create a stone that is 'both liftable and unliftable at the same time'" [Note 30]. On the same track, *Religion* declares: "Logical impossibilities, God cannot create. For example: Can God create a pair of legs that run while standing?… Can God drink the water in an empty cup?" "A stone God cannot lift is a logical impossibility… it is a 'non-thing'" (pp. 26, 27).

The passage above gathers the main points of these omnipotence-theorists' defences together, so that they can be "annihilated in one gathering," finished with one move —

The omnipotence-theorists' defence errs in "indiscriminate classification": confusing two concepts of completely different natures. The two concepts of completely different natures are: (i) "creating a stone one cannot lift oneself," or "making something the maker himself cannot lift"; and (ii) "creating a stone both liftable and unliftable at the same time," "creating a round square," "drinking the water in an empty cup," and so on. Concepts of type (ii) are indeed logically contradictory, but concepts of type (i) are absolutely not so. "Creating a stone one cannot lift oneself" fundamentally does not equal the *Review*'s so-called "creating a stone both liftable and unliftable at the same time," nor the *Religion*'s so-called "drinking the water in an empty cup." Making a (for example) giant concrete stone that the maker himself cannot lift contains no logical contradiction; it is factually possible. Since it is factually possible, it is not factually impossible; since it is not factually impossible, still less is it logically impossible.

In a word, the error of the omnipotence-theorists' defence lies in indiscriminate classification: classifying the concept "creating a stone one cannot lift oneself," which contains no logical contradiction, under the class of concepts containing logical contradictions.

#### (4) Begging the question, circular argument

Why do these omnipotence-theorists commit the error of "indiscriminate classification"? Because, in debating the "God and the stone" problem, they committed another error: the fallacy of begging the question.

They first assert "God is omnipotent," and then infer that "a stone God cannot lift" equals "a stone the omnipotent one cannot lift." Because they take "a stone the omnipotent one cannot lift" to contain a logical contradiction, they suppose "a stone God cannot lift" also contains a logical contradiction. Finally, they substitute the word "God" for the word "oneself," and so suppose "creating a stone one cannot lift oneself" also contains a logical contradiction.

That whole chain of inference begins with the opening assertion: taking "God is omnipotent" as a premise. But that is a logical foul, because the debate about "God and the stone" is precisely an examination of whether the thesis "God is omnipotent" can stand; in that debate this thesis is awaiting proof, yet those omnipotence-theorists used the thesis as a premise, treating it as already proved — using a thesis awaiting proof as if it were a thesis already proved — and so committed the fallacy of begging the question.

In discussing whether God is omnipotent, omnipotence-theorists cannot use "God is omnipotent" as a premise from which to derive the conclusion "God is omnipotent"; anti-omnipotence theorists also cannot use "God is not omnipotent" as a premise from which to derive the conclusion "God is not omnipotent"; otherwise both are circular arguments (a type of begging the question). Now anti-omnipotence theorists can give, on the "God and the stone" problem, an argument like this —

If God can create a stone He cannot lift, He is not omnipotent; if He cannot create a stone He cannot lift, He also is not omnipotent; therefore, whether or not He can create a stone He cannot lift, God is not omnipotent [Note 31].

In this argument, "God is not omnipotent" is a conclusion supported by the premises, not a premise used to support the conclusion. Conversely, the omnipotence-theorists above used "God is omnipotent" as a premise, and from it inferred that "creating a stone (God) cannot lift oneself" contains a logical contradiction, thereby defending their conclusion "God is omnipotent." Using "God is omnipotent" as a premise in this way to defend the conclusion "God is omnipotent" commits the fallacy of circular argument.

For example, *Religion* declares: "Why say there will not be a stone God cannot lift? Because God is omnipotent. A stone the omnipotent God cannot lift… is a 'non-thing.' 'If God only cannot make "non-things," that is being able to do everything. So He is still omnipotent'" (p. 26). Its premise is "because God is omnipotent"; its conclusion is "so He is still omnipotent." Going around in a circle, the end point turns out to be just the start point: this is a circular argument that can prove nothing [Note 32].

### (C) Consistency proofs and the theory of maximal power

On the exploration of "omnipotence," the above are the main joints; below, a few marginal remarks.

#### (1) From the Barber of Seville to consistency proofs

Some concepts look superficially as if they contain no logical contradiction, but on analysis reveal that they do in fact harbour one. The most famous example is the concept that Russell's paradox points to: "the set of all sets that do not contain themselves as members." Call this set "Ω." This created serious problems in set theory and the foundations of mathematics; it is comparatively specialised, and it does not matter if you do not understand why — we can use a simpler example to display a concept harbouring a contradiction. A barber declares: "I only shave those who do not shave themselves" [Note 33]. This declaration looks at first glance not at all paradoxical, and the concept "only shaving those who do not shave themselves" does not look as if it would contain any logical contradiction. The problem lies in:

Does that barber shave himself or not? If he shaves himself, he is someone who shaves himself, and so, by the declaration, he does not shave himself. On the other hand, if he does not shave himself, he is someone who does not shave himself, and so, by the declaration, he shaves himself.

That barber was a man of Seville. The lesson of the story of *The Barber of Seville* is: relying solely on a naked brain without analytic training and logical equipment to "intuit" whether certain concepts harbour contradictions is often unreliable.

The concept "only shaving those who do not shave themselves," which seems to contain no logical contradiction, requires reasoning before one can see that it in fact harbours a logical contradiction. [I conjecture the concept "absolute perfection" is likewise, because: if there are regrets it does not count as absolutely perfect; if there are no regrets it also does not count as absolutely perfect, because "having no regrets" can itself be regarded as a kind of regret.]

Similarly, "omnipotence" is also a concept harbouring a logical contradiction [Note 34]; the preceding text has constructed an "anti-omnipotence argument" to prove that "omnipotence" harbours a logical contradiction. Conversely, omnipotence-theorists have never given any argument to prove that "omnipotence" contains no logical contradiction; in logical terminology, they have not constructed a **consistency proof** for "omnipotence" — that is, they have not constructed a consistency proof for "∃x (x is omnipotent)." (This is not hard to understand: the anti-omnipotence argument logically implies that omnipotence-theorists not only in fact cannot produce, but cannot possibly produce, a consistency proof — because a concept containing a logical contradiction can never receive a consistency proof.)

#### (2) From insulting God to the theory of maximal power

Once it is proved that "omnipotence" harbours a logical contradiction, one can assert that there exists no omnipotent God, just as there exists no round-square thing. From this it follows that if there is a God, God is not omnipotent either.

"Would that not be insulting God?" some people will worry. But this worry is superfluous, groundless anxiety: for if it has been proved that "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction, then to say God is not omnipotent is like saying God is not a round square — with not the slightest insult. On the contrary, the statement "God is omnipotent" would be the insult to God (if there is such a thing as insulting God), because it is like saying God is a round square, is the water in an empty cup, is a stone both liftable and unliftable at the same time.

The last point to consider is this: some people, though convinced by the argument — though they cannot but be convinced rationally — will still feel uneasy emotionally. With the problem discussed here, although it has been proved that "omnipotence" contains a logical contradiction, and explained why denying "God is omnipotent" is not insulting God, many people will still feel uneasy.

In this situation, I suggest replacing the concept "omnipotence" with the concept "maximal power" （至能）. One may try defining "maximal power" as: "among all existents, power supreme and unsurpassed."

I do not think this definition is yet clear enough. How to clarify it further, and how to use this concept to establish a "theory of maximal power" to replace "omnipotence theory," are outside the scope of this essay's discussion; let them be left to those interested in the topic to research.

Just so: await those who come after; await the capable.

## Conclusion: The logic of thinking vs the science of thinking

The analysis and refutation of the responses provoked by the "September Debate" end here. As stated above, the purpose of these analyses and refutations is to provide worked examples of critical disputation, hoping to help improve the ability to apply thinking methods: that is this essay's aim.

This essay will be collected into the Ming Pao Publications book *The Art of Thinking* (edited by Rong Ziyou and Liang Peilin) as a supplement to *From the Journey of the Bloody Road to the Journey to the Kingdom of Heaven*. The works of mine collected in that book (below, simply "my works") each explore or exhibit the concepts and principles of the methodology of thinking and their applications.

Concerning the links comprised in the methodology of thinking, I have always divided them thus [Note 35]:

A. Semantic analysis
B. Analysis of fallacies
C. Deductive logic
D. Scientific norms
E. [Creative strategy]

The last item, "creative strategy" (strategies used in creative thinking, such as de Bono's "lateral thinking"), is enclosed in square brackets indicating an appendage, to suggest: whether there are universally effective creative rules is highly dubious.

Roughly speaking, creating "by the rules," producing "by the book," produces products with at most mediocre, commonplace creativity.

Let us collectively call the methodology of thinking's main body, items A–D, the "**logic of thinking**" （思維邏輯）; the methodology's appendage, item E, can be assigned to the "**science of thinking**" （思維科學）. The logic of thinking focuses on the aptness and reasonableness of ways of thinking (how to think correctly, precisely, and with grounds? how to discern truth from fallacy?…). Completely different, the science of thinking focuses on the physico-chemical-biological conditions and socio-cultural factors of thought activity (what causal connections are there between brain structure and states of thought? what effects do education models have on creative ability?…). The logic of thinking aims to construct a set of conceptual tools and general principles that can be used to judge right and wrong, true and false, and to judge whether thinking is accurate and effective. The science of thinking, by contrast, investigates the psychological mechanisms, physiological bases, and environmental effects of thought processes. Logicians of thinking have little interest in the thinking of the intellectually disabled and the mentally ill; scientists of thinking have great interest in it.

Today's books and courses on "right-brain creative panaceas," "developing the brain's potential in three days," "crash courses in lateral thinking," and so on, often confuse the logic of thinking with the science of thinking (de Bono is like this [Note 36] — what he discusses in substance belongs at most to the science of thinking, but he supposes it to be the main body of the methodology of thinking (the logic of thinking): a case of putting Zhang's hat on Li's head — see the supplementary essay below).

That the methodology of thinking takes semantic analysis as its foundation and first link has been expounded in my works; I will not repeat it. As for the analysis of fallacies: if the emphasis is on practical effect in general application, it, like semantic analysis, is among the links of methodology most worth studying. My works' discussion items take semantic analysis as primary and the analysis of fallacies as auxiliary. Among them, *Thinking and the Mind-Demons* fixed and organised a set of conceptual tools of semantic analysis, as the sharp thinking instruments used in the "critique of linguistic harms" (the primary task of semantic analysis). On the other hand, the "four-nots framework" （四不架構） of fallacy analysis that I have presented in lectures and talks (a framework that can systematise the main fallacy types) has not yet been discussed in my works; it awaits another article. But although my works have not introduced the "four-nots framework," most of the most common fallacy types of everyday life have been given concise treatments.

In discussing semantic analysis and the analysis of fallacies, my works constantly keep "precision and essence" in mind. W. V. Quine held that the maxim "don't scratch where it doesn't itch" expresses a very important practical orientation. Conversely, people who "scratch where it doesn't itch" have the bad thinking habit of branching into side issues and proliferating complications. Suppose a person who scratches where it doesn't itch declares "dogs have horns," and someone points out the sentence is wrong; the "scratcher where it doesn't itch" will (for example) reply: "By 'that sentence,' do you mean a sentence, a statement, an assertion, or a proposition? Sentences… propositions and so on are different and must be distinguished in detail."

That is a branching-off line of thought. The distinctions between sentences, propositions, and so on are a problem in the philosophy of language; in the situation above there is fundamentally no need to heed them, nor any need to heed whether "dogs have horns" is a sentence or a proposition — one need only consider whether the sentence is right or wrong. The essential task of thinking is to solve problems, not to manufacture superfluous problems. For example, in discussing how to measure the volume of a pyramid, a scratcher-where-it-doesn't-itch offers the lofty opinion: "The volume problem belongs to the space problem; the space problem has not yet been completely solved in modern physics; so the problem is not so simple." That is the bad thinking habit of manufacturing superfluous problems (superfluous relative to measuring the pyramid). To treat this bad habit, one may adopt what I call the practical attitude of "treat illness where there is illness; do not operate where nothing is wrong" [Note 37].

It is with this attitude that my works weigh their inclusions and omissions, and set about building semantic analysis's "framework for the critique of linguistic harms" and fallacy analysis's "anti-sophistry system" [Note 38]. Some notions in them may not previously have been raised by anyone, but many more notions were raised by predecessors. My works only fuse, connect, implement, develop, and systematise those notions, and give them (hopefully) a more elegant expression. Flaubert said: "We borrow fire from others, light ourselves with it, and pass the fire on to others, until it is shared by all."

Flaubert was speaking of literature. As for methods of thinking, it should be even more so.

A single flower cannot open a spring.
