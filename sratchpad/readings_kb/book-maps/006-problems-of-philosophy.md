# Book map — 006: Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912)

*Book layer · one of the pilot's two source textbooks. ★ = chapter feeds one of the three pilot problem entries.*

**Extraction note.** The extracted folder (`data/philosophy_readings/markdown/006 - The Problems of Philosophy/`) holds 36 files, not 15: the Vook Classics edition was split so that each chapter consists of a *stub title file* (14–20 words — the title page) followed by a *content file* carrying the chapter's full text under the generic name "The Problems of Philosophy by Bertrand Russell Vook Classics." The Preface is counted as chapter "1" in the file titles, so extraction titles run one number ahead of the book's own chapter numbers (book ch. I appears as "2. Appearance And Reality"). The map below uses the book's numbering, with extraction file numbers in brackets. Remaining files: ch01 (cover), ch02–ch03 (front-matter fragments), ch35 (credits & copyright), ch36 (empty).

- **Preface** [file ch04]. Russell confines himself to problems where something positive and constructive can be said; accordingly the theory of knowledge takes more space than metaphysics. He acknowledges G. E. Moore (sense-data and physical objects) and J. M. Keynes (probability and induction).
- **I. Appearance and Reality** [stub ch05, text ch06] ★ → *External-World Skepticism*. The table: its colour, shape, and texture vary with viewpoint, light, and instrument, so no appearance is the table as it is in itself; introduces *sense-data* vs the *physical object* and poses the chapter's two questions — is there a real table at all, and if so, what is it like? Berkeley's idealism is introduced as the first serious answer.
- **II. The Existence of Matter** [stub ch07, text ch08] ★ → *External-World Skepticism*. Descartes' method of systematic doubt, the deceitful demon, and the cogito — with Russell's caveat that only particular experiences, not a permanent Self, are certain. Argues we cannot prove objects exist independently, but instinctive belief plus the simplicity of the realist hypothesis (the cat that grows hungry while unobserved) justify accepting the external world.
- **III. The Nature of Matter** [stub ch09, text ch10]. Granting that matter exists, what is it? Works through the physicist's picture (space, time, motion, atoms and their fields) and the gap between that inferred structure and anything we experience.
- **IV. Idealism** [stub ch11, text ch12]. Idealism defined as the doctrine that whatever exists, or can be known to exist, is in some sense mental; examines Berkeley's grounds for it and where Russell thinks they fail. Supports the skepticism entry's idealism section.
- **V. Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description** [stub ch13, text ch14] ★ → *Gettier and the Analysis of Knowledge*. Two kinds of knowledge of things: acquaintance (sense-data, memory, introspection, probably the Self, and universals) versus knowledge by description ("the physical object which causes such-and-such sense-data"); ends with the principle that every proposition we understand is composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted.
- **VI. On Induction** [stub ch15, text ch16] ★ → *The Problem of Induction*. The sunrise and the chicken; the uniformity of nature; the two-part inductive principle stated for single cases and for general laws; the verdict that experience can neither prove nor disprove the principle.
- **VII. On Our Knowledge of General Principles** [stub ch17, text ch18] ★ → *The Problem of Induction*. Self-evident logical principles (the laws of thought; "whatever follows from a true proposition is true"); the empiricist–rationalist controversy settled in favour of a priori knowledge that experience elicits but does not prove; the inductive principle placed among the probability-yielding principles of inference.
- **VIII. How A Priori Knowledge Is Possible** [stub ch19, text ch20]. Kant's critical philosophy and the synthetic a priori, with Russell's account of why Kant thought such knowledge possible and where the explanation needs supplementing. Feeds the future a-priori-knowledge problem entry.
- **IX. The World of Universals** [stub ch21, text ch22]. Plato's theory of ideas; relations shown to be universals with a kind of being distinct from physical objects, minds, and sense-data. Feeds the universals problem (metaphysics).
- **X. On Our Knowledge of Universals** [stub ch23, text ch24]. Universals, like particulars, divide into those known by acquaintance (qualities given in sense-data), those known by description, and those unknown; how a priori truths about universals are known.
- **XI. On Intuitive Knowledge** [stub ch25, text ch26]. Whether beliefs need proof or probability; intuitive knowledge as the un-inferred base, and self-evidence as its mark — the topic ch. XIII develops into two kinds of self-evidence. Feeds the analysis-of-knowledge cluster.
- **XII. Truth and Falsehood** [stub ch27, text ch28] ★ → *Gettier and the Analysis of Knowledge* (background: the "true" in justified true belief). Three requisites for a theory of truth; coherence rejected as a *definition* (though retained as a test); truth defined as correspondence, via the multiple-relation theory of judgement (Othello, Desdemona, loving, Cassio).
- **XIII. Knowledge, Error, and Probable Opinion** [stub ch29, text ch30] ★ → *Gettier and the Analysis of Knowledge*. True belief is not knowledge when deduced from a false belief (the Balfour/Campbell-Bannerman case) or by fallacy; derivative vs intuitive knowledge; degrees of self-evidence; knowledge shading into probable opinion, with coherence as a criterion that raises probability but never yields certainty.
- **XIV. The Limits of Philosophical Knowledge** [stub ch31, text ch32] ★ → *External-World Skepticism* (closing stance). Against Hegel's a priori metaphysics of the Whole and against complete scepticism alike: no argument can compel someone who stands wholly outside knowledge, but scepticism of that kind is unreasonable; philosophy is the criticism, not the destruction, of knowledge.
- **XV. The Value of Philosophy** [stub ch33, text ch34]. Philosophy's value is not in definite answers but in the questions: it enlarges our conception of what is possible, diminishes dogmatic confidence, and keeps alive speculative interest in the universe.

Full text is present and was read in full for chs. I, II, V, VI, VII, XII, XIII, XIV (the pilot chapters); the remaining chapters' descriptions rest on their openings and Russell's cross-references.
