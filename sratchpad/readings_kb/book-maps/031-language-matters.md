- **Preface** [pp. ix–xii]. SLA and FLL have been treated as largely unrelated fields; FL professionals filter SLA research through pedagogy and react mainly to Krashen's Monitor / Input Hypothesis, while SLA research has ignored non-Western contexts and indigenized varieties of English (Sridhar & Sridhar; Kachru). Poses the volume's three conference questions: do classroom learners follow different acquisition paths, are the internalization processes fundamentally different, and what is the theoretical relationship?
- **Introduction** [pp. 1–2] ★ → *Second or Foreign?* Summarizes Berns (ch. 1): the foreign/second dichotomy fades from the learner's point of view. The editors' own test case: Spanish in the United States is "foreign" in Alaska (like English in Japan), "second" in Los Angeles and Dade County (like English in India), and in between in Chicago, New York and Dallas (like English in West Germany) — yet learners in the same community can differ in purpose and in whether they seek outside input at all. Berns's chapter, couched in a sociolinguistic framework, is flagged as unique in the volume.
- **1. 'Second' and 'Foreign' in Second Language Acquisition / Foreign Language Learning: A Sociolinguistic Perspective** — Margie Berns [pp. 3–11] ★ → *Second or Foreign?*; *Language as a Resource*. Following Stern (1983), a language is "foreign" if learned with reference to a speech community outside the country, "second" if inside it. Sociolinguistic profiles of English in Japan (school subject and "uplifting" pastime; business necessity for the *kokusaijin*), West Germany (school subject, media presence, *lingua franca* across Europe) and India (medium of instruction, law, administration and national literature for 18 million English-using bilinguals) show Japan fits "foreign" and India fits "second," but West Germany fits neither. Berns proposes a **cline** (continuum) of language status with Japan near the foreign pole, India near the second pole and West Germany near the midpoint, plus a third term, **"international,"** for English used between non-native speakers. Closes from the learner's perspective: speech communities need not be native-speaking, and the competence a learner needs depends on the role (tour guide, manager, tourist, opera singer, grandchild) they aim to fill.
- **Part I introduction: Some Perspectives on SLA/FLL** [pp. 13–16]. Behind the SLA/FLL question "lurks a sociology of each discipline": SLA aims to describe and explain, FL research to influence the classroom. Perspectives are coloured by researchers' language backgrounds (the ESL researcher's syntax-heavy English vs the Spanish researcher's morphology-heavy Spanish — they may not be exploring "the same thing"). VanPatten and Kramsch both want partnership, from opposite directions; Gass and Andersen offer frameworks.
- **2. Theory and Research in SLA and FLL: On Producers and Consumers** — Bill VanPatten [pp. 17–26] ★ → *Second or Foreign?* The received relationship is unidirectional: SLA produces theory, FL teaching consumes it, with FLL subsumed as a subset of SLA. VanPatten attacks its three assumptions — that SLA has answers (on input alone, eleven open questions and counting), that SLA is cross-linguistic (of 25 papers in Gass & Madden 1985, only three study learners of languages other than English; the Larsen-Freeman frequency account of morpheme order fails on Spanish *ser/estar*, where communicative value outranks frequency), and that acquisition is not FL's research domain (a historical accident of FL's turn to education schools). Proposes the reverse flow: the FL classroom, where outside variables are already controlled, is the best testing ground for SLA hypotheses, and a theory of SLA should be the overlap of FLL, ESL and untutored acquisition — a product of research in all contexts, not a precursor to it.
- **3. What is Foreign Language Learning Research?** — Claire J. Kramsch [pp. 27–33] ★ → *Communicative Competence*; *Second or Foreign?* FLL is the immediate concern of neither SLA (learner as biological/psychological entity; quantitative, ESL-based) nor FL education (teacher- and procedure-centred). Defines FLL by its setting (schooling variables: error correction, turn-allocation, encouraged vs discouraged voice), its object (understanding classroom conditions, not controlling acquisition), its methods (interpretive and ethnographic as well as quantitative) and its **concept of language**: language must not be split into separate skills; it is "quintessentially indeterminate and culturally relative," and cross-cultural communication is "the negotiation of meanings intended and interpreted within an interactional context" (quoting Buber via Cook: meaning is "between us and things"). Learning is a social construct, mediated by Bruner's Language Acquisition Support System rather than Chomsky's LAD alone; Bourdieu: social acceptability "cannot be reduced to grammaticality alone." Ends with an eight-question research agenda, including how learners pass "from one-to-one equivalence to multifunctionality within the L2 system of semantic relations" (Q1) and what "political, economic, ideological and societal forces" govern teaching regardless of research (Q8).
- **4. Second and Foreign Language Learning: Same, Different or None of the Above?** — Susan M. Gass [pp. 34–44] ★ → *Second or Foreign?*; *Names, Descriptions and Reference* (anchor only). Internal variables: the fundamental learning processes do not plausibly depend on context (all learners generalize and misinterpret; Stenson's classroom-induced *any*-errors differ in instantiation, not process); Krashen's learning/acquisition distinction cuts across contexts. **Completeness**: native-like mastery may be unattainable by *any* L2 learner — Coppieters (1987) found even "fluent" non-natives' French intuitions diverge from natives'; Swain's immersion children lack *comprehensible output* opportunities. External variables: negative evidence (information that an utterance is deviant) is frequent in conversation and typically direct in classrooms, indirect outside them; input is controlled in both settings (foreigner talk; teacher talk), but some information — e.g. the sentence-interpretation strategies Italian requires (agreement, semantic and pragmatic cues vs English word order) — may be available only from ambient speech, not textbooks. Verdict: quantitative *and* qualitative differences exist; the research question is not same-or-different ("the answer is 'yes'") but the extent and nature of each.
- **5. Models, Processes, Principles and Strategies: SLA Inside and Outside the Classroom** — Roger W. Andersen [pp. 45–68] ★ → *Names, Descriptions and Reference* (anchor only). A cognitive-interactionist **Nativization Model**: learners create an autonomous interlanguage system (nativization) that new input must assimilate to, countered by restructuring toward the input (denativization). Cognitive operating principles include **One-to-One** (one intended meaning, one clear invariant surface form — the learner's first entry into a language), **Multifunctionality** (pressure toward forms doing many jobs, driven by Slobin's charges to be expressive, quick and easy), Formal Determinism, Distributional Bias, Relevance (aspect before tense before agreement), Transfer to Somewhere, and Relexification (fall back on L1 structure with L2 words when the target pattern cannot be perceived — the pidgin case). Communication strategies and repair ("learning to say 'Huh?'") complete the model. Andersen assumes the same processes operate across settings, and casts the classroom as the better laboratory for testing them.
- **Part II introduction: Frameworks and Approaches to Research Issues** [pp. 69–71]. The interlanguage papers (chs. 6–8) all reconcile L2 and FL data at the level of theory, differently: Eubank dissolves context into universal principles × input; Odlin makes metalinguistic awareness itself context-defining; VanPatten distinguishes deep (process) from superficial (output) differences. The language-use papers (chs. 9–11) answer Kramsch's call for classroom-specific research: discourse worlds (Wildner-Bassett — noting her English learners are the FL group and her German learners the L2 group), classroom social roles (Brooks), and topic choice in testing (Bacon).
- **6. Linguistic Theory and the Acquisition of German Negation** — Lynn Eubank [pp. 73–94]. Tests acquisition theories against Pinker's **learnability** and **extendibility** conditions (how a transitional rule system could be learned from the input, and how it could then be converted — or unlearned — without negative evidence). Clahsen's processing strategies fail both conditions on German negation data from L2 and FL learners; supplementing the theory with Government-Binding principles and parameters is proposed. Implies a unified theory of acquisition in which contextual output differences trace to universal principles interacting with different input, not to different processes. (Read: opening, framing and editors' summary in full; the negation data analysis sampled.)
- **7. Word-Order Transfer, Metalinguistic Awareness and Constraints on Foreign Language Learning** — Terence Odlin [pp. 95–117]. Against universalist claims (Rutherford; Zobl) that basic word order cannot transfer: attested counter-cases (Hawaiian Pidgin English, Bamboo English, Andean Spanish, Pidgin Fijian, Cairo Nubian, Mauritian Bhojpuri, Young People's Dyirbal) show SOV order transferred into SVO contact languages. The scarcity of such transfer is explained instead by observational problems, universal constraints, and above all **metalinguistic awareness**: transfer is likeliest where awareness is low and the language system *unfocused* (Le Page & Tabouret-Keller); FLL, as instruction-focused behaviour, is focused by definition, while SLA may be either. (Read in full.)
- **8. The Acquisition of Clitic Pronouns in Spanish: Two Case Studies** — Bill VanPatten [pp. 118–139]. Compares Anthony (14, naturalistic learner in Puerto Rico; Andersen's data) with David (US classroom learner) on Spanish clitic pronouns. Orders of difficulty are broadly shared (*me* early and easy; third-person clitics hard for both); formal instruction isolates forms (David's pre-verbal *se*) without reordering acquisition, and the study cannot speak to rate. Generates eight hypotheses on instruction's impact and the deep/superficial distinction the Conclusion takes up. (Read: introduction, framing and discussion in full; quantitative tables sampled.)
- **9. Coexisting Discourse Worlds: The Development of Pragmatic Competence Inside and Outside the Classroom** — Mary E. Wildner-Bassett [pp. 140–152] ★ → *Communicative Competence*. Learners face sociocultural conventions for managing conversation that may conflict with their L1 culture (contrastive / interlanguage pragmatics; Thomas's distinction between **sociopragmatic** and **pragmalinguistic** failure). Using Coulmas's **situational frame** (participants, setting, purpose, contextual restrictions, concomitant activity) and Edmondson's **coexisting discourse worlds** — a unit of talk gets its negotiated communicative value, including its intended speaker meaning or *illocutionary value*, within a world, and several worlds (role-play, real life, native-speaker–learner, teacher–student) operate at once, with one dominantly operant — she reanalyses role-enactment data: the hotel-booking correction that only makes sense as a classroom-world switch; the bicycle-fall correction that negotiates content, not accuracy, where the asymmetry is a knowledge gap, not a power gap; and the learner whose curse "this bloody England" is cancelled by his own smile — pragmatic failure by concomitant activity, and an instance of Harder's **"reduced personality"** of the foreign-language learner, who is not permitted the full repertoire and may not take his own utterance seriously. Classroom learners can risk more under self-parody's suspension of evaluation; learners in the target culture risk negative personal judgements from sociopragmatic failure. Prescription: teach routine formulas explicitly and add metapragmatic instruction.
- **10. Foreign Language Learning: A Social Interaction Perspective** — Frank B. Brooks [pp. 153–169] ★ → *Communicative Competence*. Ethnographic study of one university elementary Spanish lesson. Two students doing a paired "communicative" activity in fact reproduce the teacher's interactional patterns — repeat-the-error correction and *muy bien* topic-shift / evaluation signals (the initiation–response–feedback pattern) — turning the simulation into quiz preparation. They had learned these norms by imitation and inference, not instruction (Student A, interviewed later, had not known he did it until he saw the video). Framework (Green & Weade): classrooms are communicative environments with asymmetrical teacher–student relations; contexts are constructed in interaction; **meaning is context-specific**; "communicative competence is reflected in appropriate behaviour"; communication is rule-governed but its rules are implicit and built over time. Students are always learning two things at once: the language, and how to be competent members of the classroom.
- **11. On Topic Choice in Oral Proficiency Assessment** — Susan Cameron Bacon [pp. 170–180] ★ → *Communicative Competence* (assessment case). 187 Spanish students, 10-minute oral interviews, free choice among structured topics (guiding questions supplied), unstructured topics and their own. Quality of speech correlated positively with quantity, with choosing unstructured/original topics, and with developing them at length; the top-rated 8% developed unstructured topics far more than structured ones, the bottom 8% the reverse — the able treated the interview as communication (circumlocuting around missing words, e.g. the 24-hours-as-a-cat answer), the weak as a test to escape ("darn! Next card"). Against the ACTFL Guidelines' suggestion that proficiency is not context-bound: context — previous knowledge, experience, interest — is "of prime importance in effective communication." Hymes (1972), quoted at the chapter's head, defines the target as knowing "when to speak, when not, and … what to talk about with whom, when, where, [and] in what manner" — decisions the classroom normally predetermines.
- **Part III introduction: From Research and Theory to Practice** [pp. 181–183]. Part III's papers seek principles and philosophies, not Monday-morning techniques. Savignon: ignorance of research breeds simple solutions to complex problems. Nattinger and Kiraly each build a curriculum on one research finding (routines and patterns; communicative competence for translators, the translator as "co-creator" of the target text). Schinke-Llano's definitions are product-oriented and her bias explicit (SLA qualitatively superior). Campbell & Lindholm reverse the direction: a curriculum (bilingual immersion) as a challenge *to* SLA research, closing the circle back to Berns — a US population that, allowed full bilingual development, would itself be a foreign-language resource. "What, then, is a foreign language in the United States?"
- **12. In SLA/FLL, Nothing is More Practical than a Good Theory** — Sandra J. Savignon [pp. 185–197] ★ → *Communicative Competence*. Keynote defence of theory against the FL profession's impatience with it. Two targets: the **ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines** (1986), promoted as a universally valid, theory-free hierarchy of performance levels — Savignon: the claim to be "not based on a particular linguistic theory" is misleading, and Kramsch's verdict is quoted (behavioural functions over notional development; static content; accuracy over discourse aptitude); and **Higgs & Clifford (1982)**, whose claim that communicative teaching without early error correction produces irreversible fossilization — the "terminal 2" profile — rests, on the seminar audit Savignon reports, on anecdote and "experiential data" rather than rigorous evidence, yet underwrites accuracy-first policy. Positive programme: communicative competence in the broad sense — grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse and strategic competence (Lapkin & Swain, 1984) — as the framework in which immersion results make sense; Savignon's own 1971 Illinois study, in which adults given the chance to communicate in French from the start outperformed on communicative measures, as the model of research-led practice; and the rule that recommendations be resisted "in the absence of compelling evidence."
- **13. Prefabricated Speech for Language Learning** — James R. Nattinger [pp. 198–206] ★ → *Communicative Competence*; *Names, Descriptions and Reference* (anchor only). Teachers should take their grammar from theories of language *use* (performance), not competence: conventional grammars offer word-lists, correctness prescriptions, or the abstract system of an idealized speaker-hearer, and "the precise relationship between the theoretical competence of an idealized speaker-hearer and the actual language performance of a specific human being is a highly tenuous matter." Acquisition research (Peters; Hakuta; Wong-Fillmore) shows learners begin with **prefabricated** chunks — unvarying *routines* and *patterns* with a movable slot ("wanna + VP") — which are not peripheral to the creative system but its raw material: chunks are stored whole, then segmented ("the words almost audibly cracked," Brown 1973, on *korewa* splitting into *kore* + *wa*) into words and rules; item-learning precedes system-learning at every level. Proposes a curriculum of chunks grouped by function — Social Interactions, Necessary Topics, Discourse Devices — revisited cyclically with widening variation, introduced after automatic control but before fossilization (the optimum segmentation point is an open research question).
- **14. A Role for Communicative Competence and the Acquisition-Learning Distinction in Translator Training** — Don Kiraly [pp. 207–215] ★ → *Communicative Competence*. Traditional European translator training rests on Catford's (1965) **equivalence paradigm** — translation as "the replacement of textual material in one language by equivalent material in another language," a search-and-replace for formal equivalents — and produces the silent, product-obsessed classroom Röhl (1984) describes. Against it, a communicative model built on Firth: language behaviour as "a network of relations between people, things, and events," every utterance embedded in a **context of situation** (participants' personalities and persons, verbal and non-verbal action, relevant objects and events, effects of the verbal action). Mapped onto Krashen's distinction: the *learner* consciously identifies elements and equivalence relations inside linguistic subsystems, while the *acquirer* uses language for self-expression inside real contexts of situation. The translator is receiver of the source text and co-creator of the target text, so **translation competence is a special type of communicative competence** — trainees must acquire, not only learn. Closing question: should translators "translate texts or words, messages or linguistic signs"?
- **15. Can Foreign Language Learning Be Like Second Language Acquisition? The Curious Case of Immersion** — Linda Schinke-Llano [pp. 216–225] ★ → *Second or Foreign?* Redefines the pair by product: SLA *results in* native-like use, FLL does not. Compares FLEX (exposure only), FLES (some proficiency) and immersion (target language as medium of instruction) for majority-language elementary children. Immersion best matches the facilitative-SLA environment assembled from Krashen (acquisition over learning; comprehensible input at *i*+1; low affective filter), Long (negotiated interaction as the necessary-and-sufficient condition), Swain (comprehensible output) and the silent period — yet immersion pupils still do not reach native-like speaking and writing, and Genesee (1986) is quoted: no student restricted to the school environment is likely to attain "totally native-like competence," because school offers only a limited functional range. Verdict: facilitative environments *can* be approximated in classrooms, and classroom-interaction research is the tool for closing the remaining gap.
- **16. Conservation of Language Resources** — Russell N. Campbell & Kathryn J. Lindholm [pp. 226–239] ★ → *Language as a Resource*. The resource: the proficiency linguistic-minority children bring to school free of charge. A hypothetical Korean-American five-year-old in Los Angeles already commands Korean phonology, most morphology and syntax, discourse rules and sociolinguistic rules (lexicon, honorifics, voice and body language varying by interlocutor) — rated, with stated limits (no literacy, thinner semantic networks), at 3+/4 on the US Government scale, where 1,400 hours of Defense Language Institute instruction yields 2/2+. US practice is nonetheless **subtractive** — "an unspoken national policy" requiring children to surrender the ancestral language "as dues" for majority membership — which the authors call misguided for the children's English and education as well as for the national FL resource base. Three additive counter-models, with first-year evaluations: elementary two-way bilingual immersion in California (112 pupils; gains in *both* languages; content learned in Spanish available when tested in English); secondary bilingual partial immersion at Mount Miguel High School (SNS > ISSL > traditional SPANISH streams on listening, reading, writing); and a UCLA–Seoul National University intensive Korean programme for heritage students (proficiency, self-esteem and ethnolinguistic loyalties monitored; analysis incomplete at writing). Programmes are "in their infancy"; the heritage population itself is under-researched and its tests inadequate.
- **17. Contexts, Processes, and Products in SLA and FLL** — Bill VanPatten & James F. Lee [pp. 240–245] ★ → *Second or Foreign?* The editors' synthesis. On terminology there is "quite simply" **no consensus** in the volume. Their own resolution sorts the distinction by research domain: it **matters for context** (social situations shape the quality and quantity of input and output); it is **of varying importance for processes** — irrelevant for psycholinguistic processes (the acquisition faculties do not change with environment), important for psychosocial processes (pragmatic and communicative devices do change with discourse context); and it is **useful for products** (eventual proficiency, degree of L1 influence in the product). Hence overlapping circles, not containment: FLL is neither inside SLA nor vice versa. For teaching, research yields **principles that constrain** rather than methods that dictate (Principle 1: learners need communicatively oriented, comprehensible input — with corollaries on interaction, written input, ease and quality). Final admission: no definitive answer has been given; whether SLA and FLL are "the same or different, overlapping or exclusive … is a function of one's own purpose."
