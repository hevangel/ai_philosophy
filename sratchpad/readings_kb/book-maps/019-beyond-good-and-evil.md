# Book map — 019: Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil: Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future* (1886), translated with commentary by Walter Kaufmann (Vintage Books edition, 1989; translation first published 1966)

*Book layer · primary text. ★ = part feeds one of this subfield's problem entries.*

**Extraction note.** The extracted folder (`data/philosophy_readings/markdown/019 - Beyond Good and Evil - Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future/`) holds 33 files, all carrying the same generic title. The layout is regular and recoverable: front matter (ch01–ch08, including the Translator's Preface at ch05, ~3,300 words, and Bibliographical Note at ch07), Nietzsche's Preface (ch09), a contents file (ch10), then for each of the nine Parts a one-page part-title stub followed by the part's full text (ch11–ch28), then the Aftersong "From High Mountains" in German and in Kaufmann's translation (ch29–ch31), and the edition's back matter (ch32–ch33). Section (§) numbers below are Nietzsche's own, as printed; the aphoristic text is complete — all 296 sections are accounted for by the part ranges, which follow the part openings in the extraction.

- **Nietzsche's Preface** [file ch09]. "Supposing truth is a woman" — dogmatism as a noble childishness built on "any old popular superstition"; Plato's "invention of the pure spirit and the good as such" named "the worst, most durable, and most dangerous of all errors so far," with Christianity as "Platonism for 'the people'"; the fight against it has bred Europe's "magnificent tension of the spirit."
- **Part One: On the Prejudices of Philosophers** (§§1–23) [stub ch11, text ch12] ★ → *Master Morality, Slave Morality, and the Problem of Morality Itself* (framing). §1 asks not what truth is but what the *will to truth* is worth: "Suppose we want truth: why not rather untruth? and uncertainty? even ignorance?" §2 names the metaphysicians' "faith in opposite values"; §4 argues the falseness of a judgement is no objection to it — the question is whether it is life-promoting, and renouncing false judgements "would mean renouncing life"; §5 charges philosophers with defending, with reasons found afterwards, "a desire of the heart that has been filtered and made abstract."
- **Part Two: The Free Spirit** (§§24–44) [stub ch13, text ch14]. Opens (§24) on the "strange simplification and falsification" in which man lives: knowledge itself rose on "a far more powerful will: the will to ignorance, to the uncertain, to the untrue! Not as its opposite, but — as its refinement!" The free spirit as the one who can live with that.
- **Part Three: What Is Religious** (§§45–62) [stub ch15, text ch16]. §45 sets the psychologist's "great hunt" through "the whole history of the soul so far"; the part works through the religious neurosis, sacrifice, and the ascetic ideal's prehistory in the soul's economy.
- **Part Four: Epigrams and Interludes** (§§63–185) [stub ch17, text ch18]. Short aphorisms ("'I have done that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually — memory yields," §68; "'Knowledge for its own sake' — that is the last snare of morality," §64).
- **Part Five: Natural History of Morals** (§§186–203) [stub ch19, text ch20] ★ → *Master Morality, Slave Morality, and the Problem of Morality Itself*. §186: the "science of morals" has so far tried to supply a *rational foundation* for a morality it simply accepted as given, so "the problem of morality itself" was never seen — the real problems "emerge only when we compare *many* moralities"; what is needed first is a *typology* of morals, and moralities are "merely a sign language of the affects" (§187). §188: every morality is "a bit of tyranny against 'nature'" whose long compulsion is what has educated the spirit. The part continues through herd morality, the morality of timidity, and the English moralists.
- **Part Six: We Scholars** (§§204–213) [stub ch21, text ch22]. §204 opens against the "unharmful shift in the respective ranks of science and philosophy": the scholar's emancipation from philosophy as an effect of the democratic order; the scholar as instrument, the philosopher as commander and legislator.
- **Part Seven: Our Virtues** (§§214–239) [stub ch23, text ch24]. §214: "we Europeans of the day after tomorrow, we firstborn of the twentieth century" — if we still have virtues they will not be our grandfathers' "simpleminded and four-square" ones; the part tests honesty, pity, and the virtue of the free spirit against that standard.
- **Part Eight: Peoples and Fatherlands** (§§240–256) [stub ch25, text ch26]. §240 opens on Wagner's *Meistersinger* overture as an emblem of Germanness; the part ranges over the English, French, Jews, and Germans, against nationalism and for the "good European."
- **Part Nine: What Is Noble** (§§257–296) [stub ch27, text ch28] ★ → *Master Morality, Slave Morality, and the Problem of Morality Itself*. §257: every enhancement of the type "man" has been the work of an aristocratic society that "needs slavery in some sense or other," and of the *pathos of distance*. §259: mutual non-exploitation universalized as society's principle would be "a will to the *denial* of life," since life "simply *is* will to power." §260 (the entry's core): the two basic types — **master morality** (good/bad = noble/contemptible; value-creating self-glorification) and **slave morality** (good/evil; pity, patience, humility honoured as the useful virtues of the suffering) — with the caveats that mixed cultures mediate and interpenetrate both, "even in the same human being, within a *single* soul." §262 closes the historical arc: when the old discipline relaxes, "the 'individual' appears, obliged to give himself laws."
- **From High Mountains: Aftersong** [files ch29–ch31]. The closing poem, given with Kaufmann's translator's note, the German text, and his English version.

## Coverage
Full text is present for all nine Parts and both Prefaces. Read in full for this slice: Nietzsche's Preface, Part One §§1–5, Part Five §186 and the §§187–188 openings, and Part Nine §§257–262. The remaining parts' descriptions rest on their opening sections. The only defects are presentational: identical generic file titles throughout, and part-title stubs interleaved with the text files. Kaufmann's commentary takes the form of footnotes inside the text (e.g. on §260's "master morality" and the Förster/anti-Semitism material in the Translator's Preface) — a help, but the entry in this KB reconstructs Nietzsche's text, not the commentary.
