- **Preface** [chunk ch02]. The book examines the presuppositions of moral theories rather than moral issues themselves. Two families of presupposition: *metaethics* (moral concepts, language, thought — are moral judgments true/false assertions or expressions of attitude? is value objective or dependent on us?) and *moral psychology* (moral agents, motivation, reason/desire/pleasure). Every theory presupposes stands on both, usually without stating them.
- **1. Objectivity and Subjectivity** (pp. 1–41) [chunk ch02] ★ → *Moral Objectivity and Realism*; *Moral Relativism and Subjectivism*; *Emotivism and Expressivism* (background). Seven interpretations of objectivity: Plato (the Form of the Good — value as impersonal, absolute, intelligible reality); Moore (good a real, irreducible, non-natural property known by intuition, though not a standalone reality); Kant (objectivity as universal rational necessity — the categorical imperative — not a mind-independent realm); Rawls's Kantian constructivism (moral facts constructed by a procedure of reasoning); Nagel's normative realism (objective *reasons*, no occult value-entities needed); Mill (naturalistic objectivity — value grounded in the empirical fact of what is desired for its own sake, i.e. pleasure); Aristotle (objective goods fixed by human nature and its proper function; the practically wise agent). Then monism vs pluralism about value (Kant/Mill monists; Moore and Nagel pluralists). Then the turn to subjectivism: Mackie's queerness argument (objective values would be metaphysically and epistemologically "queer"; the unexplained "because" linking natural features to wrongness; the inertness/motivation objection, with Hume — reason alone "has no hold of the affections"); Mackie's **error theory** (ordinary judgments claim objectivity and are all false). Subjectivity and sentiment: Hume and Adam Smith ground morality in a *common* human sentiment (the general point of view; the impartial spectator) — subjectivist but not "anything goes," and not relativist. Ayer's expressivism stated (moral sentences "do not say anything"; pure expressions of feeling). Blackburn's quasi-realist expressivism (truth *within* the moral point of view). **Relativism**: correctness relative to a culture's norms; no neutral standpoint; the argument from persistent diversity (Mackie: variations are better explained by ways of life than by distorted perceptions of objective values); relativism does *not* entail tolerance. Objectivist replies: disagreement doesn't disprove facts; explanations of diversity are compatible with objective values; the group-specification problem (which group? why not the individual? were the abolitionists wrong?); objectivity is compatible with context-sensitive application.
- **2. Moral Theory and Moral Psychology** (pp. 42–73) [chunks ch02 + ch03] ★ → *Moral Motivation: Can Reason Alone Move Us?*; *Self-Interest and Egoism*; *Moral Luck*; *Are Moral Considerations Overriding?* **Moral Motivation**: the stranded-motorist case (ten possible motives); Kant — reason alone can motivate, and action has moral worth only when done because it is right; Hume — "reason of itself is utterly impotent," morals cannot be derived from reason since morals move us and reason doesn't; Korsgaard's Kantian reply (practical identity; reflective endorsement as the source of normativity); Mackie's middle position (universalizability gives no intrinsic authority; opting out of morality is not itself irrational). **Virtue and Motivation**: Aristotle's third way — in the virtuous agent reason and desire are *aligned*; the virtuous enjoy acting well; neither Kantian nor Humean, and not a mixture of them. **Self-interest and Morality**: psychological egoism is neither a definitional truth nor supported by the "every motive is someone's motive" inference; Hobbes's strategy (morality recommended by rational self-interest; the social contract); Hume/Smith/Mill replies (benevolence and the social feelings are real, natural motives; "it pleases me to help" does not make helping self-interested); Plato/Aristotle (justice profits the just by ordering the soul — not narrow egoism); the egoist plays the skeptic's role — that egoist reinterpretations are *possible* does not show they are *true*, any more than global skepticism must be refuted before knowledge claims stand. **What about Luck?** (see the moral-luck entry): Kant's attempt to make moral worth luck-immune (worth lies in rational volition alone; Williams: Kantianism's allure is "an ultimate form of justice… solace to a sense of the world's unfairness"); Mill's indifference (motive bears on the agent's worth, intention on the act's morality — yet intentions and outcomes are both luck-exposed; Nagel: what one *did* often depends on outcome); objective vs subjective rightness as the consequentialist's way of registering luck; luck in character-formation against the virtue approach (Aristotle: upbringing is "all-important," yet virtuous activity, not fortune, controls happiness — though happiness needs external goods). **Are Moral Considerations Overriding?**: impartialist readings of Kantianism and utilitarianism (Williams: the utilitarian agent as "representative of the satisfaction system"); whether equal moral status requires blanket impartiality in attention and care; Hume and Smith on why private connexions properly prevail; Susan Wolf's "moral saints" — a life in which non-moral excellences survive only as happy accidents.
- **3. Forms of Moral Theory** (pp. 74–109) [chunk ch03] ★ → *Forms of Moral Theory: Where Is Moral Value Located?* The organising question: what is the *locus* of moral value — consequences, the act itself, character, agreement? **Consequentialism**: rightness fixed by the state of affairs brought about; Mill's principle of utility as the single criterion; secondary principles; no act-type intrinsically right (even deception can be right); the assisted-suicide case used to separate consequentialist from intrinsic-wrongness reasoning; monist (Mill) and pluralist (Moore) variants. **Kantian non-consequentialism**: moral worth "does not lie in the effect"; persons as ends in themselves, of unconditional worth; the categorical imperative as the one criterion; conditional vs unconditional value. **Intuitionist non-consequentialism**: Ross's plurality of self-evident *prima facie* duties (justice, beneficence, gratitude…), known by reason without a special faculty; actual duty all-things-considered is *not* self-evident; Rawls's "unconnected heap of duties" objection and the intuitionist's fidelity-to-complexity reply; Reid on first principles. **The virtue-centred approach**: Aristotle seeks no criterion of right action; the practically wise person as "a sort of standard and measure"; virtue basic, principle derivative (the reverse of Kant and Mill); Hursthouse on the "what ought I to do?" objection; Williams's worry that the view depended on a collapsed teleological picture of nature — virtue with or without "essentialist perfectionism." **Contractarianism**: moral facts (if any) are fixed by rational agreement — Hobbes and Locke (state of nature), Rawls (original position, veil of ignorance), Gauthier (bargaining without the veil), Scanlon (principles no one could reasonably reject); everything turns on how the contracting parties are described. Closing section: all five forms share the idiom of duty while explaining it differently; a theory need not be purely of one type (Mill's struggle to domesticate virtue and justice to utility).
- **4. Naturalism and Non-naturalism** (pp. 110–152) [chunks ch03 + ch04] ★ → *Naturalism, Non-naturalism, and the Open Question*; *Emotivism and Expressivism*; *God and Morality*. Naturalism characterised (nothing beyond nature; anti-rationalist epistemology); seven possible relations between moral and natural properties (definition, constitution, dependence, identity-without-definability, full independence, expressive understanding, supernatural grounding). **Moore**: the naturalistic fallacy and the **open question argument** — for any natural candidate D, "Is what is D good?" remains an open question, so good is simple and indefinable. **Reconstructed naturalism**: supervenience by constitution (wetness/H₂O) and the stronger identity thesis (water = H₂O) — necessary connections discoverable by theory, not by meaning, so the open question loses its bite; why Moore himself is not such a naturalist. **Non-cognitivist alternatives**: Ayer's emotivism ("mere pseudo-concepts"; ethical symbols add nothing to factual content) and Hare's prescriptivism (moral judgments as *universalizable* prescriptions); expressivism's consistency constraint (no two exactly alike cases judged differently — Hare's gallery pictures); *subjective* supervenience (uncomfortable loudness) vs objective supervenience. **Hume and naturalism**: "skeptical naturalism" — only natural materials (sentiment, propensity), no value-detection; Blackburn: the expressivist "affirms *all that could ever properly be meant* by saying that there are real obligations." **Reconnecting Facts and Values**: Foot's attack on the fact/value split (evaluative meaning cannot be detached from its object — can one understand *fairness* without understanding it as good?); McDowell's secondary-quality analogy (redness as a disposition to look red, yet genuinely possessed), the "no-priority" view, and judgments being *merited* by their objects. **Aristotle and naturalism**: normative nature and flourishing; the modern Aristotelian retrieval (Foot, McDowell) keeps character and the reason–desire alignment while dropping the teleological metaphysics — contested. **Moral Facts and Explanation**: Harman's challenge (moral facts are explanatorily idle — psychology + non-moral facts explain every moral judgment) vs Sturgeon (Hitler's depravity explains his actions) and Railton (ought-facts as a special kind of is-fact; trial by ordeal). **What about God?** (see the God-and-morality entry): divine command as the guarantor of unconditional obligation; the *Euthyphro* dilemma stated; the theist's three-part reply (commands largely rationally intelligible; a perfect being could only command what is right; some commands' rationale may be beyond us); Aquinas's natural law (morality knowable without revelation — so the atheist can have moral knowledge, differently interpreted); grace, cosmic justice and redemption as what naturalism allegedly cannot supply; the motivation question (is the non-believer *disabled* for moral motivation, or is that a psychological fact about some agents?); faith-based morality is not thereby relativism.
- **Conclusion** (pp. 153–155) [chunk ch04]. Metaethics and moral psychology raise the "resolution" of concrete problems; analogies with free will, perception, and laws of nature; positions are not finished products to be selected but conclusions to be reached by reasoning.
- **Glossary** (pp. 156–161), **Bibliography** (pp. 162–165), **Index** (pp. 166–175) [all chunk ch04]. The glossary is substantive (antirealism, cognitivism, constructivism, egoism in its psychological and ethical forms, emotivism, etc.) and was used to check terminology in this slice's entries.
