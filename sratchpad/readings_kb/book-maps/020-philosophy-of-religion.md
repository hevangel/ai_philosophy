# Book map — 020: M. David Eckel, C. Allen Speight & Troy DuJardin (eds.), *The Future of the Philosophy of Religion* (2021)

*Book layer · Boston Studies in Philosophy, Religion and Public Life 8 (Springer). ★ = chapter feeds a problem entry in `entries/philosophy-of-religion/`.*

**Identification note.** The corpus folder is labelled only "020 - Philosophy of Religion," and this volume is sometimes misdescribed as a classical anthology of the field. It is not one: it is a 2021 symposium volume — essays by a reconvened group of philosophers of religion (first gathered at Boston University's Institute for Philosophy and Religion in the early 2000s, reconvened spring 2017) on *the future of their field*. It contains no classical arguments-for-God's-existence survey and no problem-of-evil chapter; its primary-paper chapters are contemporary essays, plus two historical studies (Bhāviveka; William James) and a closing metaphysical essay (Marion). Entries in this subfield are therefore built bottom-up from what these chapters actually argue.

**Extraction note.** The extracted folder (`data/philosophy_readings/markdown/020 - Philosophy of Religion/`) holds 18 files whose titles match the book's contents exactly (front matter + Introduction + 16 chapters). Every chapter is present in full (c. 5,500–14,800 words). No stubs, no corruption. The map below uses the book's chapter numbers, with extraction files in brackets.

- **Front matter** [file ch01]. Series information (Boston Studies in Philosophy, Religion and Public Life, series editor C. Allen Speight), title page, and the volume's contents — the source for the author attributions used throughout this map.
- **1. Introduction** — M. David Eckel [file ch02] ★ → *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*. The volume's frame: the classic questions of the field "seemed parochial," new theoretical orientations bypassed them for the lives of actual practitioners, and the diversification of Religious Studies no longer permits easy assumptions about philosophy's relevance to it. Summarizes each essay under Wildman's four challenges — a global perspective, critical theory, multiple disciplinary perspectives, and religious practices and real-world problems — and notes candidly what is missing (gender studies; fuller non-Western coverage).
- **2. Reading Tea Leaves, Drinking Tea: Four Transformations in Philosophy of Religion** — Wesley Wildman [file ch03] ★ → *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*; *Religious Diversity and Salvation* (background). Four transformations are reshaping North Atlantic universities — the *global*, the *critical*, the *multidisciplinary*, and the *practical* — while philosophy of religion, typically narrowed to worldview beliefs in one (usually Christian, usually theistic) tradition, has been slow to respond; academic positions in the field are contracting rapidly. Drawing on field-wide empirical studies, Wildman uses the transformations as criteria for estimating the field's future and what reversing the contraction would require.
- **3. What Religious Studies Can Teach the Humanities: A Philosophical Perspective** — Dan Arnold [file ch04]. Against the idea that scholars of religion should only describe and never judge: following Tyler Roberts, humanistic inquiry ineliminably involves first-personal questions, and intentionality is irreducibly *normative*; in Kantian terms, freedom just is our attunement to normative considerations, which cannot coherently be explained away. Philosophy of religion's role is to identify and clarify these normative considerations; illustrated through controversies over religion in American constitutional law, with Franklin Gamwell's claim that religion is an essential dimension of human being considered as the stronger, specifically religious conclusion.
- **4. Religious Practices and the Formation of Subjects** — Kevin Schilbrack [file ch05] ★ → *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*. The study of religion has shifted from texts to social practices; philosophy of religion should expand its object of study to follow. Three anthropological approaches — Geertz's Ricoeur-inspired hermeneutics, Asad's Foucault-inspired account of discipline, Csordas's Merleau-Ponty-inspired embodiment — are usually taken to conflict over whether practices *form* the subjectivities of participants; Schilbrack argues the disagreement is a philosophical one, that the approaches properly understood complement each other, and that an integrated account is available for philosophers of religion to contribute to.
- **5. Philosophy of Lived Religion: The Next Revolution?** — Timothy D. Knepper [file ch06] ★ → *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*; *Religious Diversity and Salvation* (background). Diversifying *which religions* the field studies addresses injustices of creed but not of class. Philosophy of *lived* religion is proposed as the next step: the philosophical study of *all* acts of religious reason-giving — privileging contemporary, ordinary, and spoken acts over past, elite, and written ones — organized around the basic questions humans ask (what, who, when, where, how, why). If philosophy of religion is to be the philosophy of *all* religion, not of theism or literate elites, this is what it must attend to.
- **6. Liturgical Philosophy of Religion: An Untimely Manifesto about Sincerity, Acceptance, and Hope** — Andrew Chignell [file ch07] ★ → *Faith, Acceptance, and Hope*; *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*. A manifesto for a "liturgical turn": set aside the field's fixation on the justification of theistic *belief* (a regulative, largely academic ideal — many practitioners do not fully believe many of their tradition's doctrines, and utter them in liturgy in a mode of "insincerity upwards," mapped on a Ladder of Liturgical Sincerity from inquirer through discerner, conditional seeker, and initiate to the utterly convinced). The attitudes that actually structure religious practice are *acceptance* (a voluntary, practically motivated taking-to-be-true, distinct from belief's involuntary felt conviction), *hope*, and *trust* — Kant's "minimum of theology" is hope that God exists plus acceptance that God is really possible. Philosophers of religion should become "pistologists" and "elpistologists" — students of *pistis* (faith) and *elpis* (hope) — and attend to the thick, "vested" doctrinal objects as they are embodied in liturgy rather than to bare theism.
- **7. Belief that Matters: Religion, Anti-Black Racism and the Future of the Philosophy of Religion** — Elizabeth Pritchard [file ch08] ★ → *The Future of Philosophy of Religion*. Scholars who repudiate the category of "belief" as exclusively cognitive presume belief is *nonmaterial* — a purely mental affirmation. Pritchard argues that presumption helps disaggregate "religion" from "race," obscures Christianity's relationship to racism, and perpetuates American anti-Black racism. She makes the case for the *materiality* of belief — belief as embodied, practised, and consequential — drawing on work portraying American anti-Black racism as itself a religion and on materialist theories of belief from several disciplines.
- **8. Critical Theory, Conspiracy, and "Gullible Critique"** — Andrew Dole [file ch09]. Develops Bruno Latour's 2004 worry about the resemblance between academic "critique" (critical theory, genealogy, the hermeneutics of suspicion) and conspiracy theories: both are forms of *suspicious explanation* sharing formal features — hidden agents, debunking surface appearances, self-sealing responses to counterevidence. A critique of critique, ending with an account of what Latour meant by "gullible critique."
- **9. Learning to Do Philosophy of Religion in the Anthropocene** — Mark Larrimore [file ch10]. Philosophy of religion and Anthropocene discourse have barely noticed each other. Engaging Roy Scranton, Amitav Ghosh, Bronisław Szerszynski, and Deborah Bird Rose: in a world of "vertiginous loss of long-taken-for-granted futures," the field's grasp of histories and traditions of interpretation could free the humanities from "paleological" thinking — but only by relinquishing the secular "immanent frame," learning to discern the spectres of a post-secular present, and conversing with speculative fiction and the environmental humanities' practices of mourning and kin-making with the more-than-human world.
- **10. Skills Without Values, Rallies Without Virtues: Beyond Hipster Heideggerianism and Shining Things** — David Decosimo [file ch11]. A critique of Hubert Dreyfus and Sean Dorrance Kelly's *All Things Shining*, an influential religious naturalism proposing a non-supernatural polytheism — "luring the gods back" by cultivating skills and communal ecstasy — as an antidote to nihilism. Decosimo argues the vision fails in each dimension: its account of attunement to value (the athlete "playing out of his mind"), its confusion of mere skill (*poiesis*) with virtue, and its handling of the ethical dangers of "whooshing up" (*physis*).
- **11. A Peripheral Field. Meditations on the Status of Jewish Philosophy** — Michael Zank [file ch12]. Through the law of gleanings (Lev 23:22) — leave the edges of the field for those in need — Jewish philosophy is located at the margins of Jewish existence and of mainstream philosophy of religion alike: marginal, but "as it ought to be," marking boundaries and transitions between particular and universal, and directing an otherwise introverted community's attention outward. Traditionally skeptical, in its modern version constructive and apologetic.
- **12. Nearly Neighbors: Reflections on Philosophy of Religion and Its Close Encounters with Modern Jewish Thought** — Sarah Hammerschlag [file ch13]. Taking up Schilbrack's charge that philosophy of religion is "the least reflexive discipline in religious studies": through three modern encounters between Judaism and philosophy (Mendelssohn and Levinas among them), Hammerschlag proposes the field be understood less as a discipline adjudicating the validity of concepts and more as one investigating their historical emergence and subsequent usage and dissemination.
- **13. Who or What Created the World? Bhāviveka's Arguments Against the Hindu Concept of God** — M. David Eckel [file ch14] ★ → *The Cosmological Argument*; *The Evidential Argument from Evil* (connection). In his commentary on Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikāḥ* 1.1, Bhāviveka reads "no-cause" (*ahetu*) as an *implicative* negation — a "bad cause" (*kuhetu*), like a "no-wife" who exists but does not do what a wife does — and counts the Hindu Lord (*īśvara*) among such no-causes: he may exist, but he does not *create*. Conventionally: the Lord cannot cause the world's arising "because he causes pleasure for some and pain for others, like a cowherd" — Draupadī's complaint in the *Mahābhārata* that the virtuous Yudhiṣṭhira's ruin shows the Lord capricious and cruel, which Eckel explicitly reads as the problem of theodicy; and the world, being an object of cognition (*prameya*) like the Lord himself, has no single creator. Ultimately, anything arising from the Lord would have to be himself, different, or both — all already refuted. Conventionally, the world's real "creator" is *karma*, a self-contained cosmos of ethical retribution; the chapter develops the arguments further from Bhāviveka's *Verses on the Heart of the Middle Way* and its commentary *The Flame of Reason*.
- **14. On the Past and Future of Religion, Art and Philosophy** — C. Allen Speight [file ch15]. Critics argue "religion" is an essentially modern concept with no ancient counterpart; the same has been argued for "art." Speight compares the two cases: if both concepts have such brief histories, what should change in how the humanities approach the much longer pre-histories of religious and artistic phenomena, and what is the future role of philosophy vis-à-vis both fields under this new historical self-awareness?
- **15. Psychoanalysis and the Monotheistic Origins of Modern Science** — Kenneth Reinhard [file ch16]. For Freud, psychoanalysis continued the Enlightenment: on the side of science, allied with philosophy, against religion. Reinhard traces Freud's formation in the Berlin physicalists' synthesis (Helmholtz's and Brücke's indestructible, displaceable energy → libido), his expectation that psychoanalysis would eventually rest on a quantitative foundation, and — via Lacan's reading of Freud — speculates on the monotheistic origins of modern science and the future of religion itself.
- **16. William James Revisited: "Pragmatic" Approaches to Religion** — David C. Lamberth [file ch17] ★ → *Faith, Acceptance, and Hope*. How James conceived philosophy of religion, in three movements. (i) The pre-*Varieties* essays: thought exists for the sake of action (the reflex arc), and the concept of God is a concrete practical idea orienting us toward our "ideal interests" — in "Reflex Action and Theism" (1881), "anything short of God is not rational; anything more than God is not possible"; *The Will to Believe* (1896) belongs to this period. (ii) The psychology of religion in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*: transformative experience, individual and social. (iii) The mature pragmatic/radical-empiricist period: *meliorism* — salvation treated "neither as necessary, nor as impossible," but as a possibility that becomes more probable as its conditions are actualized — with transformative religious experience re-read in *A Pluralistic Universe* as data for a collaborative, pluralistic interpretation of reality. Lamberth's conclusion: the twenty-first century is not likely to see humankind evolving "beyond" religion in any meaningful sense.
- **17. God and the Ambivalence of Being** — Jean-Luc Marion [file ch18] ★ → *The Ontological Argument* (framing). Before deciding whether God is to be thought *with* or *without* Being, ask what "Being" means. As the metaphysical tradition (*metaphysica*) understands it, Being is perseverance in the present, beings existing according to *causa sui* — a sense that annuls itself in the aporia of the present and forgets manifestation, past, and future. But Being can also be understood by the logic of the *gift* and the *event*, in which beings are precisely by surrendering their claim to perseverance — the mode Marion sees perfectly implemented in Christ as son in relation to the Father (and, in his earlier *God without Being*, God named as *agapē*, radically outside Being). The debate about God and Being is therefore "ambivalent" until this prior question — what do we understand by Being, when it is a question of God? — is settled.
