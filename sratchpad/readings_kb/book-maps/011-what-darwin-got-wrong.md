# Book map — 011: Jerry Fodor & Massimo Piattelli-Palmarini, *What Darwin Got Wrong* (2010)

*Book layer · source textbook for the philosophy-of-science-and-history slice. ★ = chapter feeds one of this slice's problem entries.*

**Extraction note.** The extracted folder (`data/philosophy_readings/markdown/011 - What Darwin Got Wrong/`) holds 24 files and the text is essentially complete — but the file titles are misleading: most content files are named after a *section heading inside* the chapter they contain ("Conclusion to this chapter", "Rhetorical conclusion", "Environment versus ecological niche") rather than after the chapter itself. The book's own chapter titles and numbering are recoverable in full from the table of contents [files ch04–ch05, two TOC files of 482 words each, not byte-identical]. The map below uses the book's numbering, with extraction file numbers in brackets. Two files are part-divider stubs only (ch09, ch14; 21 words each). The back matter is real: Appendix [ch20], Notes [ch21, ~13,500 words], References [ch22], Index [ch23]. Nothing in the main text is a citation stub, partial, or corrupted. One caution that governs every use of this book in the KB: it is a *polemic* — a two-author critique of adaptationism — not a survey text, and its empirical claims are its authors' selection and construal of the biology literature. Entries built on it present its arguments as its authors' arguments, at their stated strength, together with the caveats the book itself enters.

- **Epigraph** [file ch03]. Two quotations that set the book's tone: Chomsky (1972) — attributing a development to "natural selection" is safe only so long as we realise "there is no substance to this assertion" — and Darwin's own concession that facts can be adduced for conclusions opposite to his.
- **Acknowledgements** [file ch06].
- **Terms of Engagement** [preface; file ch07] ★ → both entries. The book's self-description and its ground rules. Not about God, intelligent design, or creationism: both authors are "outright, card-carrying… atheists" seeking thoroughly naturalistic — mechanical, non-teleological — explanations of evolution. The main contention: "there is something wrong — quite possibly fatally wrong — with the theory of natural selection." The central confusion they allege, stated here in advance: adaptationism infers (2) creatures are *selected for* their adaptive traits from (1) creatures *with* adaptive traits are selected — an invalid inference, "what philosophers call an 'intensional fallacy'". Also announced: the Darwin/Skinner analogy (the objections that killed Skinner's learning theory apply, *mutatis mutandis*, to Darwin's), and the disciplinary complaint that neo-Darwinism is taken as axiomatic in philosophy of mind, semantics, psychology and other export markets of the theory (see Appendix). The authors concede up front that the book is "mostly a work of criticism" and that they do not know how evolution works.
- **1. What Kind of Theory Is the Theory of Natural Selection?** [file ch08; its closing pages are the chapter's own summary] ★ → *Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism*; *Historical Explanation and Narrative*. Distinguishes the genealogy of species (GS — common descent; accepted without quarrel) from the theory of natural selection (NS — the purported *causal mechanism* of speciation; the book's target). Both NS and Skinner's operant conditioning theory (OT) are presented as black boxes mapping trait distributions in a population onto successor distributions, and shown to share six "proprietary constraints": iterativity, environmentalism, gradualism, monotonicity, locality, and mindlessness. Inside the boxes, both are "generate and test" theories — a random generator of traits plus an exogenous filter — "and that is all". The chapter's verdict, to be argued in Part Two: Skinner's theory of conditioning is *false* (associationism is not true), but Darwin's theory of selection is *empty*, because ET offers no remotely plausible account of how filtering by natural selection could work.
- **PART ONE — THE BIOLOGICAL ARGUMENT** [divider, file ch09]. The empirical half of the book: recent biology as the authors read it, marshalled against the random-generator/exogenous-filter picture.
- **2. Internal Constraints: What the New Biology Tells Us** [file ch10] ★ → *Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism*. Opens with a concession and a slogan: shifting equilibria in trait distributions are real and ubiquitous, but they are phenomena to be explained, not explanations; natural selection is "tuning the piano, not… composing the melodies". Against the "unidimensional" neo-Darwinian picture (ecology explains phenotype; internal structure abstracted away — Mayr's "many roads lead to Rome" quoted against it) and against "beanbag genetics" (traits as independently selectable units; Mayr himself, and Dover, quoted against it). The evo-devo revolution: conserved master genes (Pax6 and kin) across distant phyla, development as an internal *filter* on the phenotypes selection ever sees — "evolution is the evolution of ontogenies". Further internal filters: mutation hotspots and DNA repair, RNA editing, micro-RNAs, chaperones (HSP90 as an "evolutionary capacitor" that banks hidden variation), alternative splicing (one gene, many proteins), molecular drive and biased gene conversion (fixation of variants — even deleterious ones — independently of selection). Conclusion: even if mutations were random at source, the phenotypes are not; "there is… no single 'arrow'" from random variation to selectable phenotype.
- **3. Whole Genomes, Networks, Modules and Other Complexities** [file ch11] ★ (supporting) → *Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism*. Gene regulatory networks (Davidson: microevolutionary processes cannot explain body-plan differences; network change has "inhomogeneous and discontinuous effects"); entrenchment; robustness (which lets hidden genetic variation accumulate, and whose buffering mechanisms cannot readily be disentangled into selected vs unselected); pleiotropy — "master genes are 'masters'", one viable mutation moving many traits at once (the tentative Otx/brain case: cortex features as possible *spandrels* of selection on kidneys, larynx, etc.); developmental modules; Schmalhausen's coordination (selection cannot select isolated traits, only coordinated complexes); morphogenetic explosions (Ediacara, Cambrian, white-eye radiation) against gradualism; plasticity and the *non-transitivity* of fitness (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A when all compete; criss-crossing norms of reaction). Closing caveat — the book's own, and load-bearing for honest use of it: "the majority of biologists whom we have cited here… still today endorse natural selection as the determinant par excellence of the course of evolution"; the authors settle, at this stage, for a stand-off — the empirical case makes adaptationism "less than apodictic", no more.
- **4. Many Constraints, Many Environments** [file ch12] ★ (supporting) → both entries. Selection at many levels, and phenomena that strain the canonical picture: Waddington's genetic assimilation (ether-shocked fruit flies, a second pair of wings, stabilised with *no adaptive relation* to the selecting environment — "selection without adaptation"); the *E. coli* sugar case (Hall's experiments: strains that gain sugar-2 digestion become dead ends for sugar-3; the order of selective steps is crucial and cannot be reconstructed in nature; free-riders detectable only because experimenters could dissociate coextensive traits — "natural selection doesn't perform experiments"); drift, frequency- and density-dependence, "ascent of the abundant"; West-Eberhard on environmentally initiated novelty and genetic accommodation ("genes are probably more often followers than leaders"); epigenetics and imprinting (Dutch famine and Swedish harvest records as transgenerational cases; heritability of epigenetic marks flagged as still open — "the jury is still out"); horizontal gene transfer and transposons (the "tree of descent" becoming a bush/network); interchangeability of internal and external perturbations (bearded-dragon sex determination as a GSD/TSD transition case).
- **5. The Return of the Laws of Form** [file ch13] ★ → *Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism*. Constraints "from above": physico-chemical and mathematical invariants (Fibonacci phyllotaxis, quarter-power scaling laws and the fractal "fourth dimension" of vascular networks, wiring-minimisation in nervous systems — Cherniak's "non-genomic nativism", grey/white-matter segregation, locomotion invariants, birdsong, leaf design, optimal foraging ratios, the invariant bird wing-stroke, the zombifying wasp's rigid behavioural programme). The recurring form of the argument: the search space is too large for blind trial-and-error plus filtering to have found these near-optimal solutions, and neo-Darwinism itself insists selection only *satisfices* — so if optima exist, "something else must be involved" (physics, chemistry, self-organisation). History of the idea: D'Arcy Thompson, Lotka–Volterra, Turing, Prigogine, Thom, Waddington, Kauffman, Gould & Lewontin. The authors concede these cases are unexplained at present — "we have no such explanation at present" — and rest on the dilemma, not on a rival mechanism.
- **PART TWO — THE CONCEPTUAL SITUATION** [divider, file ch14]. The a priori half of the book: the intensionality argument.
- **6. Many Are Called but Few Are Chosen: The Problem of 'Selection-For'** [file ch15] ★ → both entries. The core chapter. Free-riding after Gould & Lewontin: spandrels of San Marco (architects select *for* arches; spandrels come along willy-nilly) — but Fodor & Piattelli-Palmarini argue Gould & Lewontin's own remedy stays inside adaptationism, and that the rot is deeper. Avatars of one problem: Hempel's heart (pumping blood vs making noises — which is the *function*?); learning theory's "what is learned?" (split-stimulus and split-response experiments settle post hoc which coextensive property was the effective one); the naturalisation of content (the frog snaps at *flies* or at *ambient black nuisances* — cause alone cannot fix content). Diagnosis: selection-for problems arise wherever an explanation must distinguish the causal roles of *coextensive* properties; the distinction is decided by counterfactuals; but selection is a *local* process, sensitive only to actual causal transactions, and "merely possible predators do not affect the evolution of a population". The chapter's numbered conclusion: (1) selection-for is a causal process; (2) actual causal relations aren't sensitive to counterfactual states of affairs; (3) the selected-for/free-rider distinction turns on counterfactuals; (4) so, for coextensive T and T′, selection cannot distinguish which free-rides on which; (5) "so the claim that selection is the mechanism of evolution cannot be true." The escape available to architects and breeders — minds, which *can* represent counterfactuals — is exactly what natural selection lacks; Darwin's breeder analogy "abstracts the minds away", and that, the authors say, cannot be done.
- **7. No Exit? Some Responses to the Problem of 'Selection-For'** [file ch16] ★ → both entries. Four candidate rescues for adaptationism, each examined and rejected by the authors: (i) *Give Mother Nature a chance* — take the breeder analogy literally (fails: natural selection "doesn't have a mind"; Mother Nature, selfish genes etc. are fictions, and "fictions can't select things"); (ii) *Laws of selection* — laws support counterfactuals (fails, in the authors' view: fitness is massively context-sensitive — the "fallacy of the Swiss apple", phenotypes are fusions not bundles of independent traits, competition outcomes are interaction effects — so there are no nomological generalisations about which traits win); (iii) *Sober's sieve* — the marble-sorting example (fails twice over: the intuition works only because we know the sieve's endogenous mechanism, which adaptationist explanation typically lacks; and which marbles count as "selected" flips between sifting flour and panning for gold, i.e. with the sorter's intentions); (iv) *Adaptationism as theory schema* (fails: "adaptation" and "ecological problem" are interdefined, so "adaptations are solutions to ecological problems" is a truism, not an empirical claim). The chapter then re-describes the adaptationist explanations that *do* work: they are **historical explanations** — plausible narratives articulating causal chains (Waterloo and the mud; a banana peel), not subsumptions under laws; hence post hoc, non-predictive, and unable to support the counterfactuals trait-individuation needs. Closing analogy: explanations of why this man got rich, case by case, without any theory of getting rich.
- **8. Did the Dodo Lose Its Ecological Niche? Or Was It the Other Way Around?** [file ch17] ★ → *Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism*. Replies to what the authors call perhaps the strongest argument for adaptationism: the "exquisite fit" of organisms to their environments (Sober, Mayr quoted). Their reply is a dilemma. *Environment* (the world, common to all) must not be confused with *ecology/niche* (whatever-it-is-about-the-world in virtue of which this phenotype can make a living): "ecology" and "phenotype" are *interdefined*, so fit to one's niche is true but tautological, while fit to the *environment* simpliciter needs no selection theory — anything alive is ipso facto adapted to the world. Mayr's Platonic niches ("open niches… virtually calling" for woodpeckers in New Guinea) are mocked; Dawkins's "making a living" rejoinder is argued to belong to the same circle of interdefinition (niche ↔ adaptation ↔ good-for ↔ selection-for). The extinction of the dodo *was* the extinction of the dodo's way of making a living, so neither explains the other. What does explain adaptivity is natural history, synchronic (how does it make a living?) and diachronic (by what actual process did it come to?) — and the absence of phenotypes (no winged pigs: "there is no place on pigs to put them") is explained by endogenous constraints, not by selection against them.
- **9. Summary and Postlude** [file ch18] ★ → both entries. The short answer to "so what is the mechanism of evolution?": "we don't know… nobody knows exactly how phenotypes evolve"; possibly there is no unitary mechanism. Recapitulates the two complaints (the free-rider/intensionality argument; the underestimation of endogenous factors) and draws the philosophy-of-science moral: adaptationism falsely claims there is a *level* of evolutionary explanation. Psychology has such a level (the intentional), evolution does not; natural history "collapses across ontological levels" and is "just one damned thing after another" — as is history tout court. Darwin's mistake is paired with Marx's: imagining that history is a theoretical domain. Preferred replacement metaphor: organisms "catch" their phenotypes from their ecologies as they catch colds — multilevel, endogenous-structure-dependent contagion rather than selection. The closing claim is negative and naturalistic: no ghost in their machine — no God, Mother Nature, Selfish Genes, World Spirit, or phantom breeders.
- **Appendix** [file ch20]. " 'That' kind of Darwinian in psychology and the philosophy of mind": a dossier of quotations (Boltzmann, James, Peirce, Dewey, Quine, and evolutionary psychology/epistemology up to 2009) meant to show that hard-line adaptationism thrives in the theory's export markets even if wet biologists have abandoned it. Documentation, not argument.
- **Notes** [file ch21], **References** [file ch22], **Index** [file ch23], **Copyright page** [file ch24]. The notes carry substantive asides (including the authors' footnote-level qualifications) and were treated as apparatus, not as chapter text, for this slice's entries.
