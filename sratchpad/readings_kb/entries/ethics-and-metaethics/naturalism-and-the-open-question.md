# Naturalism, Non-naturalism, and the Open Question
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics · Also known as: the fact–value problem; moral naturalism vs non-naturalism (the is/ought gap is the popular name for the divide this entry examines — see the note in "Where the debate stands")*

## The question
An action is cruel: it was a deliberate wounding of another person, done for the sake of harming him, and the attacker took pleasure in it. All of that is factual description — the sort of thing observation and the sciences can identify. The action is also *wrong, because* it is cruel. Is the wrongness one more descriptive property of the action? How is moral value related to the natural, non-moral facts and properties of the world — defined by them, constituted by them, dependent on them, identical with them, wholly independent of them, or not a feature of the world at all, but something our judgments *express*? This is the question "Where (if anywhere) in the world is moral value?", and every answer reorganises the rest of metaethics.

## The 90-second version
Moore's open question argument begins from a contrast. Some questions are *closed*: "Are bachelors unmarried?" is answered by knowing the language — a "yes" costs nothing because it is true by definition, a barren tautology that gives no reason to act. The argument tests proposed definitions of good the same way. Suppose good just *means* pleasure (or happiness, or God's will). Then "Is pleasure good?" should be as closed as the bachelor question. It is not: competent speakers who know exactly what every word means can still genuinely ask it — it stays **open**. So no candidate X is synonymous with good; good cannot be defined. The lecture then draws a further, more skeptical moral than Moore drew: if goodness is not the property picked out by any predicate, perhaps goodness is not a property at all — "just a strange concept we invented." Hold that last step at arm's length: in the textbook's presentation, Moore himself was a *realist* about good — indefinable, but real — and the lecture's conclusion (that good does not exist) is the skeptical spin the channel puts on the argument, not Moore's. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [Moore's Open Question](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_GI1KsjBQYs).)

## Positions

### Moore's non-naturalism: good is simple, indefinable, and real
"If I am asked 'What is good?' my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter." Any attempt to define good in other terms — naturalistic terms above all — commits the **naturalistic fallacy**: it confuses good with some *other* property that good things also have, as yellowness is confused with the vibrations yellow things produce. Good things may all be pleasant, or desired, but pleasantness is not what *good* is.

**Canonical argument — the open question argument** (source: Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 4, presenting Moore, *Principia Ethica* — a formalization; the lecture's version is structurally the same):
- P1. If "good" were definable as D (pleasure, happiness, what is desired, or any other candidate), then "Is what is D good?" would not be a genuine, open question — it would collapse into "Is what is D D?", settled by the meanings of the words.
- P2. For every candidate D, "Is what is D good?" remains an open question: "it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good."
- ∴ C. Good is not definable in terms of anything else. It is a simple, *sui generis* property — real and objective, dependent for its presence on non-moral properties (a thing is good *in virtue of* its good-making properties), but reducible to none of them.

**The stakes Moore saw** — if good *could* be defined in non-moral terms, the definition would show that what we called a fundamental moral value "is really, ultimately, something non-moral": defining the moral away eliminates it. Jacobs's caution for the naturalist side: Moore's alternatives (simple and indefinable; a complex awaiting correct analysis; meaningless — "there is no such subject as Ethics") were driven by a *semantic* test, as though definition were the only way to establish a necessary connection between properties. The reconstructed naturalists attack exactly that assumption.

### Reconstructed naturalism: constitution and identity without definition
Keep cognitivism and the reality of moral value; drop the definitional route. Moral properties **supervene** on natural properties *because they are constituted by them* (Brink) — as wetness is constituted by the properties of H₂O: a real property, non-accidentally dependent on a base unlike itself, connected by discoverable (empirical, law-like) relations rather than by meanings. The stronger variant is an **identity thesis**: moral value is *identical with* certain natural properties, as water is identical with H₂O — a necessary truth, but one discovered by inquiry and theory, not by analysing words; people used "water" correctly before chemistry existed.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 4 — a formalization of the reconstructed-naturalist strategy):
- P1. A necessary connection between moral and natural properties need not be semantic: constitution (wetness/H₂O) and theoretical identity (water = H₂O) are necessary relations ascertainable only by substantive inquiry.
- P2. If moral properties are constituted by, or identical with, natural properties in either of those ways, then "Is what is D good?" can remain an open *question* for speakers — openness reflects our epistemic position, not a metaphysical gap.
- ∴ C. The open question argument does not refute naturalism; it refutes only the claim that good is *definable* in natural terms, which the reconstructed naturalist also denies.

**Objections** — Moore himself held dependence without constitution: good is *sui generis*, and (Jacobs judges) he would have balked at its being *literally constituted* by anything non-moral; his focus on definition missed the H₂O model partly because that model was not his target. Whether identity claims for value can actually be *established* the way chemical identities were is left, in this text, as the naturalist's promissory note.

### Humean ("skeptical") naturalism and expressivism
A second way to be a naturalist: deny that values are natural *properties* at all, while insisting that nothing non-natural is needed to explain moral valuing. Moral judgments express sentiments, propensities, and concerns that are themselves natural facts about human beings; the relation to non-moral properties is *subjective* supervenience. This is [Emotivism and Expressivism](emotivism-and-expressivism.md) in its ch. 4 dress: naturalism about what morality *is made of* (human nature), anti-realism about what moral judgments *say*. Jacobs's contrast is exact: realist naturalism tries "to save moral objectivity without non-naturalism"; Humean naturalism "also avoids non-naturalism but without treating moral values as objective… skeptical naturalism, in contrast to realist naturalism."

### Reconnecting fact and value (Foot, McDowell): the dichotomy itself is the mistake
Both camps above assume descriptive meaning and evaluative meaning can be cleanly separated. Foot denies it: you cannot extract an "evaluative meaning" of *good* specifiable independently of its objects — can one understand what fairness or cruelty *is* and then face a further, open question of stance toward it? McDowell's analogy: redness is "a property the ascription of which to an object is not adequately understood except as true, if it is true, in virtue of the object's disposition to present a certain sort of perceptual appearance" — the tomato *is* red, though our access to its redness runs through our sensibility. Moral properties are relevantly similar: judgments about them are **merited** by their objects (the moral difference from perception is that the relation is merit, not causation). In McDowell's later "no-priority" view, neither world nor response comes first: as with the funny — our laughing doesn't make things funny, unfunny things get laughed at, funny things go unlaughed at — the response is *apt or not* in light of its object. Corollaries Jacobs lists: no fact/value distinction of the classic kind; sensibility's role does not undermine objectivity; moral judgments are literally true or false; and none of this works by theoretically reducing moral to natural properties.

### Aristotle's naturalism
Value grounded in natures: for each natural kind there is a condition of successful actualisation, and for humans — who pursue their good deliberately, through reason and choice — the virtues are the states in which feeling and cognition are aligned toward the genuinely good ("virtue makes the goal correct, and intelligence makes what promotes the goal [correct]"). Moral significance is found *in* the facts, acts, and situations of the natural world, without identity or supervenience machinery. The standing objection: the view rides on a teleological conception of nature that modern science has abandoned — "there are no norms or values 'built into' nature." The modern retrieval (Foot, McDowell) attempts to keep the moral psychology and epistemology — character, the reason–desire alignment, good and bad pleasures — while dropping the cosmic teleology; whether the elements survive the extraction is, Jacobs says, an open question the theorists themselves are pursuing.

### Do moral facts explain anything? (Harman vs Sturgeon and Railton)
A second front, independent of definition: entities earn their place by explanatory work — we posit genes because inheritance needs them, not demons, because schizophrenia doesn't. **Harman**: a moral observation is not evidence for a moral theory the way a physical observation is evidence for a physical theory, because "the truth or falsity of the moral observation seems to be completely irrelevant to any reasonable explanation of why that observation was made" — your moral sensibility plus the non-moral facts explain your judgment that the cruel act is wrong, whether or not it *is* wrong. Moral facts are "an explanatorily idle accessory." **Sturgeon** replies that the idleness claim overreaches: it is not just judgments but *behaviour* that moral facts explain — Hitler's being depraved is part of the best explanation of what he did; had everything non-moral been the same but not constituted depravity, would he really have done exactly the same? Moral facts also explain why the virtuous are trusted with office and why just arrangements are stable. **Railton**: ought-facts are "facts of a special kind about the way things are" — supervening, hence metaphysically unsuspect — and we can ask empirically whether our judgment-forming processes are *reliable* routes to them (the near-universal rejection of trial by ordeal has factual grounds of exactly this kind).

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 4, presenting Harman, *The Nature of Morality* — a formalization):
- P1. We are justified in positing a kind of fact only if it is needed in the best explanation of some range of phenomena (genes: yes; demons: no).
- P2. Every moral judgment and observation is fully explained by two kinds of fact — facts about the psychology (sensibility, principles) of the judge, and non-moral facts about the world — without supposing the judgment true.
- ∴ C. There is no explanatory need to posit moral facts; commitment to them is unsupported by the method that supports our other factual commitments.

Sturgeon's rebuttal targets P2's scope (behaviour, not just judgment) and its counterfactual (same psychology, no depravity, same actions?); the text presents the exchange without a winner.

## Thought experiments
- **Water and wetness** (Jacobs, ch. 4). Wetness supervenes on H₂O without being definable in terms of hydrogen and oxygen; water *is* H₂O without "water" meaning H₂O. The double analogy is the reconstructed naturalist's whole case in miniature: it exhibits necessary moral–natural connections that Moore's semantic test cannot see. It pressures non-naturalism by demanding to know why value could not stand to its base as wetness stands to its base.
- **The run-over dog belongs to the bullshit entry**; this entry's perceptual case is **the ripe tomato**: its red as experienced is not an exact copy of the physical structures causing the experience — which tempts the projection model of value (value as "colouring" we add) — until McDowell's dispositional reading restores the red to the tomato.

## Where the debate stands
Jacobs's map offers seven possible moral–natural relations — definition, constitution, dependence, identity-without-definability, full independence, expressive understanding, supernatural grounding — and no verdict among them; "all of the debates that we identified remain very much alive." Two honest notes for this KB. First, terminology: the textbook never uses the label "is/ought gap." What it examines is the same divide under other descriptions — Hume's insistence that reason discovers truths but cannot, without sentiment, produce blame or approbation; Mackie's question about the "because" linking natural fact to wrongness; Moore's open question; the twentieth century's fact/value distinction. Readers arriving with the "is/ought" label should map it onto those discussions rather than expect the slogan. Second, the lecture's closing move — from "good is indefinable" to "goodness is not a property / does not exist" — is a step *beyond* both Moore and Jacobs's presentation of him, in which indefinability is combined with full-blooded realism about good; the entry has kept the two apart deliberately.
