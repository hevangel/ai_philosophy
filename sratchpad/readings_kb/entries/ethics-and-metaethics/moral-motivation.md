# Moral Motivation
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics (moral psychology) · Also known as: internalism vs externalism about motivation (the textbook does not use these labels; see "Where the debate stands")*

## The question
Can knowing what is right *by itself* move someone to act? Or is motivation always separate — supplied by desire, feeling, or interest — so that a person could fully accept a moral judgment and remain utterly unmoved? The question matters twice over. It is a question in moral psychology: what actually moves moral agents? And it is a lever in metaethics: if moral judgments were bare reports of fact, their apparent power to move us would be mysterious — the inertness charge Mackie pressed against objectivism. Our textbook chapter opens it with a deliberately ordinary case and three great rival answers: Kant's, Hume's, and Aristotle's.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture is devoted to this problem; the following is distilled from the textbook (Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 2) and is marked accordingly, per the build rules.* A motorist is stranded by the roadside, his arm in a cast, unable to lift the hood of his car. A passer-by could stop and help. Why might he? The textbook lists ten distinguishable motives: because helping is the right thing to do; because he hopes for a reward; because he knows what helplessness feels like and would want help himself; sheer sympathy; because his father taught him never to pass someone in trouble; because he wants to feel good about himself; because he thinks it will please God; and more. Most motives are *sentiments or desires*, not recognitions of duty. Kant and Hume then fight over the special case: the motive "because it is right." For Kant, the moral motive must be reason itself — an act has moral worth when done *because* it is right, and reason alone, without the cooperation of inclination, can determine the will. For Hume this is impossible: "reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular; it cannot either prevent volition or oppose the preference of any passion or emotion." A bare cognition that an act is right moves nothing; something must already *care*. Aristotle offers a third picture in which the fight dissolves: in the virtuous person, reason and desire are *aligned* — trained together from childhood — so that doing right is also what one enjoys; the question "which moved him, reason or desire?" has, for such a person, no sharp answer.

## Positions

### Kant: reason alone can motivate, and only the rational motive has moral worth
Moral requirements bind rational agents as such; correspondingly, the will can be determined by reason's own recognition of duty, independently of desire. An action done from sympathy, or hope of reward, or habit, may *conform* to duty, but it lacks moral worth: worth requires that the agent act *because the act is right* — the rightness itself, apprehended by reason, is the motive. Jacobs notes the purifying consequence the theory intends: moral worth is thereby insulated from the accidents of temperament and fortune (see [Moral Luck](moral-luck.md)).

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 2, presenting Kant — a formalization):
- P1. A motive grounded in the agent's desires or inclinations binds only conditionally: it moves the agent only while the inclination lasts, and its authority is that of the inclination, not of morality.
- P2. Moral requirements have unconditional authority for rational agents — they bind the agent *as* a rational agent, whatever his inclinations.
- ∴ C. The distinctively moral motive must be reason itself: the recognition that the act is required. Only action from that motive — duty done because it is duty — has full moral worth.

**Objections** — Hume's impotence charge (below) is the direct denial of the conclusion's psychology. A second pressure, from within the chapter: the plurality of the motorist's motives suggests that recognising duty is rarely how human beings are actually moved, and a theory of moral worth that discounts all ten motives but one risks describing an agent nobody is. Kant's framework can reply that moral worth is a standard, not a census — but the chapter leaves the exchange open.

### Hume: reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions
Reason's office is to discover truth — relations of ideas and matters of fact. But "where the truths which they discover are indifferent, and beget no desire or aversion, they can have no influence on conduct and behaviour." Motivation requires a passion; reason can inform a passion (correct its factual beliefs, find means to its end) but cannot originate one, and cannot oppose one except by another. Applied to morality: the judgment that cruelty is wrong moves us only because a sentiment — sympathy, the moral sense — is already engaged; the judgment's motivating force is borrowed from feeling, never from cognition alone.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 2, presenting Hume's *Treatise* and *Enquiry* — a formalization):
- P1. Cognition alone is motivationally inert: a state that merely represents how things are cannot, by itself, produce volition or oppose a passion.
- P2. Moral judgments do motivate — reliably, across agents who sincerely make them.
- ∴ C. Moral judgment must involve more than cognition: it essentially engages sentiment. (This is the engine of [subjectivism](moral-relativism-and-subjectivism.md) and of [expressivism](emotivism-and-expressivism.md): if judgments move us, and bare facts cannot, judgments must express the feelings that move us.)

**Objections** — Kant's position is the standing counter-thesis (reason's self-determination). Jacobs also records Korsgaard's more recent Kantian development: rational agents act under a **practical identity** — a conception of themselves (as parent, friend, professional, moral agent) — and motivation flows from the need to act in ways one can reflectively endorse as consistent with that identity; to act against it is to lose one's integrity as that kind of agent. This gives reason a motivational office without making it a rival passion — though whether "reflective endorsement" is reason moving us or a desire to be a certain sort of person is precisely the Kant–Hume dispute restated.

### Aristotle: in the virtuous agent, reason and desire are trained into alignment
Not a compromise between Kant and Hume but a different shape of answer. The virtuous person does not first apprehend a duty and then struggle to want it, nor act on raw feeling unguided by reason: upbringing and habituation have formed the desires so that they *track* what reason discerns as good. The virtuous enjoy acting well — pleasure in right action is a *sign* of achieved virtue, not a contaminant of it (contra Kant) — and their reason is genuinely practical, not a spectator of their passions (contra Hume). Moral motivation, on this view, is a *developmental achievement*, which is why Aristotle makes upbringing "all-important" — and why the theory inherits the luck problems of [Moral Luck](moral-luck.md).

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 2, presenting Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* — a formalization):
- P1. In excellent practical agency, what reason asserts is what desire pursues: the practically wise person's judgments and appetites have been formed together, by habituation, so that the objects reason discerns as worthy are the objects desire finds attractive.
- P2. Neither element explains the achievement alone: untrained desire is blind to worth; unhabituated reason knows the good without reliably wanting it (the incontinent agent is the standing case of the second failure).
- ∴ C. Moral motivation is neither reason's solo act (Kant) nor passion's (Hume), but the condition of a character in which the two have been educated into one orientation toward the good.

### Mackie's rider: opting out is not irrational
Even granting morality its psychological machinery, a deflationary point survives. Mackie held that someone who simply declined to be moved by moral considerations — who opted out of the moral point of view — would not thereby be *irrational*: reason, on the Humean showing, cannot convict him of an error, since it cannot supply the missing sentiment. The textbook presents this as the sceptical residue of the debate: morality's authority over motivation is real for those inside it, but its grip on the amoralist cannot be demonstrated from reason alone. (Compare the egoist's role in [Self-interest and Egoism](self-interest-and-egoism.md).)

## Thought experiments
- **The stranded motorist** (Jacobs, ch. 2). The arm in a cast, the hood too heavy, the passer-by who might stop — and ten motives for stopping that are genuinely different from one another. The case's work is taxonomic: it forces the question of *which* motive, if any, is the moral one, and whether "the moral one" is even a single thing. Any theory of moral motivation must sort the ten without merely defining the answer in.
- **The incontinent agent** (Aristotle, via ch. 2). He judges, correctly, that he should not; he wants, anyway; he does. He is the standing refutation of simple intellectualism (knowing the good is not sufficient) and the datum the Aristotelian alignment view is built to explain: what he lacks is not information but formation.

## Where the debate stands
Unsettled, and the textbook is explicit that the three-way structure (Kant / Hume / Aristotle) is a standing menu, not a historical sequence with a winner. Two clarifications for the KB. First, terminology: the contemporary labels *internalism* and *externalism* about moral motivation are not used in this text; the debate is present in substance (must sincere moral judgment motivate? Kant and Hume divide over the mechanism) but readers should not import the modern taxonomy's finer subdivisions and expect to find them here. Second, the debate's metaethical leverage: Hume's P1 is the same premise as Mackie's inertness objection to objectivism — whoever wins here constrains the objectivity debate — and Jacobs's own leaning is visible only in the architecture: the Aristotelian third view is presented last in its section and at greatest sympathy, without a verdict.
