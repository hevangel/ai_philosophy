# Bullshit vs Lying
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics (philosophy of language / epistemology of discourse) · Also known as: Frankfurt on bullshit; bullshit as a category distinct from lying and from nonsense*

## The question
What is bullshit, and how does it differ from lying? The liar and the bullshitter both produce discourse we distrust, but Harry Frankfurt's celebrated analysis — the occasion for the anthology that is this entry's source — claims they are doing *fundamentally different things*: the liar is engaged with the truth (he must know or believe it, in order to conceal it), while the bullshitter is **indifferent** to whether what he says is true or false; his eye is on the impression he makes, not on the facts. If that is right, bullshit is not failed truth-telling but a different enterprise altogether — and, Frankfurt concluded, "a greater enemy of the truth than lies are." The anthology's contributors then fight over the definition: whether indifference is the essence or merely the usual tactic; whether some bullshit is identifiable in the *product* rather than the producer; whether the real wrong is semantic or pragmatic; whether bullshitting can be blameless — or even, in the case of politeness, part of what manners *are*. **Source declaration**: Frankfurt's own *On Bullshit* is *not* in this corpus. Everything attributed to Frankfurt below is as presented and quoted within the anthology *Bullshit and Philosophy* (book 005), and is labelled as such.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture treats bullshit (a filename search of the full lecture corpus found no bullshit or sincerity lecture); the following is distilled from the textbook (book 005) and is marked accordingly, per the build rules.* Frankfurt's puzzle, with which the anthology opens: why do we tolerate bullshit so much more readily than lying? The liar at least honours the truth by hiding from it; the bullshitter does not care whether his statements are true or false, so long as they serve his purpose — typically, making an impression or getting something past an audience. Two ingredients, on the anthology's best survey: **indifference to truth** in the content, and **pretence** about the enterprise — the bullshitter misrepresents what he is *doing* (he represents himself as engaged in truth-telling inquiry while being engaged in something else). Hence the slogan: bullshit is a greater enemy of the truth than lies are, because the liar remains in the truth's orbit — he needs it, tracks it, and can be caught by it — while the bullshitter has left the orbit entirely. The volume then complicates its hero: maybe pretence is not essential (the openly indifferent politician bullshits without pretending); maybe some bullshit is blameless or even socially necessary — the polite formula, the faked apology, the pleasantry nobody believes and nobody is deceived by — so that, in one contributor's phrase, bullshit constitutes the greater part of civility; and maybe there is a third kind Frankfurt missed, the pseudoscientific, which is neither insincere nor obscure but simply *insensitive to evidence*.

## Positions

### Frankfurt's account: truth-indifference plus pretence (as presented in the volume)
Bullshit is discourse produced with indifference to its truth-value, where the speaker represents himself as engaged in a truth-aimed enterprise. The bullshitter is not (typically) trying to get a falsehood believed — that is the liar's game, and the fraud's; he is trying to get *himself* believed in: as knowledgeable, as sincere, as the sort of person who holds forth on this. That is why detection so often fails: the sentences may be true, and checking them misses the point.

**Canonical argument** (source: Hardcastle & Reisch (eds.), *Bullshit and Philosophy*, Introduction and ch. 11 (Maes & Schaubroeck), presenting Frankfurt, *On Bullshit* — a formalization):
- P1. The liar intends to deceive about the facts: he believes (or takes) the truth to be one thing and asserts another; his enterprise is defined by its relation — opposition — to the truth.
- P2. The bullshitter's assertions are unconstrained by belief about the facts either way: he "does not care whether the things he says describe reality correctly"; he selects them to suit his purpose, while representing his enterprise as the truth-telling one the liar honestly opposes.
- ∴ C. Bullshit is not a species of lying but a distinct and, in one respect, graver offence against discourse: lying respects the authority of truth enough to hide from it; bullshit disregards that authority altogether — "bullshit is a greater enemy of the truth than lies are."

**Internal exclusions** (Frankfurt, as reported by Maes & Schaubroeck) — mere bull *sessions* (men talking loosely, not pretending to precision) are not bullshit proper, since the pretence ingredient fails; the volume's critics contest whether that line can be held (below).

### Cohen: bullshit as product — unclarifiable unclarity
G. A. Cohen argues Frankfurt located the phenomenon in the wrong place: in the producer's process rather than the product. There is a kind of discourse identifiable as bullshit *on its face* — discourse that is **unclarifiably unclear**: it resists every attempt to state clearly what it says, and its obscurity is not the fertile obscurity of poetry (which rewards interpretation with suggestion) but a dead obscurity that persists when suggestiveness is subtracted. The test is a kind of negation: take the discourse, remove what can be clarified, and ask whether anything remains that could be true or false — Cohen's bullshit leaves nothing standing. Producers of such discourse may be perfectly sincere (seminar-room bullshit often is), which Frankfurt's process account cannot capture.

**Canonical argument** (source: *Bullshit and Philosophy*, ch. 8, Cohen, "Deeper into Bullshit" — a formalization):
- P1. Some discourse is recognisably bullshit independently of anything known about its producer's intentions or sincerity: its sentences cannot be clarified into any determinate claim — every paraphrase either changes the subject or exposes a banality the original's obscurity was concealing.
- P2. A process-based (intention-based) definition classifies discourse by its producer's attitudes and so must call such discourse non-bullshit whenever the producer is sincere — the wrong result.
- ∴ C. Bullshit exists as a *product*-category — unclarifiable unclarity — alongside whatever intention-based category Frankfurt identified; a complete theory needs both senses (which the OED, Cohen notes, already distinguishes).

**Objections in the volume** — Cohen's own caution: the "essence" formulation he himself reaches for risks the same overreach he charges Frankfurt with; and the tactic/goal distinction (Pavel's Minsk/Pinsk joke: the peasant who names the wrong station *tactically*, to misdirect, vs *strategically*) shows intention-analyses also fragment. Maes & Schaubroeck's survey lets both definitions stand as marking different, overlapping phenomena.

### Bernal: the target — bullshit as obscuring something specific
Against Frankfurt's indifference, Bernal argues the bullshitter is usually *connected* to the truth through a **target**: something specific he needs hidden. Two modes: (a) *indirect implication* — saying something literally true in a way engineered to implicate a falsehood (a resort advertised as "alive with pleasure"); (b) *distraction* — flooding attention elsewhere so the target goes unexamined (the anthology's example: the Niger yellowcake story's role in fixing attention during the Iraq WMD case). Consciousness of the target is a spectrum: split-brain confabulation shows the mind producing confident, false, self-serving narrative with no conscious deceiving self behind it — bullshitting without a bullshitter fully in charge — which is why Bernal links the phenomenon to personality and habit: unconscious bullshitting, lacking conscience's sting, is the easier habit to fall into.

### Reisch: bullshit as pragmatic cloaking
The editor's own rival definition relocates the essence from semantics to **pragmatics**: bullshit cloaks one kind of speech-engagement inside another — advocacy dressed as inquiry, salesmanship dressed as scholarship. The test case is Intelligent Design: its advocates are *not* truth-indifferent (they care intensely about a specific conclusion), so Frankfurt's definition fails them; what they conceal is not a fact but an *agenda* — the engagement is evangelism conducted in the costume of science. This also dissolves Frankfurt's puzzle about tolerance: the liar sins against semantics (against the statement's relation to fact, which we police), the bullshitter against pragmatics (against the terms of the engagement, which we police less, and differently). A corollary Reisch calls **purism**: properly defined, there is much less real bullshit than casual usage supposes — most of what gets called bullshit is error, disagreement, or ordinary persuasion.

### Maes & Schaubroeck: two revisions and a third kind
The volume's most systematic revision. **Revision 1 — pretence is not essential**: Pascal's self-description (he is like "a dog that has been run over" for philosophy — Wittgenstein's borrowed line) and the openly indifferent politician show bullshit without any misrepresentation of enterprise; Frankfurt's exclusion of bull sessions on pretence-grounds is, they argue, artificial. **Revision 2 — bullshit is not always bad**: banter, Wildean paradox, and the polite formulae of social life (Thomas Nagel's "How nice to see you" said to someone one is not pleased to see) are bullshit by the definition and *good* — the lubricant of civility, deception-free because nobody is meant to be deceived. **A third kind — pseudoscience**: sincere, often crystal-clear, so neither Frankfurt's nor Cohen's category fits; its marks are insensitivity to evidence and fallacious reasoning — bullshit by epistemic conduct rather than by indifference or obscurity.

### Kimbrough: why we let it slide — bullshit, politeness, and manners
The volume's opening chapter takes up Frankfurt's puzzle (our tolerance) functionally. We tolerate bullshit when its *ends* are served cheaply and its costs low: entertainment (the bull session as play), **politeness** — via Jennifer Miller's *Faking It*, the argument that insincere-seeming performances (the faked apology, the performed enthusiasm) are not failures of civility but its mechanism, so that "bullshit constitutes the greater part of civility" — and evasion where directness would cost more than the truth is worth there. Tolerance ends where bullshit becomes a method: Kimbrough's positive proposal is to see bullshit as **lame methods of justification** — discourse that licenses dismissal of what should have been answered — whose social function, for its producers, is exactly that licence. Some bullshit is blameless (Newton's alchemy: sincere, methodical, wrong — the false content carried by honest enterprise); what we should restrict is not the category but its use as an excuse for not giving reasons. (This chapter, with Maes & Schaubroeck on polite formulae, is where this corpus's *manners* material actually lives — see the declaration under "Sources read.")

**Supporting voices in the volume** — Preti (ch. 2): the missing third element is the *audience's* compliance — bullshit is partly our fault; Moore's "What exactly do you mean?" is the model resistance. Aberdein (ch. 10): Frankfurt's category is better named **semantic negligence** — and Frankfurt's own definition is a *persuasive definition*, though (Aberdein argues) a diligent rather than negligent one. De Waal (ch. 7): the literature divides into *intentionalist* and *structuralist* schools; bullshitting as *fake inquiry* violates an "epistemic imperative" — with permissible cases (politeness, play) conceded. Fuller (ch. 16) dissents from the whole detector stance: the bullshit-hunt moralises epistemics, and the category slides toward sophistry/hypocrisy charges against whoever one dislikes.

## Thought experiments
- **The polite formula** (Nagel, via Maes & Schaubroeck). "How nice to see you," said without pleasure to someone one would rather avoid. Indifferent to truth, conventionally produced, nobody deceived, socially valuable. If it is bullshit, bullshit is not always a vice; if it is not, the definition owes an account of why not that does not collapse into "bullshit we approve of." The case is the volume's pressure-point between Frankfurt and the revisionists.
- **The faked apology** (Miller, via Kimbrough). An apology performed without felt contrition, which nonetheless repairs the relationship — both parties may know the feeling is absent. Civility running on performed sincerity: the "bullshit" *is* the manner. Readers arriving at this entry from the manners question should start here.
- **Pavel's stations** (the Minsk/Pinsk joke, via Cohen). Pavel says he is going to Minsk — actually Pinsk — so that when you conclude he lies, you will think Pinsk… The joke's analytic use in ch. 8: it separates *tactical* deception (the local false move) from *strategic* aim, showing that intention-based analyses of discourse must specify which level of intention they mean — a complication for every position above.

## Where the debate stands
The volume is an anthology, not a treatise, and its honest state is a contested definition with converging diagnostics. Agreement: bullshit is not lying, and is not mere falsehood or error; something about the speaker's *relation to truth* (or the discourse's relation to clarifiability, or the engagement's honesty) is the differentia. Disagreement: whether the essence is indifference (Frankfurt), target-obscuring engagement with truth (Bernal), pragmatic cloaking (Reisch), unclarifiable unclarity in the product (Cohen), evidence-insensitivity (Maes & Schaubroeck's third kind), or negligent speech (Aberdein) — and whether the category is a vice-term at all (Kimbrough and the politeness cases say: not always). The entry takes no side; the KB's rule against settling what sources leave open applies with special force to a book whose chapters argue with each other.
