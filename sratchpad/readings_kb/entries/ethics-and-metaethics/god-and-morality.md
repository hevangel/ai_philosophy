# God and Morality
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics · Also known as: divine command theory and the Euthyphro problem; the authority problem for morality*

## The question
Does morality need God — as its author, its ground, its guarantor, or its teacher? The standing suggestion is the **divine command theory**: an act is right because God wills it. The suggestion promises a great deal: a single source of unconditional obligation, a cosmic order in which justice is completed, an answer to "why be moral?" Against it stands a dilemma first posed in Plato's *Euthyphro* and never since retired. In our corpus the question is examined from both ends: the textbook asks what religious belief adds to (and subtracts from) morality, and two Carneades lectures press the dilemma and then test the most famous modern reply to it.

## The 90-second version
The Euthyphro dilemma is a fork. Either God commands an act *because it is right* — but then rightness is independent of God, God is merely a transcriber of moral facts that exist without him, and he is not really the author of morality at all; or an act is right *because God commands it* — but then morality is arbitrary: had God commanded, say, the yearly murder of a hundred children, that would by definition be good, a conclusion no believer can accept; and praise of God's own goodness goes empty, since "God is good" would mean only "God does what God does." The lecture's verdict is that the dilemma is a serious problem for divine command theory and that we should be skeptical of the view, perhaps suspending judgment on it. The second lecture presents William Lane Craig's escape: the dilemma is a *false* dilemma, because goodness is not something God *wills* nor something *outside* God — God simply *is* the Good; his nature is the standard. The lecture then weighs a counter-dilemma: if God has no control over his nature, is he not again powerless, a transcriber of logic? The charitable reply available to the theist is that having no control over one's nature does not imply impotence — God has no control over logic either, yet omnipotence was never thought to require doing the illogical — but grounding goodness in God's nature as a matter of *logic* is, the lecture concludes, a "tall order." (Distilled from two Carneades lectures: [The Euthyphro Dilemma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hMOD7ofD9Ck) and [God is the Good: William Lane Craig and the Euthyphro Dilemma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aV-GpEcv554).)

## Positions

### Divine command theory (as presented in the textbook)
On the theistic view Jacobs presents, "right action is action in accord with God's will": God is the ultimate authority to which morality answers, and this adds "an element of authority or of unconditional obligation" to morality that secular accounts struggle to supply. Jacobs is careful about what the theory is not: it is not the promise that good people will be rewarded and bad people punished (a prudential, desire-driven consideration), and it does not make God a moralist with cosmic police powers. What it claims is that on this view God is *necessarily* associated with morality — he is perfect goodness and his will accords with it.

**Canonical argument — the Euthyphro dilemma against divine command** (source: Jacobs, ch. 4, presenting Plato's *Euthyphro* — matching the lecture's fork):
- P1. Either acts are right because God commands them, or God commands them because they are right.
- P2. If the first: rightness is arbitrary (any command, however horrific, would constitute rightness), and God's own goodness becomes vacuous — he could not be praised for commanding what is right rather than what is not.
- P3. If the second: morality stands independent of God, divine authority is redundant to moral truth, and Kant's caution applies — "even the Holy One of the Gospel must first be compared with our ideal of moral perfection before we can recognize him as such."
- ∴ C. Divine command cannot be the fundamental ground of moral rightness: it either makes rightness arbitrary or makes God unnecessary to it.

**The theist's reply** (as Jacobs reconstructs it) — God's commands are not unintelligible: "God's commands are intelligible and acceptable to reflective people because they make sense in terms of largely shared values." Commands cannot turn "morality on its head," because a *perfect being* cannot command just anything — the very idea of a cruel, wasteful, or mendacious ultimate authority is at odds with the perfection in question. Concede, though, that our understanding of why some things are commands may exceed our grasp; the authority rests on perfection, not on our ability to audit every rationale. Whether this reply answers the dilemma or restates the second horn with confidence (the perfections doing the work are recognisably *ours* — see Kant's comparison test) is left, in the text, to the reader.

### Craig's identity reply: God *is* the Good
The third option the fork omits: goodness is neither external to God (first horn) nor a product of his arbitrary will (second) — it is identical with God's nature. God does not *decide* what is good, and goodness does not *constrain* him from outside; his own character is the standard, and his commands express it. (As presented in the lecture; Craig's own text is not in this corpus.)

**Canonical argument** (source: lecture presentation of Craig — a reconstruction from the lecture's exposition):
- P1. The Euthyphro fork assumes goodness is either willed by God or independent of God; a third possibility is that goodness is identical with God's nature.
- P2. If God is the Good, then his commands are good neither arbitrarily (they flow from a nature that *is* goodness) nor redundantly (there is no standard apart from God to which they answer).
- ∴ C. The dilemma is a false dilemma, and divine command theory survives in modified form: rightness is grounded in God's commands *as expressions of* his necessarily good nature.

**The lecture's counter-pressure** — if God's nature is not up to him, he lacks control over the moral standard in the same way the first horn's transcriber does; and the defence (no control over logic, either; omnipotence never required the illogical) works only if goodness follows from God's nature with something like logical necessity — "a tall order."

### The epistemic and motivational questions (the textbook's wider frame)
Jacobs separates three claims that are easily run together. (1) *Moral knowledge without God?* Even Aquinas's natural-law theism allows that human reason perceives important moral truths without reference to revelation — someone who does not believe in God "can have moral knowledge," though the rest of his worldview will be very different. (2) *Moral motivation without God?* If lack of belief disables someone from being appropriately motivated, that "would be a psychological fact about that person," not a metaethical result. (3) *What religion adds*: not a new foundation so much as dimensions naturalism lacks scope for — grace, cosmic justice, faith, redemption, the struggle with sin: "there is more to life than we see or than science can disclose," and religions present a *meaningful order* that the naturalist, even the objectivist naturalist, does not claim to find. And a clarifying caution: a faith-based ethics is not a relativism — believers "do not think that their view is just one among many perspectives"; each believes his or her view true.

### Can theists be moral? (the reverse question)
A third lecture turns the usual question around and tests five arguments *against* the morality of theists. Its structure matters for this entry: each argument overreaches. **Clifford's ethics of belief**: it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence (the shipowner who convinced himself his rotten ship was seaworthy, and drowned others, is guilty even when the ship happens to survive) — so theists qua believers violate an epistemic duty; the argument turns on a contested epistemology and would convict much ordinary belief besides. **Discrimination**: many religions discriminate in who can be saved or achieve nirvana — but that is *exclusivism*, arguably bad, not discrimination in the pejorative sense of arbitrary exclusion, and it is not true of every religion. **Complicity**: theists support organised religions that commit atrocities — but "theist" extends far beyond members of any atrocity-committing body; the argument fails against *all* theists. **The evil God**: an omnipotent, omniscient God who knowingly permits horrors might himself be evil, and then his followers immoral — but this targets one specific (Abrahamic, traditional-attribute) conception, and concludes at most that *that* God's followers might be immoral. The lecture's general lesson mirrors the textbook's caution against overreach: morality is not a binary possession — partial moral failure in one respect is not total immorality — and arguments that purport to settle "the theist question" wholesale prove, at most, something about some theists. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [Can Theists Be Moral? (5 Arguments)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3Pd5GWAE_Z4) — the file's title promises five arguments; the lecture works through four before generalising.)

## Thought experiments
- **The yearly command** (lecture). Suppose God commanded that a hundred children be murdered each year. On the "right because commanded" horn, that would be *good* — and no theist will say it. The case is the arbitrariness horn made vivid; the theist's textbook reply (a perfect being could not command it) applies here directly, and the debate then turns on what "could not" means.
- **Clifford's shipowner** (lecture, from W. K. Clifford). A shipowner, knowing his vessel is old and suspect, talks himself into sincere belief that she is seaworthy; she sinks. Guilty of the deaths, says Clifford — and guilty *even if* she had completed the voyage, because he had no right to believe on such evidence. The case is the engine of the "ethics of belief" argument against theists; it also stands on its own as a claim about epistemic duty, usable far beyond this debate.

## Where the debate stands
The Euthyphro fork remains the organising pressure, and neither source in this corpus declares it dissolved. The lecture recommends skepticism about (or suspension of judgment on) simple divine command theory, and treats Craig's nature-identity move as the live option whose cost — goodness grounded in God's nature with near-logical necessity — is still to be paid. The textbook is more ecumenical and less verdictive: it presents the dilemma, the theist's three-part reply, and then relocates much of the interest to epistemology (natural law: moral knowledge without revelation) and to what religion adds *around* morality rather than under it (grace, cosmic justice, redemption). Both sources agree on two negative points: that divine command is not best understood as a reward-and-punishment scheme, and that a faith-held morality is not thereby a relativism.
