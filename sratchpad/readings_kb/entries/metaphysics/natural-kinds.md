# Natural Kinds
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: natural kinds and classification; carving nature at its joints*

## The question
Some groupings seem to be found in nature and others merely made by us. "Gold", "water", "tiger" and "electron" appear to pick out categories that would be there whether or not we classified anything; "object bigger than a car" does not. What, if anything, makes a kind *natural* — a real structure in the world that science discovers, rather than a convenient way of talking? And if naturalness is real, what form does it take: an essence every member shares, a cluster of properties that hang together, or something discipline-relative?

## The 90-second version
Natural kinds are groups of things that occur naturally in the world, independently of our choice to classify them: elements such as hydrogen, compounds such as water or carbon dioxide, and — more controversially — species such as *Homo sapiens*. Scientific realism is vindicated, on this picture, if science's classifications map onto those naturally occurring types. The questions then split three ways. Metaphysical: do kinds exist, and what are they — irreducible entities, universals, or clusters of properties? Semantic: how do kind terms refer? And from the philosophy of science: does the metaphysics actually match what each science does? Chemistry is the strongest case: elements fall into their categories by number of protons, not by anything we stipulated — a view called microstructuralism. Biology is harder: species can be delineated by interbreeding or by common descent, the two delineations cross, and that pushes some toward saying species are conventions, and others toward a "promiscuous realism" on which many overlapping delineations are all natural and we simply pick out some of them. Positions run from naturalism/realism (kinds are real and discovered), through cluster-kind realism (kinds are co-occurring clusters of properties used for induction), to conventionalism (kinds are only ways scientists classify). (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [What are Natural Kinds?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXgnn8u5Hho).)

## Positions

### Metaphysical essentialism (Brian Ellis, as the volume presents and tests him)
A kind is natural only if it meets strict, a priori statable conditions. Ellis's six criteria, as the editors summarize them, require a natural kind to (1) be objective (mind-independent), (2) be categorically distinct, with no ontologically vague boundaries, (3) be demarcated from all other kinds by its intrinsic properties, (4) allow species variation (isotopes share an element-kind by essence — atomic number — while differing in atomic mass), (5) form a species-to-genus hierarchy where a particular belongs to two or more kinds, and (6) have an intrinsic essence both necessary and sufficient for membership.

**Canonical argument** (source: Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary, Introduction, book 013 ch. 1 — a formalization of the editors' presentation of Ellis):
- P1. Predictive and explanatory success comes in degrees, with no cut-off anywhere in the spectrum from physics through chemistry and biology to common sense; so success cannot *define* a metaphysically distinctive category of kinds.
- P2. A metaphysically distinctive category therefore needs a priori criteria of the Ellis type — objectivity, categorical distinctness, intrinsic demarcation, hierarchy, and a necessary-and-sufficient intrinsic essence.
- ∴ C. Kinds failing the criteria — biological species, whose boundaries evolution makes vague, among them — are not genuine natural kinds.

**Objections** — the editors: the criteria rule out by stipulation what the sciences treat as paradigms. Species are excluded for violating categorical distinctness; "protein" and "enzyme" — which on Tobin's showing "indicate real ontological differences in kind" — violate the hierarchy criterion (see [Classification and the Hierarchy Thesis](classification-and-the-hierarchy-thesis.md)). If natural kindhood is instead fixed by the explanatory and predictive role of kind concepts in scientific theorizing, nothing requires kinds to be hierarchically arranged or sharply bounded.

### Homeostatic property cluster kinds and accommodation (Richard Boyd)
Boyd's is the volume's fully developed alternative. On his accommodation theory, classificatory practices are epistemically reliable insofar as they are *accommodated* to the causal structures of their subject matter — and some kinds are defined not by an essence but by a "naturally occurring clustering of properties", with the consequences that (1) the kind lacks precisely defined membership conditions and sometimes (2) the properties in the defining cluster vary over time and space.

**Canonical argument** (source: Boyd, "Realism, Natural Kinds, and Philosophical Methods", book 013 ch. 12):
- P1. Biological species are paradigmatic natural kinds, yet evolutionary theory entails that they will ordinarily lack completely determinate boundaries; any precisified definition would misrepresent biological reality and undermine accommodation.
- P2. The vagueness of an HPC kind term's extension therefore reflects not imprecision but a *precise accommodation* of classification to causal phenomena.
- ∴ C. A kind can be natural without an essence or sharp boundaries; and naturalness is discipline-relative — there are no kinds natural *simpliciter*, only kinds natural with respect to the inferential architecture of a particular discipline.

**Objections** — from the essentialist side (as the Introduction frames the dispute): if naturalness is discipline-relative and degree-like, the metaphysically distinctive category the essentialist wanted has been given up, not explained. Boyd accepts the consequence and draws the metaphilosophical moral instead: epistemology and the theory of kinds are themselves empirical, anti-foundationalist inquiries. His §4.1 adds the deliberately provocative corollary that "natural kinds are social constructions" — Locke's "workmanship of men" — while insisting (§4.2) that this is compatible with their reality and mind-independence, since the workmanship succeeds only when accommodated to causal structure.

### Similarity and projectibility (Quine, as the Introduction presents him)
Quine claimed that kindhood and similarity are "variations or adaptations of a single notion", but that *theoretical* standards of similarity lend themselves to better inductions: "by primitive standards the marsupial mouse is more similar to the ordinary mouse than to the kangaroo; by theoretical standards the reverse is true", and cosmically "colours would not qualify as kinds".

**Canonical argument** (source: Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary, Introduction, book 013 ch. 1, presenting Quine 1969 — labelled formalization):
- P1. A kind term earns its keep by supporting inductions: from something's being a member, much besides membership should be inferable (the invented kind "narg" — objects bigger than a car — supports almost nothing).
- P2. Which similarities count is fixed not by our innate quality space but by mature theory.
- ∴ C. Natural kinds are those groupings that align with theoretical standards of similarity and so deliver superior inductive and explanatory success.

**Replies / limits** — this is explicitly *not* Quine's own resting point: he held that maturity in a science is marked by its no longer needing "an irreducible notion of similarity and kind" at all. And the editors note the pressure this account puts on the essentialist (success comes in degrees), which is precisely what drives Ellis to his a priori criteria above.

## Thought experiments
- **"Nargs"** (Introduction, ch. 1). Coin a simple word for *objects bigger than a car* — buses, elephants, office blocks. The term is semantically simple, yet the grouping supports no inductions beyond a size bound. Meant to show: naturalness is not semantic simplicity, and a real kind must underwrite inference and explanation. Pressures purely linguistic criteria of kindhood.
- **The marsupial mouse** (Quine, via Introduction, ch. 1). By surface similarity the marsupial mouse belongs with mice; by theoretical (evolutionary) standards it belongs with kangaroos. Meant to show that the similarity natural kinds track is theory-mediated. Pressures common-sense classification.

## Where the debate stands
Nothing in this volume is settled, and the volume is built to display that. The editors' own framing leaves three live options standing: essentialism with a priori criteria (which buys metaphysical distinctiveness at the price of excluding species and crosscutting chemical kinds), Boyd's accommodationist HPC account (which keeps species and vagueness at the price of discipline-relativity), and the semantic route — settle what natural kind *terms* are first — which the next entry shows collapsing into disagreement of its own. Tobin's chapter (see Connections) removes one apparently safe assumption shared by several parties: that genuine kinds must nest in a single hierarchy.
