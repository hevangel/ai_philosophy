# Essences and Theoretical Identities
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: natural kind essentialism; discovered vs stipulated identities*

## The question
"Water is H₂O"; "gold is the element with atomic number 79". The Kripke–Putnam tradition says such theoretical identity statements report *discoveries*: scientists find the essences kinds had all along, and the terms meant those kinds from the start. Or is the truth less tidy — do scientists, faced with disrupted concepts, *decide* how the old terms will be used, so that the identities become true by stipulative precisification rather than being found true? Three chapters of book 013 stage this as a direct dispute: LaPorte against the tradition, Bird against LaPorte, Hendry testing both against the actual history of chemistry.

## The 90-second version
Putnam's Twin Earth makes the essentialist intuition vivid. Imagine, before anyone knew water's chemistry, a planet exactly like Earth except that its watery liquid is not H₂O but a different compound, XYZ — while looking, tasting, and behaving just like water. Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule twin on Twin Earth have identical brains and use the word "water" identically, yet Oscar refers to H₂O and Twin Oscar to XYZ, because Oscar has only ever encountered H₂O and Twin Oscar only XYZ. So meaning "just ain't in the head": what a kind term refers to is fixed partly by the world, and — the essentialist adds — by the underlying nature of the samples speakers originally encountered. Whether science then *discovers* essences, or *decides* them, is what the positions below fight over. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [Twin Earth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KE8NL9G_Fq8).)

## Positions

### The Kripke–Putnam tradition (as LaPorte states it, in order to answer it)
"So-called 'natural kind terms' that were coined before much science was known, like 'oak', 'water', and 'mammal', refer to kinds with theoretically interesting essences"; scientists learn those essences empirically and state them in theoretical identities, the paradigm being "water = H₂O"; and these are "discoveries, not stipulations" — "scientific discoveries of species essence do not constitute a 'change of meaning'; the possibility of such discoveries was part of the original enterprise" (Kripke, as quoted by LaPorte).

**Canonical argument** (source: LaPorte, "Theoretical Identity Statements, Their Truth, and Their Discovery", book 013 ch. 6, §1 — the tradition in LaPorte's own summary; labelled formalization):
- P1. A kind term's reference is fixed by the samples its speakers originally encountered, not by the descriptions they associated with it.
- P2. Those samples have an underlying nature (an essence) which empirical science can subsequently uncover.
- ∴ C. A theoretical identity such as "water = H₂O" states what the term referred to all along: it is discovered to be true, not made true.

### LaPorte: true, perhaps — but not discovered
LaPorte's verdict is deliberately mixed: usual theoretical identity statements "(i) are not *discovered* to be true, but, at least sometimes, (ii) they *are* true". Essences are not the problem — on cladistic systematics, *Mammalia* is the clade consisting of the nearest common ancestor of horses and echidnas ("M") plus all and only M's descendants, in every possible world, and *Chordata* likewise. The problem is the discovery claim.

**Canonical argument** (source: LaPorte, book 013 ch. 6, §1):
- P1. Kind terms have *open texture*: vernacular use leaves boundary cases undetermined, so more than one precisification is consistent with everything earlier speakers meant.
- P2. When science disrupts a concept, different responses are each acceptable and none is required by prescientific use: we kept "whales are not fish" but reversed "birds are not dinosaurs" on relevantly similar evidence; scientists also disagree among themselves about what delimits a taxon (interbreeding vs common descent), and even given one agreed characterization, the *scope* of a kind (a stem population and its descendants, or something broader/narrower) must still be fixed by stipulation.
- ∴ C. Scientists change the meanings of kind terms by stipulatively deciding how to use them in response to conceptual disruption; essence-stating identities are not discovered to have been true all along.

### Bird: discovery defended
Bird accepts LaPorte's framework — precisification divides a concept's undetermined boundary region — and denies his cases meet its conditions.

**Canonical argument** (source: Bird, "Discovering the Essences of Natural Kinds", book 013 ch. 7):
- P1. A precisification may divide the boundary of the old concept anywhere; but some of LaPorte's cases are not precisifications at all, because the old concept itself already determined the disputed cases. In particular, by the division of linguistic labour it is *chemists'* facts that determine the identity of substances; chemical facts class D₂O (heavy water) with the other isotopic variants of H₂O — same structure, same reactions, differing only quantitatively (reaction rates) — so "water" already included D₂O, and excluding it would have been a conceptual *shift*, not a permitted precisification.
- P2. Many essential truths are not identities at all, and LaPorte's stipulation story does not account for them; and there are identities between two *scientific* terms, neither vernacular nor highly vague — elements named in the nineteenth century before atomic structure was understood — where the later identity plainly extends knowledge rather than recording a decision.
- ∴ C. "There remains considerable scope for essentialists to maintain that many essentialist truths, both those asserting essentially necessary conditions and those asserting identities, are not stipulated but are discovered *a posteriori*."

### Hendry: the chemist's caution
Hendry tests the dispute on the elements, the essentialist's best case, and splits the difference in a specific place: the *inference* from necessity to essence.

**Position** (source: Hendry, "The Elements and Conceptual Change", book 013 ch. 8, §5): it is a fallacy to *deduce* essence from metaphysical necessity — Fine's singleton set necessarily contains its member and the member necessarily belongs to the set, yet the set's identity depends on the member's, not vice versa, so necessity alone establishes no priority. But necessity can be *evidence* within an explanatory argument. Chemical practice itself holds water's composition fixed in counternomic explanation (why water's boiling point is "unexpectedly high": hydrogen bonding, assessed against what would happen were it absent, with nuclear charges and electron counts kept fixed); and nuclear charge is the kind of property that can ground essence — causally important across contexts, and explaining why the elements are countable rather than a continuum. Lavoisier, on Hendry's account, could refer to populations of atoms of nuclear charge eight without knowing it, because element names were grounded in the actual samples chemists studied.

**Objections** — Hendry also records the standing worries about the whole project: that essentialism is "smuggled in" to allegedly purely semantic premises about rigidity (Salmon's objection), and that Twin Earth elicits divergent intuitions — some judge XYZ is not water because it is not H₂O, others, just as surely, that it *is* water because it is colourless, tasteless, and boils at 100 °C.

## Thought experiments
- **Twin Earth** (Putnam 1973/1975; lecture above; used by every party). Earth Oscar / Twin Oscar, H₂O / XYZ, pre-chemistry. Meant by Putnam to show reference is world-involving; used by the tradition to support essences; used by LaPorte (rerun with D₂O) to argue the concept leaves the verdict open; answered by Bird via the division of linguistic labour. Pressures descriptivism and internalism about meaning.
- **Whales, fish, and birds that are dinosaurs** (LaPorte, ch. 6). Two reversals handled oppositely on similar evidence. Meant to show no unique response to conceptual disruption is dictated by earlier use. Pressures the discovery picture specifically.
- **Fool's water** (Kripke, via Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary, ch. 9). "Just as there is a fool's gold there could be a fool's water": a substance with a completely different atomic structure that resembles water in feel, appearance, and taste. Kripke's judgement: not water. This intuition is the load-bearing premise for the necessity of "water is H₂O" — see [The Necessary A Posteriori](the-necessary-a-posteriori.md).

## Where the debate stands
The volume leaves the dispute exactly where its three chapters leave it. All three parties accept that some theoretical identities are true and that kinds such as clades can be given essence-like specifications. They divide on the *epistemology of the identity statements*: stipulative precisification of open-textured concepts (LaPorte), discovery constrained by the division of linguistic labour and by non-identity essential truths (Bird), or — for chemistry — discovery of reference across conceptual change, with essence following from explanatory practice rather than from necessity alone (Hendry). No chapter claims the others are refuted; Bird's conclusion is explicitly a claim about "considerable scope" for essentialism, not a demonstration of it.
