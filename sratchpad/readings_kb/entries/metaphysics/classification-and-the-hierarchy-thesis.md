# Classification and the Hierarchy Thesis
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: crosscutting kinds; the no-overlap principle*

## The question
The Linnaean picture is seductively tidy: species nest in genera, genera in families, and so on up to kingdoms, so that whenever two kinds overlap, one contains the other. The hierarchy thesis says real natural kinds must form such a single nested hierarchy. But actual scientific classification, in biology *and* in chemistry, is full of kinds that overlap without nesting — *crosscutting* kinds. Does that refute realism about natural kinds, show the classifications are merely conventional, or only show that the hierarchy requirement should be dropped?

## The 90-second version
*No lecture in the corpus covers this problem; this version is distilled from the textbook (Tobin, book 013, ch. 10) and marked as such.* Kinds crosscut when they overlap but neither contains the other. Humans and dogs are both mammals; dogs and crocodiles are both quadrupeds; but humans and crocodiles share neither kind — so "quadrupeds" and "mammals" overlap in dogs without nesting. Chemistry, the paradigm home of natural kinds, does it too: albumin and renin are both proteins; renin and the hairpin ribozyme are both enzymes; but albumin and the hairpin ribozyme are neither both proteins nor both enzymes — enzymes are not a subkind of proteins, nor proteins of enzymes. The hierarchy thesis therefore fails as a description of scientific taxonomy. Tobin's question is what follows: Khalidi and Dupré say, reject the thesis; Hacking says, further, that kind distinctions are merely conventional; Ellis-style realists say crosscutting categories cannot be real kinds at all. Tobin defends the first answer against the second and third: crosscutting refutes the hierarchy thesis, not realism.

## Positions

### The hierarchy thesis (Ellis, Kuhn — as Tobin presents them)
Natural kinds form a nested hierarchy: if any two kinds overlap, one is subsumed under the other as a subkind. Ellis builds this into his criteria for natural kindhood (criterion 5 in the editors' list — see [Natural Kinds](natural-kinds.md)); Kuhn's version is the *no-overlap principle*, motivated semantically: real kind terms in a taxonomy must not overlap except as genus to species — "there is no gold that is also silver, and that is what makes the terms 'silver' and 'gold' kind terms" — and scientific revolutions are marked precisely by taxonomies that cross-classify the same things.

**Canonical argument** (source: Tobin, "Crosscutting Natural Kinds and the Hierarchy Thesis", book 013 ch. 10, §§1–2 — the thesis in Tobin's statement; labelled formalization):
- P1. A taxonomy of real natural kinds carves nature at its joints; a joint-carving classification cannot leave two legitimate kinds partially overlapping, since neither would mark the single natural division.
- P2. Paradigm taxonomies take the hierarchical form: Linnaean ranks (species → genus → family → order → class → phylum → kingdom), and in chemistry the periodic system, where sodium nests under alkali metals under metals.
- ∴ C. Where apparent kinds crosscut, at least one of them is not a genuine natural kind.

### Reject the thesis, keep the kinds (Tobin, with Khalidi and Dupré)
**Canonical argument** (source: Tobin, book 013 ch. 10):
- P1. Crosscutting categories are not marginal or disreputable: the protein/enzyme case occurs in chemistry itself, the essentialist's paradigm science, and the classifications involved "would appear to be more than merely conventional".
- P2. If crosscutting entailed conventionalism, these well-confirmed chemical classifications would have to be dismissed as mere convention — the Hacking inference Tobin rejects (*pace* Hacking 2007, rejecting the hierarchy thesis "does not entail conventionalism about natural kinds").
- ∴ C. "Crosscutting categories in science entail the rejection of the hierarchy thesis" — and metaphysical accounts of natural kinds must be rebuilt to *allow* crosscutting categories, not to explain them away.

### Pluralism and the species-as-individuals response (Kitcher, Dupré, Ereshefsky — as Tobin reports them)
Crosscutting in biological taxonomy is often taken as evidence that species should be construed as *individuals* rather than kinds, and more generally as support for pluralism: different, equally legitimate classifications answer to different interests and branches of inquiry, with no single taxonomy privileged. Tobin groups this with her own conclusion on the hierarchy thesis while keeping the question of what crosscutting shows about realism separate.

**Objections — the realist's dilemma (as Tobin states the consensus she is answering)**. Before her chapter, the shared assumption across otherwise opposed views was that "cases of crosscutting seriously jeopardize the view that natural kinds can be construed realistically": realists (Ellis) protect realism by denying crosscutting categories are kinds; conventionalists (Hacking) protect the classifications by denying realism. Tobin's chapter is precisely the refusal of that shared assumption.

## Thought experiments
There is no single thought experiment driving this problem; its work is done by actual taxonomies. The two load-bearing cases, both from Tobin ch. 10:
- **Mammals and quadrupeds** (biology). Dogs belong to both; humans only to the first; crocodiles only to the second. On the hierarchy thesis, "quadrupeds" must be rejected as a legitimate kind category (Khalidi, as Tobin cites him).
- **Proteins and enzymes** (chemistry). Albumin is a protein but not an enzyme; the hairpin ribozyme is an enzyme but not a protein; renin is both. Because this crosscut occurs among molecular kinds individuated by structure and function, it cannot be waved away as biological untidiness — it strikes the paradigm case for the hierarchy thesis (Ellis's criterion 5 fails even for chemistry).

## Where the debate stands
Tobin reports a consensus that crosscutting is a serious problem for *realism construed hierarchically*, and her chapter argues the consensus mislocates the damage: what falls is the hierarchy thesis (and with it Ellis's criterion 5 and Kuhn's no-overlap principle as a constraint on real kinds), not the reality of the kinds themselves. The volume offers no reply chapter; Boyd's discipline-relative, cluster-based account (see Connections) is the one position in the book that accommodates crosscutting without strain, since HPC kinds were never required to nest.
