# Causal Necessity and Hume's Dictum
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: Humeanism vs non-Humeanism; constitutional vs causal necessity*

## The question
Humeans and non-Humeans agree on a surprising amount: there can be necessary connections between things that are not wholly distinct — a set and its members, a whole and its parts, a kind and the intrinsic properties essential to its members. They divide over one line: Hume's Dictum, that there are *no* necessary connections between wholly distinct entities — in particular, no necessary *causal* connections, so that a cause, even with all background conditions in place, could always have failed to produce its effect. Jessica Wilson's chapter in book 013 argues the Humean's line is unprincipled: accept the constitutional necessities, and you should accept certain causal necessities too.

## The 90-second version
*No lecture in the corpus covers this problem; this version is distilled from the textbook (Wilson, book 013, ch. 11) and marked as such.* Everyone in the debate accepts some necessities: water's identity with H₂O, sets being constituted by their members, fusions by their parts. These are *constitutional* necessities — the connected entities partly constitute each other, so they are not wholly distinct. Hume's Dictum permits exactly those, while forbidding necessary connections between wholly distinct things — paradigmatically causes and their separate effects. Wilson's move is a conditional: *if* you accept certain constitutional necessities, *then* consistency requires accepting certain causal necessities. Her lever is the *warrant*: the proof that identities are necessary is a special, compelling, broadly logical proof, but no such proof exists for the other constitutional necessities Humeans happily accept — sets, fusions, determinates and determinables. Those beliefs rest on the same softer warrants (systematization of beliefs, contemplation of hypothetical scenarios, inference to the best explanation) that are available in the causal case. So the Humean can keep the Dictum only at, in Wilson's phrase, costs of "implausibility and ad hocery" — and the non-Humean framework, which feels no tension here, comes out ahead.

## Positions

### Humeanism under Hume's Dictum
**Canonical statement** (source: Wilson, "From Constitutional Necessities to Causal Necessities", book 013 ch. 11, opening):
- P1. There may be necessary connections between entities that are identical (Hesperus/Phosphorus, water/H₂O) or merely partly distinct — sets and their members, fusions and their parts, determinates and determinables, members of certain natural kinds and certain of their intrinsic properties, and (for physicalists) certain physical and mental states.
- P2. (Hume's Dictum) There are no necessary connections between entities that are *wholly* distinct — in particular no necessary causal connections, even when the background conditions requisite for causation are in place.
- ∴ C. The differential treatment is principled: commonly accepted necessary connections involve constitutional relations (existential ontological dependence), whereas causes and effects do not constitute each other.

Wilson's chapter is the argument that P2's principled appearance is "not genuine".

### Schaffer's principled basis — and its limit
Jonathan Schaffer (as Wilson presents him) tries to ground the differential treatment epistemically: intuitions of causal contingency ("like charges might have attracted") should be trusted, unlike intuitions that identities are contingent, because for identity there is an *independent reason* — a compelling, broadly logical proof of the necessity of identity — to think our contingency intuitions misdescribe the case (Shoemaker's Kripkean redescription strategy for causation is thereby rejected: the law that strychnine is fatal may be *imagined* to fail only because we imagine something passing the tests for strychnine, or for a human being, rather than the real thing surviving).

**Wilson's objection** (source: Wilson, book 013 ch. 11, §1.1):
- P1. Schaffer's compelling-proof criterion warrants accepting necessities of *identity* while rejecting causal necessities.
- P2. But Humeans and non-Humeans alike also accept constitutional necessities between non-identical entities — set membership, parthood, the determinable/determinate relation, essential instantiation, realization — and for none of these is there any compelling proof: set theory is silent on the modal individuation of sets, mereology's axioms do not yield that fusions are necessarily constituted by their parts, and for several such relations there are not even candidate axioms.
- ∴ C. The criterion "fails to warrant acceptance of many constitutional necessities that Humeans and non-Humeans alike commonly and reasonably accept" — so it cannot be what entitles the Humean to the differential treatment.

### Wilson's conditional: Constitutional → Causal
**Canonical argument** (source: Wilson, book 013 ch. 11, introduction and §§1–3):
- P1. The warrants actually supporting the accepted constitutional necessities are broadly non-logical: "systematization of relevant beliefs, contemplation of hypothetical scenarios, transcendental arguments, or… inference to the best explanation" of experiential, semantic, scientific, and philosophical facts — convincing, often, but not the sort to *compel* assent as a logical proof does.
- P2. Warrants of exactly those kinds are available for certain causal necessities; there is no difference in the *type or strength of warrant* that maps onto the constitutional/causal line.
- ∴ C. *Constitutional → Causal*: if one accepts certain constitutional necessities, one should accept certain causal necessities. The Humean can resist only at attendant costs of implausibility and ad hocery; the non-Humean framework faces no such tension and "in this respect comes out ahead".

**Scope note (the author's own)**: the argument is presented as leverage for assessing "the two main frameworks in the metaphysics of science" precisely because direct criticism of either framework has tended to presuppose a stance on the Dictum and so talk past the other side; Wilson's arguments instead rest on "general and independent considerations about which facts in the world might plausibly warrant our beliefs in constitutional necessities". The conclusion is conditional and comparative — it does not, by itself, assert any particular causal necessity.

## Thought experiments
This problem is driven not by a thought experiment but by a pair of matched intuitions, both from Wilson ch. 11:
- **Like charges attracting.** Hume and Schaffer intuit that actual causes might have had different effects — that it is possible for like charges to attract, even with the background conditions for repulsion in place. The contingency intuition is the Humean's datum.
- **Strychnine survived** (Shoemaker). Imagine ingesting vast amounts of what passes every test for strychnine and surviving: Shoemaker says you have imagined test-passing stuff, not strychnine — the same redescription Kripke licenses for "water might not have been H₂O". Whether the redescription is legitimate *here* is exactly what is disputed: Schaffer says no independent reason compels it; Wilson's reply is that the compulsion standard, applied evenly, would also dissolve most constitutional necessities.

## Where the debate stands
Wilson's verdict is explicit but bounded: on the warrant question, the non-Humean framework "comes out ahead", and the Humean's remaining options carry costs of implausibility and ad hocery — costs she argues for, in the chapter, rather than merely naming. Hume's Dictum itself is not refuted: the conclusion is a conditional about consistency of commitments, addressed to theorists who already accept the constitutional necessities. The volume contains no Humean reply chapter.
