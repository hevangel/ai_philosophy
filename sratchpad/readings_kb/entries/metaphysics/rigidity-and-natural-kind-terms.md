# Rigidity and Natural Kind Terms
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: natural kind semantics; general terms as designators*

## The question
Proper names, Kripke argued, are rigid designators: they pick out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists, and they do so directly, not via a description. Are natural kind terms — "water", "gold", "tiger" — a parallel, semantically distinctive class of *general* terms? Four consecutive chapters of book 013 give four different answers, and the disagreement is not over details: it is over whether there is a nontrivial phenomenon here at all.

## The 90-second version
A rigid designator refers to the same object in all possible worlds where that object exists. "Saul Kripke" refers to Kripke whether he is a philosopher in this world, a wizard in another, or a lawyer in a third; by contrast "the 44th President of the United States" picks out different people in different worlds and is non-rigid — although some descriptions, like "the predecessor of 3", rigidly designate (the number 2) in every world. The distinction was introduced for singular terms such as names. Everything in this entry is about whether it survives the move to general terms. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [What is a Rigid Designator?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h0GNeCRdx9s).)

## Positions

### De jure obstinate rigidity (Corine Besson)
The starting problem is *trivialization*: extended to general terms, Kripke's definition makes natural kind terms rigid — but so does it make all other general terms, including descriptive ones, rigid. Besson's remedy relocates the distinction from semantics to metasemantics.

**Canonical argument** (source: Besson, "Rigidity, Natural Kind Terms, and Metasemantics", book 013 ch. 2):
- P1. (Kripke Rigidity) A rigid designator designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and nothing in worlds where it does not exist; on this definition, applied to general terms, natural kind terms and descriptive general terms alike come out rigid — the distinction trivializes.
- P2. Natural kind terms are introduced by "dubbing" — name-acquiring transactions subject to clearly defined metasemantic conditions that descriptive general terms cannot satisfy — and their rigidity is therefore obstinacy *de jure* (by stipulation in the dubbing), not merely *de facto*.
- ∴ C. Natural kind terms are de jure obstinately rigid designators, and other general terms are not: the asymmetry between the classes is real, but grounded in how the terms are introduced, not in their truth-conditional behaviour alone.

### The View defended — and the bullet bitten (Genoveva Martí & José Martínez-Fernández)
"The View" holds that general terms designate universals — kinds, colours, species — as singular terms designate individuals. Against it stand the trivialization problem, the overgeneralization problem, and the charge that it cannot explain the necessity of theoretical identifications.

**Canonical argument** (source: Martí & Martínez-Fernández, "General Terms as Designators", book 013 ch. 3 — as summarized in the editors' Introduction and stated in the chapter):
- P1. The trivialization problem alleges no difference in truth conditions between rigid and non-rigid readings of a general term used as a predicate; but there are cases where the contribution to truth conditions does differ between the readings.
- P2. The overgeneralization problem alleges that if "water" rigidly designates the water-kind, "fridge" equally designates the refrigerator-kind; the correct response is to accept the symmetry — "there is just no interesting semantic distinction" between simple general terms such as "philosopher" and "tungsten".
- P3. Theoretical identities are claims about *kinds*, not universally quantified biconditionals about the objects falling under kind predicates; distinguishing a term's denoting (kind) use from its predicative (exemplifier) use explains why true theoretical identities are necessary.
- ∴ C. The View survives all three objections — but at the cost, cheerfully paid, of denying that natural kind terms are semantically set apart from other simple general terms.

**Note the tension**: Besson and Martí & Martínez-Fernández both claim to save rigidity for general terms, but Besson saves it by making natural kind terms special (metasemantically) and Martí & Martínez-Fernández save it by making them *not* special (semantically uniform with the rest). The volume does not adjudicate between them.

### Natural kind terms are not special (Åsa Wikforss)
Wikforss rejects the Kripke–Putnam account in favour of a version of descriptivism, on grounds that begin one step earlier than the others: we cannot even identify the alleged class without doing empirical science.

**Canonical argument** (source: Wikforss, "Are Natural Kind Terms Special?", book 013 ch. 4):
- P1. A semantic category should not depend for its membership on sophisticated empirical theory; yet identifying natural kind terms — on the microstructural conception, whether a term picks out a kind with a unifying microstructural essence — does: witness the failures, "jade", "sugar", "air", "sand", and many plant, animal, and disease terms, where no such property exists.
- P2. Neither of the two leading semantic marks works: rigidity does not single the terms out, and neither does non-descriptionality; nor does externalism about how meaning is determined rescue a special *semantic* content.
- ∴ C. Natural kind terms have no distinctive semantics or metasemantics. "Any special significance they have is a product of the role they play in scientific explanation" (the editors' summary).

### A Fregean reconciliation (Harold Noonan)
Noonan, unconvinced by Kripke's arguments against Frege–Russell, asks how the commonalities between names and kind terms can be explained *within* a Fregean account: via (Fregean) rigid designation, deferential intentions in propositional attitudes, and a Fregean reading of Twin Earth.

**Position in brief** (source: Noonan, "The Commonalities between Proper Names and Natural Kind Terms", book 013 ch. 5): the modal argument does not force Millian direct reference; the phenomena Kripke collects can be accommodated with sense as well as reference. Noonan also dismisses the necessary a posteriori for kind terms — on his view it rests on conflating an essential property with an a posteriori but contingently true thought (developed in [The Necessary A Posteriori](the-necessary-a-posteriori.md)).

## Thought experiments
- **The rigidified description** (Besson, ch. 2). "The *actual* pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander" designates Aristotle in every world — a description can be made rigid by "actual", and descriptions of necessary existents ("the smallest prime") are rigid outright. Meant to show: bare rigidity is too cheap to mark off names, let alone kind terms; the interesting question is always *why* a term is rigid. Pressures every purely semantic criterion in this entry.
- **Twin Earth** (Putnam; Noonan ch. 5, §3). See [Essences and Theoretical Identities](essences-and-theoretical-identities.md), where it does its main work; Noonan's chapter is the volume's attempt to show a Fregean can absorb it.

## Where the debate stands
Unresolved, four ways. All parties grant the trivialization problem is real for a naive extension of Kripke's definition. They divide on the remedy: metasemantic specialness (Besson), semantic uniformity (Martí & Martínez-Fernández), descriptivism and no special category (Wikforss), or Fregean sense (Noonan). The editors' own closing questions for the section stand as the honest summary: is there a nontrivial phenomenon of rigidity for kind terms at all — and if so, does it apply to the terms denoting the metaphysician's genuine natural kinds?
