# The Absurd and Sisyphus
*Subfield: History of Philosophy & General (Existentialism) · Also known as: absurdism; the absurd hero*

## The question
Suppose the world offers no built-in meaning: the gods' sentence on Sisyphus — rolling a rock up a mountain forever, watching it fall back, forever — is the honest picture of a human life, work repeated daily toward nothing final. Is such a life only a punishment, bearable by not thinking about it? Or can consciousness of the futility itself become a kind of victory — can Sisyphus, knowing everything, be imagined *happy*? The corpus contains Camus' answer in exactly one place: the concluding chapter of *The Myth of Sisyphus*, anthologized as the finale of book 018. The earlier chapters of that book, where Camus defines the absurd and argues that suicide is "the one truly serious philosophical problem," are **not in the corpus** — so this entry presents the answer the finale gives, and declares the missing argument rather than reconstructing it from outside knowledge.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus treats Camus or the absurd, so this version is distilled from the textbook text itself (book 018, ch. TEN) and is marked accordingly.* The gods thought there was "no more dreadful punishment than futile and hopeless labor." Camus looks instead at the moment of descent, when Sisyphus walks back down to his rock: "that hour like a breathing-space which returns as surely as his suffering, that is the hour of consciousness." A fate fully known is a fate partly owned: "If this myth is tragic, that is because its hero is conscious… The lucidity that was to constitute his torture at the same time crowns his victory. There is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn." The modern workman lives the same repetition, tragic only at the rare moments it becomes conscious. Sisyphus' joy is not hope — nothing will change — but ownership: "His fate belongs to him. His rock is his thing." The essay ends on its famous instruction: "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

## Positions

### The absurd hero: lucidity and scorn (Camus)
Sisyphus is "the absurd hero… as much through his passions as through his torture. His scorn of the gods, his hatred of death, and his passion for life won him that unspeakable penalty in which the whole being is exerted toward accomplishing nothing."

**Canonical argument** (source: Camus, "The Myth of Sisyphus," concluding chapter, book 018, ch. TEN — a formalization of the essay's reasoning about the descent):
- P1. The torture of the sentence depends on unconsciousness or hope: "Where would his torture be, indeed, if at every step the hope of succeeding upheld him?" — and the labourer's fate "is tragic only at the rare moments when it becomes conscious."
- P2. In the descent Sisyphus is fully conscious — he "knows the whole extent of his wretched condition" — and consciousness reverses the relation to the fate: at each such moment "he is superior to his fate. He is stronger than his rock."
- ∴ C. A fate seen whole can be surmounted by scorn rather than endured in ignorance: "There is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn," and Sisyphus' "silent joy" is that the fate becomes his own — "His fate belongs to him. His rock is his thing."

**The Oedipus parallel** (same source) — Camus generalizes the pattern beyond the myth: Oedipus "at the outset obeys fate without knowing it. But from the moment he knows, his tragedy begins," and knowing ends in the remark Camus calls sacred: "Despite so many ordeals, my advanced age and the nobility of my soul make me conclude that all is well." Hence the essay's yoking: "Happiness and the absurd are two sons of the same earth. They are inseparable" — with the correction that neither simply causes the other: "It happens as well that the feeling of the absurd springs from happiness."

**What the response is not** — the essay's scorn is directed at escapes: Sisyphus "drives out of this world a god who had come into it with dissatisfaction and a preference for futile sufferings," and "makes of fate a human matter, which must be settled among men." The absurd man "says yes and his effort will henceforth be unceasing" — no appeal, no higher destiny ("if there is a personal fate, there is no higher destiny, or at least there is but one which he concludes is inevitable and despicable").

### The framing caution: is this existentialism at all? (Kaufmann)
The anthology places Camus last but flags the placement in its preface: Camus "is often called an existentialist, though many critics insist that this is an invidious error," and the two men it links him to — he "was close to Sartre at one time, but the two men broke after Sartre decided to make common cause with the Communist Party in France." Within this corpus, then, "the absurd" is filed under existentialism by editorial decision, under protest recorded on the same page.

**Objections** — the corpus's copy of the essay is the finale only, so the objections Camus himself spends his book answering (above all: why not suicide, if life is absurd?) are present in the corpus only as the pressure this chapter answers, not as arguments that can be stated. The strongest internal pressure is visible anyway: the conclusion is an *instruction to imagine* ("One must imagine Sisyphus happy"), not a demonstration — the essay shows a stance being taken toward a fate whose absurdity it assumes from its missing earlier chapters.

## Thought experiments
- **Sisyphus** (Camus, the essay itself). The whole entry is driven by this one image, used precisely: not the climb but the *descent*, the pause in which consciousness returns. What it is meant to show: that the difference between punishment and victory can lie entirely in whether the labourer knows his condition whole. Which positions it pressures: any view that makes meaning depend on outcome or progress — and any view that treats awareness of futility as pure loss.
- **The workman of today** (Camus, same passage). "The workman of today works every day in his life at the same tasks, and this fate is no less absurd." The myth is not an antique curiosity but a template for ordinary repetitive life — which is why the essay's question survives translation out of the underworld.

## Where the debate stands
On the ground this corpus actually contains, nothing is debated: there is one text, and it concludes rather than argues. What the corpus can honestly report is the shape of the answer — lucidity instead of hope, scorn instead of appeal, ownership of the fate instead of meaning imposed on it — and two open seams: the missing foundation (the definition of the absurd and the reply to suicide are absent), and the disputed label (whether this belongs under "existentialism" at all). Sartre's neighbouring answer — invent man, choose for all — is a genuine alternative staged in the same volume, and the two are best read as rivals, not as chapters of one doctrine.
