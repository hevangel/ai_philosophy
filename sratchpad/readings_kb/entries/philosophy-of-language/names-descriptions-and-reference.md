# Names, Descriptions and Reference
*Subfield: Philosophy of Language · Also known as: theories of reference; denoting*

## The question
How does an expression latch onto an object? A name like "Saul Kripke" seems to point straight at a person. A description like "the 44th President of the United States" points by way of a condition — whoever satisfies it. An indexical like "I," "here" or "now" points only relative to who is speaking, where and when. These mechanisms look interchangeable in ordinary speech, and the classical puzzles of the philosophy of language arise exactly where they come apart: substitute one co-referring expression for another inside a belief report and a truth can turn false; ask what a description picks out in a world where things went differently and names and descriptions diverge; try to state the truth of "I am here now" without the context and the truth will not stay still. **A note on sources, before anything else:** this entry is lecture-led. The Carneades series covers reference richly; book 031 — an applied-linguistics volume — contains no theory of reference at all, and contributes only neighbouring material on how learners handle form–meaning relations, flagged as such where it appears. Nothing below presents book 031 as a semantics text.

## The 90-second version
Some expressions refer the same way in every situation; others borrow their reference from the situation. A definite description — "the tallest building in the world," "the largest two-digit prime number" — picks out whatever uniquely fits the condition, and Russell's proposal was that such phrases do not really name anything at all: "Yuri is the smartest" unpacks into the claim that for everyone, if they are the smartest, they are Yuri. Names behave differently. A rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world where that object exists: "Saul Kripke" refers to Kripke whether he is a philosopher, a wizard or a lawyer in that world, while "the 44th President" is a *flaccid* designator, free to pick out someone else in a world where the election went otherwise. Indexicals split the difference by tying reference to the occasion: "I," "here," "now," "yesterday" refer to different people, places and times depending on who utters them and when, so the sentence "I am standing" has no truth value until a context supplies one. Three devices, three behaviours — and the puzzles begin when a theory built for one device is applied to another. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is a Definite Description?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=btLfQX0PJ5k), [What is a Rigid Designator?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h0GNeCRdx9s), [What is a Flaccid Designator?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=txLEbIplCPQ), [What is an Indexical?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n-Mxfe6Yz6M).)

## Positions

### Russell: definite descriptions are not names (as the lecture presents him)
Descriptions only *appear* to behave like names; their logical form is quantificational, which dissolves the puzzles they generate (the lecture instances the riddle of negative existentials and the bald king of France).

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, "What is a Definite Description?" — a formalization of the analysis the lecture states):
- P1. A sentence of the form "A is *the* X" is true just in case: there is something that is X, and for every object B, if B is X then B is identical to A (the lecture's example: for all people, if someone is the smartest, that someone is Yuri).
- P2. So understood, the description contributes a uniqueness-and-identity condition, not a referring constituent that must have a bearer for the sentence to be meaningful.
- ∴ C. Definite descriptions have "a very different logical form" from names, despite surface appearances.

**Objections** — the lecture itself ends by putting the worry to the audience: is Russell's version "too revisionist"? The analysis trades a simple utterance for a much more complicated one, and whether the logical form "truly represent[s] what we mean" is left as an open question, not a verdict.

### Kripke: names are rigid, most descriptions are flaccid (as the lectures present him)
Reference across possible worlds separates the devices cleanly.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, "What is a Rigid Designator?" and "What is a Flaccid Designator?" — a formalization):
- P1. A rigid designator refers to the same object in all possible worlds in which that object exists; proper names (Saul Kripke, Barack Obama, Earth, China) are the paradigm — there is no world where China is not China, though plenty where China is not the most populous country.
- P2. Most definite descriptions are flaccid: "the most populous country on Earth in 2020," "the Queen of England," "the smallest star in the Milky Way" can pick out different objects in different worlds — even when they contain rigid parts (the Milky Way stays fixed while its smallest star varies). Descriptions go rigid only where logic or mathematics fixes the satisfier in every world ("the predecessor of 3" always picks out 2; "the sum of four and five" always picks out 9).
- ∴ C. Names cannot be flaccid designators, and name–description equivalence in this world does not make the two devices the same device.

**Payoff — necessary truths known only by experience** (source: Carneades, "Kripke's Necessary, A Posteriori Statements" — a formalization):
- P1. If two rigid designators refer to the same object, they refer to it in every world where it exists, so the identity statement between them is true in all such worlds — necessary.
- P2. Hesperus and Phosphorus are both rigid designators (ancient names for the morning and evening appearances of Venus), yet that they co-refer was discovered by astronomers, not by reflection on the concepts — Lois Lane likewise needs empirical evidence that Superman is Clark Kent.
- ∴ C. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary and a posteriori — a combination the traditional alignment of necessary with a priori said should not exist. (The companion lecture, "Kripke's Contingent, A Priori Statements," runs the separation the other way with the standard-metre stick; both lectures flag their own pressure points — whether "here" really rigidly designates, whether the metre case is genuinely knowable without measurement — and leave them open.)

**Objections** — the Flaccid Designator lecture raises the border troubles itself: nickname-like names ("Al 'Scarface' Capone") seem partly descriptive and world-dependent; honorifics inside names ("Sir Terry Pratchett") are plainly contingent. Behind them sits the unanswered question of transworld identity — what makes the Obama who lost in one world the same person as the Obama who won in another, and what the criteria for successful cross-world identification by name even are. The lectures pose these; they do not resolve them.

### Indexicals: reference that will not leave the context behind
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, "What is an Indexical?" — a formalization):
- P1. Indexicals ("I," "here," "now," "you," "yesterday," "in an hour") take their reference from the context of utterance — who speaks, where, when, and for demonstratives ("this," "he" with a gesture) what the speaker is doing.
- P2. Their contribution cannot be eliminated by substituting a context-free equivalent: believing "the meeting is at 4:00 p.m." is compatible with not believing "the meeting is now," even when it is 4:00 p.m. — so "now" is not translatable away without loss.
- ∴ C. Sentences containing indexicals have no truth value independent of context, and indexicals resist reduction within a purely extensional logical framework.

The lecture refines the class: *pure* indexicals ("I," "here," "now") need only the context's coordinates, while *demonstratives* ("this," "that," "he/she" on an occasion) need a demonstration besides. It also notes — without developing — that indexicals behave badly inside belief contexts, which is the bridge to the next position.

### The attitude-reports puzzle: substitution fails where belief begins
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, "The Puzzle of Propositional Attitude Reports" — the puzzle stated as the lecture states it):
- P1. The principle of identity substitution: if a = b and a satisfies F, then b satisfies F. In extensional contexts it validates obviously good inferences — from "Kikuchi is tall" and "Kikuchi is Mr. Khalichi" to "Mr. Khalichi is tall"; from "the Morning Star is a planet" and "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" to "the Evening Star is a planet."
- P2. Inside propositional attitude reports — reports of a subject's belief, hope, desire or claim toward a proposition — the same substitutions fail: Victor may think Mark Twain is a good writer while having no thought that Samuel Clemens is; Tommy may say he wants to be Batman without saying he wants to be Bruce Wayne; Sanji may believe 90/9 is the answer without believing 10 is; and the speaker who claims to like eating Soylent Green does not thereby claim to like eating people.
- ∴ C. The principle holds in extensional contexts and breaks in *intensional* (attitude) contexts; what it is about those contexts that breaks it — a defect in the principle, or something about how attitudes individuate their objects — is the puzzle.

**The de re / de dicto distinction** (source: Carneades, "Semantically De Re and De Dicto"): one diagnostic tool the series offers is scope-like. "Ralph believes the man he is pointing at in the dark is a spy" is true *de dicto* only: substitute the co-referring "the mayor" (the man is Orchid, the mayor, though Ralph does not know it) and the belief report goes false. But "the mayor is such that Ralph believes of him that he is a spy" is true *de re* — true *of the man*, under any designation. The lecture presents the distinction as a way of marking where substitution is and is not licensed; it does not present it as a solution to the puzzle, and the series' promised Fregean and Russellian solutions are announced for later videos that are not part of what was read for this entry.

### What book 031 adds — neighbouring evidence, honestly labelled
Book 031 contains no position on reference. What it contains is three accounts of learners colliding with the fact that form–meaning links are not one-to-one — the developmental shadow of this entry's problem, included as anchoring context and nothing more. Kramsch (ch. 3) sets as the first question of the FLL research agenda how learners "acquire lexical meanings … and pass from one-to-one equivalence to multifunctionality within the L2 system of semantic relations" — a question posed, not answered. Andersen (ch. 5) gives the mechanism its names: learners begin under a **One-to-One Principle** (one intended meaning, one clear invariant surface form) and are driven by the **Multifunctionality Principle** toward forms that do many jobs at once — the learner's version of discovering that reference and meaning do not pair off neatly. Gass (ch. 4) adds the comprehension side: establishing the relations among a sentence's elements uses language-specific cues (English leans on word order — "The picture kisses the girl" can only be the picture kissing; Italian distributes the work across agreement, semantic and pragmatic factors), English learners of Italian differed sharply by environment on exactly this, and some such sensitivity may be unavailable from textbooks at all, only from ambient speech. None of this is a theory of reference; it is evidence that the devices this entry describes are not transparent to the people who must acquire them.

## Thought experiments
- **Hesperus and Phosphorus** (Kripke lectures). Two names, one planet, centuries of competent speakers who did not know it. If names referred *via* the descriptions speakers associate with them (the morning star / the evening star), the identity would be a confusion of two objects; because the names are rigid, it is instead a discovery about one. The case is the engine of the necessary-a-posteriori argument above.
- **Ralph, Orchid and the man in the dark** (De Re and De Dicto lecture). Ralph believes the man he points at in the dark is a spy; the man is Orchid, the mayor; Ralph also knows Orchid as the mayor and does not believe the mayor is a spy. One man, two designations, opposite belief verdicts — the cleanest exhibition in the read lectures of why attitude contexts are intensional, and of what the de re/de dicto distinction is for.
- **Superman and Clark Kent** (Necessary A Posteriori lecture; also the Attitude Reports lecture's Batman/Bruce Wayne case). The same co-reference does double duty in the series: as an identity between rigid designators it is necessary; as material inside Lois Lane's (or Tommy's) attitudes it is exactly what cannot be substituted. Holding both facts about one pair of names is the shortest statement of this entry's problem.

## Where the debate stands
As the read sources leave it: the distinctions are in good order and the puzzles are open. Russell's analysis of descriptions is presented with an explicit "too revisionist?" doubt attached; Kripke's rigidity is presented with its border cases (Scarface, honorifics, transworld identity) flagged by the lecturer himself; the attitude-reports puzzle is posed, the de re/de dicto distinction supplied as a diagnostic, and the Fregean and Russellian solutions explicitly deferred to videos outside this entry's reading. The corpus therefore supports the *statement* of the reference problem and its main devices, but no adjudication among theories of reference — and any entry claiming more would be writing from outside its sources. Book 031's contribution stands at the level of acquisition: speakers demonstrably begin by assuming one form per meaning and must learn their way out of it, which is a fact about us, not about reference itself.
