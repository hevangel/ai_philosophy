# Language as a Resource: Conservation and Policy
*Subfield: Philosophy of Language · Also known as: language conservation; additive vs subtractive bilingualism*

## The question
A five-year-old arrives at school already fluent in Korean, Spanish or another home language — years of acquisition, completed free of charge, to a level adult students rarely reach after thousands of classroom hours. What should the school system do with that competence? One answer treats the home language as an obstacle: schooling replaces it with the majority language, and the child's first language withers as the price of membership. The other treats it as a *resource* — in the ecological sense of the word: something valuable, already produced, that a foolish policy can squander and a wise one can conserve and develop. The question is therefore not only pedagogical but normative and political: is a minority language a private inheritance to be traded away, or a collective asset — the child's, the family's and the nation's — that policy should protect? Book 031 contains the corpus's one sustained treatment, Campbell & Lindholm's "Conservation of Language Resources," and it is deliberately one-sided: it argues the resource case against a subtractive default it describes but no chapter in the volume defends. This entry keeps that asymmetry visible.

## The 90-second version
*[Textbook-distilled — no Carneades lecture covers this problem; distilled from Campbell & Lindholm, ch. 16, of book 031.]* Picture a Korean-American five-year-old starting kindergarten in Los Angeles. She already speaks Korean the way native five-year-olds do: the sound system complete, nearly all the grammar for building sentences and holding conversations, and the social rules too — different vocabulary, honorifics and even body language for different listeners. On the US Government's proficiency scale she would rate, allowing for a child's vocabulary and illiteracy, around 3+ or 4. An adult at the Defense Language Institute needs some 1,400 hours of intensive instruction to reach a 2 or 2+ — routine social demands and limited work requirements. So the child walks in holding, at no public cost, what the state elsewhere pays thousands of hours to produce — and the system's normal response is to let it disappear, requiring her to give up the ancestral language as dues for joining the monolingual majority. The conservation proposal is simply to reverse the default: teach *through* both languages, so the home language keeps developing toward adult, literate proficiency while English is added — bilingualism as addition rather than replacement. Early results from programmes built that way show children gaining in both languages at once, which is exactly what the subtractive picture says should not happen.

## Positions

### The subtractive default (as Campbell & Lindholm characterize it)
The position under attack, stated in the form its critics give it — no chapter in book 031 argues it in its own voice.

**The default as characterized** (source: Campbell & Lindholm, ch. 16): despite periodic official alarm about national foreign-language deficits, actual practice amounts to "an unspoken national policy to do whatever is necessary to deny opportunities for the conservation of the competence held by these linguistic minority students"; the policy "is basically 'subtractive' in nature: we seem to insist that linguistic minority children give up their ancestral languages as dues if they wish to become members of the generally monolingual English-speaking majority club." Most bilingual education for minority children, the authors add, "seldom provide[s] for the development and maturation of adult-level linguistic competence in the children's L1."

**Objections** — the characterization is the objection's target as much as its statement: a reader should note that "unspoken policy" is an inference from outcomes (programmes that do not develop L1), not a documented intent, and the volume offers no defence of assimilationist schooling against which to test it. The entry therefore treats the subtractive picture as the foil the chapter constructs, and the conservation argument below as an argument against that foil.

### The conservation argument: the resource, its magnitude, and its waste
**Canonical argument** (source: Campbell & Lindholm, ch. 16 — a formalization of the chapter's opening case):
- P1. Linguistic-minority children enter school with substantial, already-acquired competence in a home language: by age five, complete phonology, nearly all morphology and syntax for sentence formation and discourse, and sociolinguistic rule-governed variation by interlocutor — rated, with the stated limits of child semantic networks and literacy, at 3+/4 on the Interagency Language Round Table scale, against the 2/2+ that roughly 1,400 hours of Defense Language Institute instruction produces in adults.
- P2. This competence was acquired "without the expenditure of community resources," while reproducing it through foreign-language instruction would cost thousands of hours per learner "at huge financial costs to taxpayers" — if the instructional technology to reach it existed at all.
- P3. Subtractive schooling does not merely neglect the resource but destroys it, and — on the bilingual-education literature the authors invoke (Cummins; Skutnabb-Kangas; and others cited in the chapter) — works against the children's general education and their progress in English itself, as well as against the national interest in foreign-language capacity.
- ∴ C. Policy should conserve and enhance the resource — priority to maintaining and developing the home language through schooling — rather than squander it.

### The additive models: bilingual immersion and its variants
Conservation is given institutional form: use both languages as media of instruction, with the home language developing alongside, not before, English.

**The elementary model** (source: Campbell & Lindholm, ch. 16): two-way bilingual immersion mixes native English speakers with native speakers of the partner language (roughly one-third / two-thirds), teaches mainly through the partner language in kindergarten–grade 2 (with a small daily English block), rising to 50% English by grades 4–6, never mixing languages within a period, and integrating content with language instruction. First-year evaluation (112 kindergarten and first-grade pupils in four California schools): all pupils gained in *both* languages (Spanish speakers' Spanish gains highly significant; English speakers gained English despite minimal English instruction), both groups scored average to above average on achievement tests, and content learned in Spanish was available when tested in English — the pattern the model's assumptions predict, and the reverse of the subtractive expectation that home-language instruction costs English or achievement.

**The secondary and university variants** (same source): at Mount Miguel High School, a bilingual partial-immersion programme streams heritage speakers (Spanish for Native Speakers) separately from second-language learners for a year, then teaches content jointly in Spanish; across two years, heritage speakers outscored the intensive second-language stream, which outscored traditional grammar-based Spanish classes, on listening, reading and writing — and heritage speakers' profile problem is the inverse of beginners': educated almost entirely in English, they hold more *academic* proficiency in English than in their native language. At UCLA, heritage Korean students with spoken competence take an intensive Seoul National University programme (30 hours a week for ten weeks, content courses in sheltered Korean, homestays) aimed at adult literacy; proficiency, self-esteem and ethnolinguistic loyalties were being monitored, with analysis incomplete when the chapter was written — the authors record optimism, not results.

**Objections — the authors' own** (same source): the three programmes "are in their infancy"; the heritage population has not been adequately studied by second-language researchers; "more information is needed about their process of Spanish language development"; and there is a "lack of tests that can adequately measure the Spanish language skills that these students possess." The positive findings are first-year and partly single-site; the university programme's case rests on design and expectation. The chapter argues conservation is the right priority for research and policy; it does not claim the programme science is finished.

## Thought experiments
- **The Korean-American five-year-old** (Campbell & Lindholm, ch. 16). The chapter's central device is a deliberate hypothetical: one child, described competence by competence — phonology, morphology and syntax, discourse, honorifics and interlocutor-sensitive variation — and then priced, twice: once against the Defense Language Institute's 1,400 hours to a lower rating, and once against a university student's 600 hours over four years, who "would be fortunate" to reach the same 2/2+ range. The device converts an abstract policy word ("resources") into a measurable asymmetry: the state pays enormously to build in adults a fraction of what it lets evaporate in children. Everything in the chapter's normative case depends on the reader granting that the child's competence really is the same kind of thing the proficiency scale measures in adults — the authors flag the disanalogies themselves (no literacy; semantic networks limited to a five-year-old's worlds).

## Where the debate stands
Within this corpus there is no debate to report, and the entry does not manufacture one: book 031 stages the conservation position at length and the subtractive default only as its foil. What the chapter itself leaves open is considerable. The causal chain from home-language maintenance to better English and achievement is asserted on cited literature, not demonstrated in the chapter's own data, which are first-year programme evaluations with comparison groups that are small (at elementary level, 39 non-immersion pupils on one test) or absent (the university programme). The normative step — from "the competence is valuable and costly to replace" to "schools ought to conserve it" — is taken briskly, via the national-interest frame ("resolution of our national foreign language resource deficits"); the child's and community's own claims on the language appear in the data instruments (ethnolinguistic loyalties, self-esteem, attitudes) rather than as argued premises. And the classification question from the sibling entry cuts both ways here: the heritage secondary students are simultaneously native speakers and, academically, second-language learners of their own language — a profile the foreign/second scheme has no box for, and which the authors flag as a research gap in its own right.
