# Semantic Analysis and the Clarity of Meaning
*Subfield: Logic & Critical Thinking · Also known as: 語理分析 (linguistic–conceptual analysis); the critique of linguistic harms; "What does it mean?"*

## The question
A large share of disputes never reach disagreement about facts: the parties are using the same words in different senses, or words with no settled sense at all, and so argue past each other indefinitely — sometimes lethally. What should a thinker do *before* asking whether a claim is true? Li Tianming's answer is a discipline of its own: first establish what the claim means — or whether it means anything. No Carneades lecture is devoted to semantic analysis itself; the front door below pairs the closest lecture (on the kinds of unclarity) with Li's own procedure, and is marked accordingly.

## The 90-second version
Unclarity comes in kinds, and the kinds matter. A term is **ambiguous** when it has several distinct definitions in play ("bank": riverside or financial institution). A term is **vague** when it has absolute borderline cases no inquiry can settle — how many grains make a heap, how sparse hair must be to count as bald; vague terms generate the sorites paradox, in which one grain never makes a heap yet heaps somehow form. A term is **general** when its meaning is clear but ranges over many things ("prime number" — no borderline cases, hence not vague). Li's programme starts where the lecture stops: most consequential confusion is not innocent vagueness but *undetected* unclarity — words taken as clear because they are familiar, or taken as profound because they are obscure. His method, semantic analysis, is simply the disciplined habit of asking "What does it mean?" about the key terms of a problem before debating its answer: clarify "democracy" first, and the question "Is Vietnam a democracy?" splits into a meaning-question (settleable by analysis) and a fact-question (settleable by investigation). A statement that survives no clarification at all is worse than false — it is *not even false*, since a claim must have a meaning before truth or falsity can apply to it. (Lecture part distilled from [Vagueness vs Ambiguity vs Generality](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8SGizqyt8EM) and [The Paradox of the Heap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b2yC-kQK_1o); the programme part distilled from Li's textbook chapters, there being no lecture on semantic analysis in the series.)

## Positions

### Semantic analysis as the first link of methodology (Li)
Thinking methodology has four departments — semantic analysis, logical method, scientific method, fallacy analysis — and semantic analysis is the first link, because "when we think about a problem, in principle our first step must be to know what the problem asks." It is also one half of the key habit of critical thinking: the two questions "What does it mean?" and "What are the grounds for it?", meaning first, grounds second — with the rider that both questions must be asked *aptly*: where meaning is already clear enough, asking again is nitpicking, and Li is explicit that semantic analysis "is absolutely not nitpicking over words; it is the sharp instrument that specialises in breaking word tricks."

**Canonical argument** (source: Li, *Methods of Thinking and Independent Thinking*, book 002 ch09, Part I §I — a formalization of the chapter's methodological argument; read in my English translation):
- P1. A question can be answered — and a statement assessed as true or false — only once it is known what the question asks and what the statement means. ("When we can recognise a statement as true or as false, we already know what it means.")
- P2. Many disputes and statements fail exactly here: their key terms are ambiguous, floating without fixed standards, or such that no meaning can be extracted at all.
- ∴ C. Clarifying meaning is methodologically prior to seeking answers and appraising truth: it settles the meaningful questions into parts that investigation can then decide, and dissolves the meaningless ones at the root ("not even false").

### The two illusions of clarity (Li)
Two standing errors protect unclear speech (source: ch09, Part I §II):
- **Taking obscurity for profundity.** Obscure speech is admired as deep; the more muddled the speaker, "the more the listener admires him" (Li quoting Thouless). Li's counter-maxim: "Muddled speech does not reflect profound thought; muddled speech reflects only a muddled brain." A test with teeth: clear statements expose their own defects, whereas obscure ones hide whether they have any others — including fallacies.
- **Taking familiarity for clarity.** Grammatical, familiar formulas — "from the viewpoint of history…," "with a Chinese heart," "do heroes make the times or the times make heroes?," "which matters more, money or friends?" — pass as meaningful because they are heard daily. Clarification of the last example shows the method in miniature: "friends" ranges from death-oath intimates to nodding acquaintances and "money" from coins to fortunes, and the answer flips with the clarification; before it, the question has no discussion-basis at all.

### Pseudo-semantic projection (Li)
A third ill, named in ch09 §II(C): assuming a compound expression must have a meaning because its parts do. "Half a catty" and "music" mean something; "half-a-catty music" need not. The chapter's worked target is theological God-talk — "omnipresent but not in space and time, without emotions yet wrathful" — and the two standard defences, demolished in sequence:

**The defence, as Li reconstructs it** (source: ch09 §II(C)):
- P1 (defence). Such statements exceed human understanding — they are inconceivable and unspeakable.
- P2 (defence). They are, instead, metaphorical and symbolic speech about God.

**Li's refutation** (same source):
- Against P1: what is too deep to understand (recursion theory, the proof of Gödel's theorem, for most people) is still *meaningful*; a meaningless string is not beyond understanding but beneath its application. Appending "inconceivable and unspeakable" to "Yoh-zi-oh-xi ▲● Geba-ge" does not give it meaning either — and saying at length that God is unspeakable is itself a performance of self-contradiction ("slapping one's own face").
- Against P2: if x is inconceivable, no one can know what resembles or symbolises x; and metaphorical *speaking* is still speaking. The defence therefore re-commits the contradiction it was meant to escape.

### The taxonomy of linguistic harms (Li)
*Thinking and the Mind-Demons* (book 002 ch10) organises the failure modes into three categories — the "mind-demons" of the title — each exhibited, not merely defined:
1. **Semantic ambiguity** （語意曖昧）: words like "mission" and "fate" (usable of any outcome whatever), the dialectical use of "negation" (under which one cannot say whether the negation of knowing is not-knowing); **terminological camouflage** — jargon and symbols as smokescreen (statistics without base numbers; pseudo-formulas in aesthetics); **semantic floating** （虛浮）: words without fixed standards, where Li insists on degree — "young" has approximate bounds (18 yes, 80 no) where "counter-revolutionary" has none; **semantic incoherence** （錯亂）: word-salad that no sympathetic reading can rescue.
2. **Empty verbiage** （言辭空廢）: statements with meaning but no information content are taken up in *Closed Systems and Pseudo-Explanation*; the bridge is Category III:
3. **Conceptual slippage** （概念滑轉）: sliding between senses mid-argument — **conceptual confusion** between two licit senses ("same" as identical vs similar, which makes "no two people can communicate, since no two have the same experiences" either empty or false), and **conceptual distortion** into senses usage does not licence (criticism of religion relabelled "infringing religious freedom"; "another kind of freedom," "real equality" — **calling a deer a horse**). Voltaire's Holy Roman Empire — neither holy, Roman, nor an empire — is Li's emblem for the cure: check the substance a stirring name refers to.

### The boundary cases: vagueness and the heap (the lectures)
The lecture taxonomy insists on one point Li's framework needs and does not supply in the same form: some unclarity is *in the predicate*, not in the speaker. "Bald," "heap," "short" have absolute borderline cases; no amount of counting hairs or grains settles them, and the sorites argument (one grain is not a heap; one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap; so there are no heaps) shows vagueness generating paradox, not mere muddle. The lecture ends in questions rather than a verdict: are vague terms sloppiness to be engineered away, or does language lose something real if every predicate is given a cut-off? It also notes the recursive sting — "vague" itself has borderline cases — against the view (attributed to "Fraga" in the captions, presumably Frege) that vague predicates are simply meaningless.

**Objections to Li's harder line** — from within Li's own text: his notes concede that whether speech counts as *floating* is context-dependent (a phrase with legal articles or precedents behind it may be clear enough), that elastic words are often indispensable, and that demanding logical precision in everyday talk is itself a student error he corrects. The calculus he actually applies is proportionality: clarification is owed where the dispute turns on the term, not everywhere.

## Thought experiments
- **The nursemaid's application** (Li, ch10 introduction). "I eat anything, and I am most fond of children": fond as in *dotes on*, or fond as in *likes to eat*? The hiring decision cannot even begin before the clarification — Li's domestic proof that "don't sign before you've seen clearly" applies to sentences as to contracts.
- **The three birds** (Li, ch10 §III(B)). "Three birds on a tree; one is shot dead; how many are left?" As arithmetic, 2; as an empirical question, 0–3 (and "more than 3" is arguable) depending on deaf, silly, stunned, and branch-caught birds. The school's single mandated answer is, for Li, conceptual confusion institutionalised.
- **"Half a wife"** (Li, ch09 §II(C)). Lover? fiancée? concubine? the half rescued from cannibals? The parts mean; the compound's meaning is exactly what is in question — the pseudo-semantic projection test case.
- **The heap** (lecture). Add grains one by one: the paradox is that every step is innocent and the destination undeniable — vagueness with a proof-shaped shadow.

## Where the debate stands
Within these sources the programme is argued, not merely announced: Li's ch09 answers the two standing objections — that analysis is pedantry (the "don't scratch where it doesn't itch" rule; target the problem's core) and that language itself is the prison ("shackles thesis" — refuted, in his view, by the performative fact that its advocates must use language, at length, to urge escaping it, and by the chaos that its formula "non-x is x" licenses). The sources disagree more quietly at one seam: Li's paradigm harms are *curable* unclarities — senses unsettled because nobody has done the work — while the vagueness lecture's borderline cases are presented as incurable in principle (no inquiry settles the last grain), and it leaves open whether that is a defect of language or a feature thought cannot do without. Li's degree doctrine for floating terms ("young" has approximate bounds) sits between the two, and nothing in the corpus forces a choice. Book 025's title-level pairs (Profundity/Obscurity; Discussion/Argument) gesture at the same territory, but its text is absent from the corpus and can settle nothing.
