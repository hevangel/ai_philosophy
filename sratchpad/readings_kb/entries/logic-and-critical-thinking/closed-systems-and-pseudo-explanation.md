# Closed Systems and Pseudo-Explanation
*Subfield: Logic & Critical Thinking · Also known as: irrefutability; empty propositions; 空廢命題； 封閉系統； hindsight explanations （馬後炮解釋）*

## The question
Some doctrines never lose. Every outcome confirms them; every objection is absorbed; no conceivable fact would count against them. Is that strength — the mark of a deep, all-embracing truth — or is it exactly the defect? Li Tianming's answer: a statement that no evidence could disconfirm is not thereby protected truth but a *closed statement*, insulated from the world; and a system built from such statements explains everything in the way that "x is so because x is x" explains things. There is no Carneades lecture on falsifiability or closed systems in the corpus, so this entry's 90-second version is distilled from the textbook and marked as such.

## The 90-second version
*(No lecture covers this problem; distilled from Li's textbook chapter, book 002 ch10.)* Start one step earlier, with the **tautology**: a sentence true by meaning alone — "there are fish in West Lake, or there are no fish" — which is necessarily true and precisely thereby says nothing about the world: it holds in every possible circumstance, so it describes none. Tautologies are innocent in mathematics and logic, where they are tools and analyses, not reports. They turn vicious as **empty propositions** when they masquerade as factual statements — the weather report that "tomorrow it will rain or it won't," the research result that all juvenile delinquents' mothers are women. Emptiness can be manufactured, too: stretch a word until the claim cannot fail (reflection that includes *distorted* reflection, which "can equal not reflecting," yields "every thought reflects or does not reflect class interests"), or rescue a claim by forcing its definition (if a man is not innately good, he is "not human," and "all humans are innately good" becomes as safe — and as empty — as "all mothers are female"). The general diagnosis comes in the chapter's conclusion: semantic ambiguity, empty verbiage, and conceptual slippage look like three different faults, but they share one property — **evadability of refutation**. Statements so built form **closed systems**: the divine-will theory ("why did Judas betray Jesus? God's will; why did others not? Also God's will") and the class-imprint theory survive everything because they risk nothing; they can only ever be fired as hindsight cannons — after the church stands or falls, the "explanation" is pronounced, and beforehand they predict nothing at all. That is a **pseudo-explanation**: the syntax of "because," without the substance.

## Positions

### Tautology vs empirical truth (the machinery)
Li's ch10 §II(I) sets up the pair with the West Lake sentences P and Q (source: book 002 ch10 — read in my English translation):
- **Empirical truth**: determinable only by examining the facts; has information content; lacks necessity. (P: there are fish in West Lake.)
- **Tautology**: determinable by understanding the meaning alone ("all bachelors are unmarried"); has necessity; lacks information content, because a sentence true under all circumstances describes no particular circumstance.
- "Having no information content" ≠ "having no meaning": Q has a clear meaning. The defect appears only at the masquerade — the tautology offered as a factual report.

### Empty propositions and their three disguises (Li)
Ch10 §II(II) exhibits the disguises:
- **Empty talk used as grounds**: "what is to happen will in the end happen" deployed as a reason for inaction — even for not dodging a car. "Do such people really believe it? — such grounds are obviously invalid," yet uttered with the air of a discovery about the world.
- **Concealed emptiness**: "Language is just language; science is not everything" — accusations of the form "x is only what x is." And reflection theory, whose "distorted reflection" quietly redefines "reflects" to include "does not reflect," converting a risky theory into "reflects or does not reflect."
- **Forced definitions that manufacture emptiness （強定成空）**: rescuing "talent always meets its opportunity" by ruling that unmet talent was never real talent; "no dishonest person can be called a Christian." Li's meta-point: if a definitional rescue can defend *any* statement — Wilde's aphorism included — it defends none.

**Canonical statement of the pattern** (source: ch10 §II(C), formalized): a claim C protected by redefining its key term T, mid-refutation, so that counterexamples to C no longer count as T:
- P1. As originally used, C has information content and the counterexample is genuine.
- P2. After the forced redefinition, C follows from the meaning of T alone.
- ∴ C. The defended C′ is a different, empty proposition; the original claim has been abandoned, not saved.

### Closed systems (the generalisation)
The chapter's conclusion (ch10, "On Closed Systems") names the unity:
- **Closed statement**: one made irrefutable by conceptual slippage, or irrefutable by nature — semantically ambiguous or empty. **Closed system**: a system of such statements. Both lack falsifiability — Li takes the word broadly, as *disconfirmability*, expressly to avoid importing the Popper–Kuhn–Lakatos disputes (his note 47).

**Canonical argument** (source: ch10, Conclusion and §I — a formalization of the chapter's reasoning):
- P1. A statement has cognitive, factual content only if some possible evidence could count against it — only if it describes some circumstances and excludes others.
- P2. A closed statement excludes nothing: ambiguity gives it no truth-value to test, emptiness makes it true on every outcome, slippage lets it retreat to whichever sense the evidence spares.
- ∴ C. A closed system, however comprehensive it feels, makes no contact with the factual world: it is "an insulated system," and its explanations are pseudo-explanations — hindsight without prediction.

### The two specimen systems (Li)
- **The divine-will theory.** After plague and the Great Fire, a Commons committee (Li citing Russell) found God's anger — at Hobbes's books — the cause, and banned them; no plague or fire of that scale returned, which "seemed very effective." Li's three counts: the theory *deepens* perplexity (why is God's will thus rather than otherwise?); it is indefensible against escalation (the super-God whose will wills God's will); and it is post hoc only — standing church or fallen church, the verdict is identical and issued afterwards.
- **The class-imprint theory.** Every phenomenon "reflects class interests — either faithfully or distortedly"; every event becomes "yet another instance" of the theory — Li's sentence ends with his own question mark of disbelief.

### The truth-illusion (the social corollary)
Why do closed systems *attract*? Ch10's conclusion §II: their irrefutability is misread as the mark of absolute truth ("the truth-illusion"), and the holder becomes a self-appointed **messenger of truth**: always correct because he represents truth, entitled to inspect and reform others' thoughts; utopia is planned from a blueprint detached from the world, failure is blamed on hidden enemies (visible, invisible, yesterday's, tomorrow's, "and finally enemies who are not enemies"), and living people become means to the ideal. Li's closing measure of the stakes: "from saviour of the world to enslaver of the world is only a hair's breadth; from messenger of truth to forger of tragedy is only half a step." Ch13 §III adds the epistemic twin in the September Debate: Horner's six "God is the best explanation" arguments are **hindsight-cannon explanations** — unlike the scientist's micro-to-macro explanations, they carry no verifiability, direct or indirect, and therefore no predictive power; an undetectable demon "explaining" the knock at the door is Li's control case.

## Thought experiments
- **The weather report** (ch10 §II(I)). "Tomorrow it will rain or it will not rain; there may be a typhoon, and there may not." Necessarily true — and a sackable offence as a forecast. The cleanest possible exhibition that necessity and informativeness trade against each other.
- **The stone church** (ch10 Conclusion §I(A)). St Peter's stands after the earthquake: God's will. It falls: God's will. The theory's verdict is known before the evidence is — because the evidence is never consulted.
- **The undetectable demon** (ch13 §III(A)). A knocking at the door, "explained" by a demon no test could detect. If it explains exactly as well as "someone is knocking," and predicts nothing beyond it, the explanation is seeming, not real.

## Where the debate stands
Li's own caveats mark the unsettled edges inside his text: the falsifiability he invokes is the broad *disconfirmability*, deliberately not Popper's technical falsificationism (note 47), so the criterion's known hard cases are not adjudicated here; tautologies used as reminders or as analytic results are expressly *not* defects (note 27), so the target is the masquerade, not the form; and emptiness is judged relative to context — a claim is empty *as offered*, not in the abstract (note 35). Nothing in the corpus answers Li from the other side: the corpus contains no defence of unfalsifiable systems as deep or of explanation without prediction, and the closed-system analysis consequently stands in the KB as a one-sided but internally qualified construction. Its boldest claim — that the three linguistic harms are *one* phenomenon because they share irrefutability — is asserted by enumeration of the categories, and Li does not consider a harm that is refutable yet misleading (a plain falsehood does the job, but it is not a "linguistic harm" in his scheme at all — a boundary his definitions, not his arguments, draw).
