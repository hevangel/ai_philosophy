# Democracy and the Voting Paradoxes
*Subfield: Political philosophy · Also known as: the paradox of voting (Downs' paradox); the problem of inclusion; procedural democracy*

## The question
Democracy is rule by the people — but the deceptively simple definition conceals two problems that this entry keeps apart, because our sources do. The first is *procedural*: granted an association needs binding decisions, what must its decision procedure look like to count as democratic, and who must be included in "the people"? The second is *motivational*: voting is democracy's basic act, yet a simple calculation suggests that for a rational, self-interested person, voting is not worth the trouble — the paradox of voting, or Downs' paradox. If both problems bite, democracy is a procedure nobody rational performs, aggregating preferences by rules that (per the companion entry on Arrow) cannot be fully fair.

## The 90-second version
Downs' paradox argues it is irrational to vote, because the costs outweigh the benefits. The cost of voting is small but real — time, travel, lost hours. The benefit is not the value of your candidate winning; it is that value multiplied by the probability that *your* vote is the decisive one — the election exactly tied without you. In any large election that probability is minuscule: even if a million dollars rode on the result for you personally, a one-in-a-million chance of being decisive makes the expected benefit about two dollars. So a rational, self-interested agent — where "self-interested" generously includes wanting others to be better off — stays home. The lectures' replies deny that decisiveness is the only benefit: votes also build a candidate's *mandate* and *express* who you are; voting is a collective-action problem, like overgrazing the village green, that societies rightly solve with mandates and social pressure; your vote swells your group's share, and politicians court groups that turn out; and in *local* elections — the many contests actually on your ballot — margins are small, single-vote ties have really happened, and the stakes touch daily life. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is the Paradox of Voting? (Downs' Paradox)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BheQT7GEISk), [The Voting Paradox (Part II)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hvPSU7KlHJ0), [What is Democracy?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HlONC5qbXds).)

## Positions

### Democracy defined — and graded (the lecture; Dahl)
Democracy is, in the lecture's plain definition, "the power of the people": decisions made by the people directly or indirectly through elected representatives — contrasted with autocracy (one person), plutocracy (the rich), theocracy (the church). The hard part is not the slogan but implementation: the lecture (drawing on the V-Dem classification) grades democracies — electoral, then liberal (adding civil liberties and human rights), participatory, deliberative, and egalitarian — and insists democracy is "more often a spectrum than a binary system": voting every few years with no real opposition, controlled parties, or districts redrawn to nullify your choice is democracy in weakened form.

**Dahl's procedural criteria** (source: Dahl, "Procedural Democracy," anthology 3rd ed., ch. 11): Robert Dahl argues that from minimal assumptions about an association — it needs *binding decisions*; its process has an agenda-setting and a decisive stage; binding decisions should be made *only by members*; equally valid claims justify equal shares ("any procedure for allocating X… must give equal regard to the claims of each") — one is driven to accept criteria specifying democratic procedures, such that "an association that satisfied all of the criteria would be fully democratic in its procedures." The assumptions alone do not yet require democracy: if one member were judged by all to be definitely more qualified on every matter, rule by that member would violate nothing so far. The specifically democratic step needs the further premise Dahl goes on to defend about members' equal qualification to govern themselves.

**The problem of inclusion** (source: Dahl, same chapter): "Since their origins in classical Greece, democratic ideas have been plagued by the problem of inclusion: what persons have a rightful claim to be included as citizens with full and equal rights to participate in governing the association?" Dahl's strategy is to set the criteria first and the boundary question second, then to show the well-known solutions — those of Schumpeter, Locke, Rousseau, and Mill — defective, before offering his own. The corpus chapter's map entry records the argument's shape; the inclusion problem itself is the part of procedural democracy that no voting rule can settle, since every rule presupposes an electorate.

### Downs' paradox (as the lecture formulates it)
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, "What is the Paradox of Voting? (Downs' Paradox)" — a formalization of the lecture's three propositions):
- P1. It is rational and good for your self-interest to vote. (The intuition to be tested; "self-interested" is construed broadly — your benefits include the satisfaction of seeing goods you want, for anyone, enacted.)
- P2. A rational, self-interested person will not take actions for which the costs outweigh the benefits.
- P3. For voting, the costs *do* outweigh the benefits: the expected benefit is (probability your vote is decisive) × (the difference the outcome makes to you); the probability of decisiveness in a large election — an exact tie broken by you — is "astronomically small," shrinking any benefit, however huge, "practically to zero," while the cost of voting is "higher than next to nothing."
- ∴ C. P1 is false: it is not rational, on this accounting, to vote. (The lecturer's own verdict is skeptical rather than triumphant: he votes, citing the replies below and the guilt of the counterfactual in which his one vote would have decided it.)

**Objections / replies, from the two lectures** —
- *The mandate response.* You vote not to be decisive but to enlarge your candidate's margin — the mandate with which she can claim approval for legislation, or shrink the opponent's. The lecturer's reservation: whether mandates really move legislation is doubtful, and a slightly larger mandate's expected benefit may still fall below the cost.
- *The expressive response.* Voting is like wearing your team's jersey: it expresses your views and places you in a group; the benefit is the expression, not the outcome. The lecturer notes people plausibly vote from a *combination* of motives — impact, mandate, expression, guilt-avoidance, and the knock-on effect of visibly voting, which persuades others (convince ten people and your effective impact multiplies).
- *The collective-action objection* (Part II lecture). Voting resembles the tragedy of the commons: grazing one more animal, or staying home, costs the individual nothing measurable, but universal defection destroys the common good — here, democratically elected government itself. Collective-action problems are standardly solved by mandates (fines for overgrazing; compulsory voting or social pressure to vote), and there may be a moral duty to cooperate even before any mandate exists, "a categorical imperative" against doing what would be ruinous if everyone did it.
- *The coalition objection* (Part II). Politicians court *voters*, not citizens: a demographic that turns out at twice another's rate gets its views written into platforms even though no single ballot is decisive. (The lecture's example: evangelical and unaffiliated Americans are similar population shares, but evangelicals vote at roughly twice the rate and are courted accordingly.)
- *The local-election objection* (Part II). The paradox's arithmetic assumes a huge electorate and a single contest. Local races have small margins — the lecture cites a 1994 Wyoming state-house race and Nevada council races in 2002 and 2011 that ended in exact ties — larger effects on daily life (schools, judges, sheriffs), and a single trip to the polls casts votes in many such contests at one cost.

## Thought experiments
- **The million-dollar voter** (Downs lecture). A candidate would give you $1 million; the other would take $1 million from you. Surely voting is rational *now*? With a one-in-a-million chance of being decisive, the expected benefit is (1/1,000,000) × $2,000,000 = $2 — likely below the cost of the trip. Meant to show the paradox does not depend on apathy or small stakes: it is driven entirely by the decisiveness probability, which is why all five replies work by finding benefits *other than* decisiveness.
- **The village green** (Voting Paradox Part II). Everyone may graze; one overgrazer ruins nothing; universal overgrazing starves all the animals. The green is the lecture's model of voting as a collective-action problem — and of the standard fix: a rule everyone has reason to vote *for* precisely because individual incentives point the other way.

## Where the debate stands
The two halves of this problem do not meet in our sources, and the entry does not force them together. Dahl's question — what procedure counts as democratic, and who is included — is answered at the level of criteria and left open at the boundary (inclusion), where the chapter finds the standard solutions defective. Downs' question is answered by its lecturer with a hedge: the paradox succeeds against the *decisiveness* rationale for voting, and the five replies relocate voting's rationality in mandates, expression, collective duty, coalition share, and local margins — with the mandate reply flagged by the lecturer himself as possibly too weak to cover the cost. The honest summary is his: "as a skeptic I don't know if it's right to vote, I don't know if it's rational to — but I am doing it anyway." A naming caution for readers of the corpus: the file "The Voting Paradox (Part II)" is part II of the *Downs* paradox treatment, not of the Condorcet voting paradox; the cyclic-profile paradox lives in the Arrow lectures (see the companion entry).
