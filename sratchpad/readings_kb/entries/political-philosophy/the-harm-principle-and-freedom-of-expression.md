# The Harm Principle and Freedom of Expression
*Subfield: Political philosophy · Also known as: Mill's liberty principle; the harm principle*

## The question
When may society — by law, or by the quieter coercion of public opinion — stop an individual from doing, or saying, what she wants? Every age answers with its customs and calls the answer self-evident. John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859) proposes a single test meant to govern all such interference, and then gives freedom of expression the strongest defence in the canon: even a lone dissenter against all mankind may not be silenced. Where exactly the test bites — what counts as *harm*, and whether speech can ever be it — is the problem this entry tracks.

## The 90-second version
Mill's harm principle says there is one and only one situation in which free speech can be impeded: the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. But "harm" is narrower than feeling offended. In the lectures' distillation, harm in Mill's sense arises from action, not from words — words count as harming only when they are direct incitements to harmful action. Mill's own example is the corn dealer: you may print that corn dealers starve the poor, but you may not shout it to an angry mob outside the corn dealer's house. The lectures then run Mill's four arguments for letting even offensive and immoral opinions be heard: the opinion might be true, and we are not infallible; if false, it likely still carries a grain of truth no one else is supplying; a truth never contested is held as blind prejudice; and a belief nobody has to defend loses its force and meaning. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is the Harm Principle?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GgljoDujt38), [Trump vs the Corn Dealers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SwOf0GG7re8), and the series introduction [The Case For Free Speech](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S4fS1hpn7jU).)

## Positions

### Mill's principle: sovereignty over the self-regarding (Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. I)
"The sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection." The principle governs compulsion by law *and* by "the moral coercion of public opinion" — Mill's subject includes the tyranny of the majority and the "social tyranny" of prevailing feeling, which "leaves fewer means of escape, penetrating much more deeply into the details of life, and enslaving the soul itself," than political oppression does.

**Canonical argument** (source: Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. I):
- P1. The only part of anyone's conduct for which he is amenable to society is that which concerns others; in what merely concerns himself, "his independence is, of right, absolute. Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."
- P2. A person's own good, "either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant" for compulsion: he cannot rightfully be forced "because it will be better for him to do so, because it will make him happier, because, in the opinions of others, to do so would be wise, or even right" — those are grounds for remonstrating, persuading, entreating, not compelling.
- ∴ C. Power may be rightfully exercised over an individual against his will only to prevent harm to others.

**Qualifications Mill himself builds in** — the principle applies only to human beings "in the maturity of their faculties" (children are excluded, and may be protected against their own actions); positive duties exist alongside it (giving evidence, bearing a fair share in common defence, saving a fellow creature's life), whose omission can make one "justly accountable" — though Mill warns that answerability for *not preventing* evil "requires a much more cautious exercise of compulsion" than answerability for doing it. And the ground of the whole doctrine is not abstract right: "I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions; but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being."

### The case for free expression: four grounds (Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. II)
Ch. II applies the principle to thought and discussion and states the famous extreme: "If all mankind minus one, were of one opinion, and only one person were of the contrary opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind." Silencing is "robbing the human race; posterity as well as the existing generation."

**Canonical argument** (source: Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. II, Mill's own recapitulation of the four grounds):
- P1. If an opinion is compelled to silence, it may, for aught we can certainly know, be true; to deny this is to assume our own infallibility — all silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility.
- P2. Though the silenced opinion be an error, it may, and very commonly does, contain a portion of truth; and since the prevailing opinion is rarely or never the whole truth, only "the collision of adverse opinions" gives the remainder of the truth a chance of being supplied. If the opinion is wrong, hearers still gain "the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error."
- P3. Even if the received opinion is the whole truth, unless it is "vigorously and earnestly contested" it will be held "in the manner of a prejudice, with little comprehension or feeling of its rational grounds"; and, P4, its meaning will be lost or enfeebled, the dogma becoming "a mere formal profession."
- ∴ C. Freedom of opinion and of its expression is necessary "to the mental well-being of mankind (on which all their other well-being depends)" — on all four grounds jointly, not on the truth of the protected opinion.

### The limit inside the doctrine: expression as instigation (Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. III)
Freedom of opinion does not make expression unconditionally immune, because expression can cross into action.

**Canonical passage** (source: Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. III): "No one pretends that actions should be as free as opinions. On the contrary, even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act." The same proposition — that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor — is protected in the press and punishable before the mob. Ch. IV adds the social side of the limit: a person who merely displeases us in self-regarding matters may be avoided, pitied, even disliked, but may not be punished or made an enemy of society; only infringement of "the rules necessary for the protection of his fellow-creatures" makes him "an offender at our bar."

**Objections** — *Offence as harm.* The standing pressure on the principle, as the Harm Principle lecture frames it, is interpretive: many claim to be *harmed* by statements — "your statement offended me, so I was harmed" — and on a wide reading of harm the principle becomes very restrictive. The lecture's Mill replies by narrowing: the harm Mill targets comes through action, and speech harms only as direct, immediate incitement to harmful action — "not some long indirect chain of events." → **Reply from the text** — Mill's own machinery supports a narrower line than bare offence: ch. IV separates what merely displeases or lowers someone in our consideration (a cost the person "already bears… the whole penalty of his error") from conduct that infringes others' rights, and only the latter is punishable. But note the honest limit: Mill gives the boundary through cases (corn dealer, nuisance, positive duties), not through a definition of harm — the lecture's "direct incitement" gloss is a distillation of the corn-dealer case, and the entry does not present it as Mill's formula.
- *The scope exceptions.* Ch. I withdraws the principle from children and from "those backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage," where "despotism is a legitimate mode of government… provided the end be their improvement." These exceptions are in the text and are recorded here as stated; they are the part of ch. I most in tension with the principle's own universality, and our sources offer no resolution of that tension.

## Thought experiments
- **The corn dealer** (Mill, ch. III; lecture [Trump vs the Corn Dealers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SwOf0GG7re8)). Same words, two contexts: printed in the press — protected; shouted to an excited mob outside the dealer's house, or circulated as a placard among that mob — punishable instigation. Meant to show that the unit of regulation is not the proposition but the expression-in-circumstances. The lecture applies the template to a modern case — a politician addressing an angry crowd outside the legislature and urging it to "fight" — arguing Mill would protect the speaker's false and unsupported claims made elsewhere, but not the mob-facing speech; it also tests the template against social media (a virtual mob: more like the newspaper, the lecture suggests, except doxxing, which points the mob at a real-world address).

## Where the debate stands
In these sources the principle is asserted with great confidence and defended at length for the case of expression, but two things are left genuinely open. First, *harm* is bounded by examples rather than defined: the text distinguishes offence, displeasure, and self-regarding imprudence from rights-infringing harm, and protects speech short of instigation — but borderline cases (Mill's own ch. V wrestles with dealers who profit from others' excess) are decided by the principle's spirit, not by a test the text supplies. Second, the foundation is utilitarian — "the permanent interests of man as a progressive being" — so the principle's absoluteness ("one very simple principle, as entitled to govern absolutely") rests on a ground that is itself a matter of consequences; Mill does not hide this, he announces it. The lectures add that Mill's are more permissive than the speech laws of most liberal democracies, with the United States as the outlier closest to his position.
