# The Cosmological Argument
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: cosmological arguments (Thomistic and kalām families)*

## The question
Everything we meet exists, but might not have: it was caused, made, set in motion by something else. Follow the chain back — causes of causes, movers of movers — and one of two things must be true: the chain goes back forever, or it stops at something that does not itself need a cause: a first cause, an unmoved mover, a necessary being. The cosmological argument is the family of arguments that says the chain must stop, and that what stops it is God. Its mirror image also belongs to this entry: a classical Buddhist argument, preserved in book 020, that the chain cannot stop at a *Lord* — because a creator who deals out pleasure and pain unevenly looks less like an explanation than like one more thing to explain.

## The 90-second version
Think of the most obnoxious question a child can ask: *why?* — and then ask it again about every answer. Why are the leaves green? Chlorophyll. Why chlorophyll? Energy. Why…? The cosmological argument is the refusal to let that conversation either run forever or end with "because I said so." Aquinas's version works with change: whatever changes is changed by something else, and a chain of changers where every member is itself being changed cannot regress infinitely — so there is a first mover that moves without being moved. The kalām version works with time: everything that *begins* to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist (a beginningless series would be an actual infinite, and an actual infinite is impossible); so the universe has a cause, and since no physical explanation can cover the origin of the physical itself, the cause must be a personal agent — God. The critics ask whether the chain really can't regress (Hume: explain every part and you've explained the whole; Russell: inferring a cause for the whole from causes of the parts is the fallacy of composition — "the universe just is"), and whether the "necessary being" at the end is the ontological argument in disguise (Kant). The lecture series closes by conceding the argument may not prove God — while insisting it leaves the denier with the child's question still unanswered: *why is there something rather than nothing?* (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [St. Thomas Aquinas's Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lX-2htH5wnM), [Kalam Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=22ELxgVDwb4), [Final Thoughts on the Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Apg8jbAa1Y).)

## Positions

### Aquinas's argument from contingency (the Thomistic cosmological argument)
Aquinas argues not from a temporal beginning but from dependence here and now: an *essentially ordered* regress — where each cause is simultaneously sustaining the next — cannot be infinite.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [St. Thomas Aquinas's Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lX-2htH5wnM), following the Stanford Encyclopedia's outline of Aquinas — the lecture's own formulation):
- P1. A contingent being — a being that might not have existed — exists. (Your computer: there are possible worlds with no computer, no you, no Earth.)
- P2. This contingent being has a cause of its existence. (It didn't just pop into existence from nowhere.)
- P3. The cause of this contingent being's existence is something other than the contingent being itself. (It didn't cause itself.)
- P4. What causes or explains a contingent being must either be solely other contingent beings, or include a necessary being.
- P5. Contingent beings alone cannot provide an adequate causal account of the existence of a contingent being.
- ∴ C. The cause of a contingent being must include a necessary being — God exists.

### The First Way: the unmoved mover (Aquinas)
The first of the Five Ways (*Summa Theologica*) argues from *motion* in Aristotle's sense — change, the actualization of a potentiality (the hot stove actualizing the water's potential to be warm).

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [The Unmoved Mover (Aquinas's First Way)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YFtBC9lJmo) and [St. Thomas Aquinas's Five Ways (Proofs of God)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=unExjvKb7xU) — a formalization of the lectures' presentation, labelled as such):
- P1. Some things are in motion (change from potentiality to actuality).
- P2. Whatever is moved is moved by another: a thing's actuality causes change in others, while its own potentiality is actualized by something already actual (nothing is simultaneously actual and potential in the same respect).
- P3. A regress of movers, each moved by another, cannot proceed to infinity in an essentially ordered series.
- ∴ C. There is a first, unmoved mover — pure actuality — which Aquinas identifies with the God of the tradition (the lectures note Aristotle's beyond-physics cause of all motion was ready to hand for the identification, and that Aquinas's immediate goal is to prove *a* god, some god, exists — that it is the Christian God requires further argument).

The Five Ways as a set — first way (unmoved mover), second (first cause), third (necessity and contingency), fourth (degrees of perfection), fifth (teleology, from the directedness of things) — are mostly cosmological in character, the fifth excepted; the lecture series examines each with its objections in turn ([Objections to St Thomas Aquinas's First Way](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XJzCYqiLWt0) for the first).

### The kalām cosmological argument
The kalām family (presented in the lectures in William Lane Craig's formulation) turns on *temporal* regress: the universe cannot have existed forever, because a beginningless temporal series would be an actual infinite.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Kalam Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=22ELxgVDwb4); the lecture's own premises):
- P1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause of its existence.
- P2. The universe began to exist. (Support: an actual infinite cannot exist; a beginningless temporal series of events is an actual infinite; therefore a beginningless temporal series cannot exist.)
- ∴ C1. The universe has a cause of its existence.
- P3. No scientific explanation in terms of physical laws can provide a causal account of the origin of the universe; all explanations are either scientific or personal; so the cause's explanation must be personal.
- ∴ C. The cause of the universe is a personal agent — God.

The series devotes a lecture each to the pressure points: the beginning of the universe ([Objections to the Beginning of the Universe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmhADdYk9Sg)), the causal principle ([Objections to the Principle of Causality](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=53tcPgmmCK8)), and the personal-explanation step ([Objections to Personal Explanation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGaMuuw6vyU)). This entry does not reproduce those objection lectures' contents in detail; the three canonical critics below are the ones the Aquinas series develops, and the kalām objections stand as the corpus's record that each kalām premise is independently contested.

### The classical objections (Russell, Hume, Kant — as the lecture series presents them)
**Russell: the fallacy of composition** ([Russell's Objection to the Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ppBxkTTGoRQ)). From "every part has a property" it does not follow that the whole has it: bricks can each be small while the wall is big. Just because everything *in* the universe has a cause, it does not follow that the *universe* has a cause; just because every part is contingent, the whole need not need an explanation. Russell's own verdict, per the lecture: "the universe just is." The lecturer reports finding this the most convincing of the three.

**Hume (i): explaining the parts is sufficient** ([Hume's Objections to the Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RF6GVgZgiWA)). Give me twenty balls of matter and explain each one, and I have explained the twenty — no further explanation of "the whole" is owed. Likewise the universe. The lecture's own pushback: explaining parts *in terms of each other* (chicken by egg, egg by chicken) is circular, and a satisfactory explanation seems to require stepping back to the whole system — so Hume's first objection is "a little worrisome" rather than decisive. **Hume (ii): the causal principle is dubious.** We can conceive of something without a cause; what is conceivable is possible; so "everything has a cause" is questionable. The lecture again notes the symmetry problem: arguments from conceivability are themselves questionable, since we can seem to conceive a necessary truth false merely by not fully understanding it.

**Kant: the argument leans on the ontological argument** ([Kant's Objection to the Cosmological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gqrRl-1UT70)). The cosmological argument concludes to a *necessary* being — but necessity in which sense? If logical necessity (contradiction inconceivable), note that we *can* imagine a world without God, unlike a four-sided triangle; and the claim that God is logically necessary anyway "seems to presuppose the truth of the ontological argument" — God as greatest possible being whose existence is built in. Modal necessity (existing in all possible worlds) falls into the same trap: it needs an argument for God's *possibility* of the ontological kind, and a being modally necessary but not logically necessary might exist in no world at all. Either way: "if the ontological argument was successful, then the cosmological argument would also be successful. But, if the ontological argument failed, the cosmological argument would not be able to stand on its own two feet."

**The lecturer's final thought** ([Final Thoughts](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Apg8jbAa1Y)): after objecting "to almost every premise," the lecturer concedes the argument's residual force as a *question*. The denier "has not given a sufficient answer to the central question of the cosmological argument" — why is there something and not nothing, why is *this* possible world the real one? "Although the cosmological argument does not seem to prove the existence of God, it does pose a very good question for anyone who does not believe in God."

### The anti-creator argument: Bhāviveka against the Lord (book 020, ch. 13)
The corpus's primary text against a creator is not Western at all. In his commentary on Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikāḥ* 1.1, the sixth-century Buddhist philosopher Bhāviveka reads the verse's "no-cause" (*ahetu*) as an *implicative* negation — a "bad cause" (*kuhetu*), as a "no-wife" is a wife who exists but does not do what a wife does. Among such no-causes he lists the Hindu Lord (*īśvara*): the Lord may exist, but he does not *create* the world. Eckel's chapter (the source for this section) stresses that Bhāviveka grants the Lord's conventional existence throughout — what he attacks is only the Lord's capacity to function as the world's cause.

**Canonical argument 1 — the uneven hand** (source: Eckel, book 020, ch. 13, §13.3, reporting Bhāviveka's inference from conventional truth):
- P1. The Lord, on the opponent's own showing (the *Mahābhārata*'s Draupadī: humans "impelled by the Lord … may go to heaven or hell"), causes pleasure for some and pain for others — dealing them out like a cowherd, with the virtuous (Yudhiṣṭhira: "upright, gentle, bountiful, modest, and truthful") ruined at dice while the vicious prosper.
- P2. A cause whose effects show such capricious, cruel unevenness cannot be the cause of an orderly, just world.
- ∴ C. "The Lord cannot be the cause of the arising of the world even conventionally."

Eckel is explicit that P1 is a theodicy argument: Draupadī "is raising what we would call the problem of theodicy" (see [The Evidential Argument from Evil](evidential-argument-from-evil.md)).

**Canonical argument 2 — no single creator** (source: same):
- P1. The world is an object of cognition (*prameya*).
- P2. The Lord himself is an object of cognition (*prameya*) — known, on the opponent's own texts, by meditating yogis.
- ∴ C. The world shares the relevant property with the Lord: as the Lord was not created by the Lord, "the world certainly does not possess a single creator called the 'Lord.'" (Eckel presents this as the *beginning* of an argument — the opponent may reply that world and Lord differ in the relevant respect.)

**The ultimate-level argument** (source: same): even granting the Lord as cause, the effect must be either the Lord himself, different from him, or both — and each option has already been refuted in Nāgārjuna's tetralemma analysis of arising (nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause). "Therefore there ultimately is nothing that arises from the Lord."

**The alternative**: if "Lord" is to name the world's cause at all, let it name *karma* — "the cause of the multifaceted world of sentient beings along with their physical environment," which produces pleasure and pain, arising and destruction. Taken conventionally Bhāviveka accepts this; taken ultimately he does not (karma does not ultimately arise either). Eckel, citing Weber, notes why this answers the uneven-hand argument: karma makes the world "a completely connected and self-contained cosmos of ethical retribution," shifting the explanation of Yudhiṣṭhira's loss from a capricious agent to the agent's own prior action — "the most complete formal solution to the problem of theodicy."

## Thought experiments
- **The obnoxious child** (Aquinas's Cosmological Argument lecture). Every cosmological argument, the lecturer suggests, has the form of the child's endless *why?*: either the conversation goes on forever (infinite regress), or it terminates in something that cannot itself be explained further — and "the only thing that can't be explained is God." An oversimplification, the lecture admits, offered for the intuition: the argument is a theory of where the why-chain may legitimately end.
- **The computer** (same lecture). Your computer is the paradigm contingent being: easy to picture a world without it, without you, without computers, without Earth. It didn't pop into existence from nowhere and didn't cause itself. The mundane case carries P1–P3 of the Thomistic argument before the contested steps (P4–P5) begin.

## Where the debate stands
The lecture series ends where it ends deliberately: unconvinced by the argument as a proof, unwilling to say the underlying question is answered. Each load-bearing premise has a named, argued objection in the corpus — composition (Russell), sufficiency of parts and the causal principle (Hume), borrowed necessity (Kant), and premise-by-premise objection lectures for the kalām — and no corpus source stages a systematic theistic reply to the trilogy. Book 020 contributes the debate's non-Western mirror: Bhāviveka's conclusion is narrower than atheism (the Lord may exist; he does not create; ultimately nothing arises from anything), and the positive doctrine he defends — karma as the world's conventional cause — is a *dissolution* of the first-cause question rather than an answer of the argument's own shape. Two honest cautions: the teleological member of the family (Aquinas's fifth way, argument from design by analogy) cannot be treated from this corpus — its lecture has no transcript (see Sources) — and the "cause" the arguments conclude to is, even on their own lectures' telling, only *a* necessary being or personal agent: identifying it with the God of a particular religion is further work none of these sources does.
