# The Evidential Argument from Evil
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: the inductive / probabilistic / empirical argument from evil; the evidential problem of evil*

## The question
Suppose it is granted — as nearly every contributor to the modern debate grants — that the existence of evil does not *logically contradict* the existence of an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God. A harder question remains: does the evil we actually find — its amount, its kinds, and above all particular horrors that seem to serve no purpose at all — make it *unlikely*, or unreasonable to believe, that such a God exists? The evidential argument from evil says yes: not that theism is disproved, but that the evidence of suffering counts against it, rationally supports atheism, or gives us a prima facie reason to reject it. The theist's replies do not claim to explain the suffering; they claim we are in no position to draw the inference the argument needs.

## The 90-second version
Start with the horrors. Walk through a forest after a fire and picture a fawn, trapped and burned, lying in agony for days before it dies — suffering that, so far as anyone can see, serves no purpose whatsoever. The evidential argument begins there: if a perfectly good, all-powerful God existed, he would prevent suffering like that unless preventing it meant losing some greater good; we can see no such good; so, probably, there is no such good, and no such God. The other side answers with a warning about eyesight. Not seeing something is only evidence that it isn't there when you could reasonably expect to see it if it were — you can conclude there is no dog in the garage that way, but not that there is no flea. Between a creator's view of all the goods there could be and ours, the gap is so large that "we see no reason" may tell us about our limits rather than about God's. A second argument skips the horrors entirely: pain and pleasure are biological tools, shaped for survival, and that whole pattern is exactly what you'd expect if no benevolent or malevolent mind is in charge of sentient life — and much less what you'd expect if a perfectly good God were. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture [The Problem of Evil](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CYEg3SgfEDc), which presents the logical and evidential problems side by side; the argument itself is staged in book 027, *The Evidential Argument from Evil*.)

## Positions

### Rowe's argument from apparently pointless suffering (William L. Rowe)
Rowe focuses on intense human and animal suffering — "in itself bad, an evil," even when it is sometimes justified as a means. His target is theism in the narrow sense: an omnipotent, omniscient, eternal, supremely good creator.

**Canonical argument** (source: Rowe, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," book 027, ch. 1):
- P1. There exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.
- P2. An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.
- ∴ C. There does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being.

P2, Rowe argues, states only a *necessary* condition on a good being's permitting suffering, and "accords with our basic moral principles, principles shared by both theists and nontheists." The fight is over P1, and Rowe is explicit about its epistemic status: the fawn's case "does not *prove* that (1) is true," and "we cannot know with certainty that instances of suffering of the sort described in 1 do occur in the world." His claim is weaker and, he thinks, enough: given the "enormous variety and profusion" of suffering, much of it apparently unrelated to any greater good and none of it plausibly *all* so related, "it is, nevertheless, altogether *reasonable* to believe that (1) is true."

In his later restatement (book 027, ch. 14), the argument is pinned to two cases — E1, the fawn ("Bambi"), and E2, the rape, beating, and murder of a five-year-old girl ("Sue") — and the support for P1 is made an explicit induction:
- P. No good *we know of* justifies God in permitting E1 and E2.
- ∴ (inductively) Q. No good *at all* justifies God in permitting E1 and E2.
- ∴ (deductively) Not-G. There is no omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good being.

Rowe also develops a Bayesian version using argued-for probability assignments, and a further variant that argues from P toward the probable falsity of theism without passing through Q. In ch. 14 he concedes ground: the bare enumerative induction from P "increases the antecedent probability of Q" but does not, as it stands, make Q more likely than not.

**The theist's best reply, on Rowe's own telling** — not the *direct attack* (pointing to goods suffering serves: character development, free choice). That fails twice over, Rowe argues: suffering routinely exceeds what such goods require and much of it does not result from free choices; and the theist "does not know what greater goods might be served … by each instance" anyway — his own tradition says God's purposes in particular sufferings are not given to us. The best reply is the *indirect attack*, the **G. E. Moore shift**: construct the equally valid counter-argument from not-C and P2 to not-P1, and let the choice between the two arguments turn on which first premise it is more rational to believe — God's existence, or P1.

**Rowe's verdict on the dialectic** — he is himself persuaded by the argument, yet defends **friendly atheism**: some theists, including informed modern ones, are rationally justified in believing theism anyway, because rational justification does not require truth, and disputants' evidence and situations genuinely differ (his example: your friends are justified in believing your plane went down with no survivors while you, bobbing in a life vest, are justified in believing you are alive).

### Draper's argument from pain and pleasure (Paul Draper)
Draper's is, in Howard-Snyder's taxonomy, an *explanatory* evidential argument: it never claims any evil is pointless, and never needs a premise saying theism is incompatible with a fact about evil. Its force is comparative — following a question Hume asked and (Draper says) contemporary philosophers ignored: does some serious hypothesis inconsistent with theism explain facts about good and evil *much better* than theism does?

**Canonical argument** (source: Draper, "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists," book 027, ch. 2 — formalization labelled as such):
- P1. Let O report our observations and testimony about sentient beings experiencing pain and pleasure, and let HI (the Hypothesis of Indifference) be: neither the nature nor the condition of sentient beings on earth is the result of benevolent or malevolent actions performed by non-human persons.
- P2. Because of the biological role pain and pleasure play in goal-directed organisms, O is antecedently much more probable on HI than on theism: Pr(O/HI) >! Pr(O/theism) (claim C).
- P3. Theistic stories (theodicies) do not significantly raise Pr(O/theism) (defended in the chapter's §III, against free-will, moral-advancement, and "infinite intellect" theodicies).
- ∴ C. We have a prima facie good epistemic reason to reject theism: theism is less probable than HI, hence less probable than not.

The probabilities throughout are *epistemic* — relative to a person's epistemic situation — and the conclusion is expressly *prima facie*: sufficient reason to reject theism "unless overridden by other reasons."

### Theodicy: the goods require the evils (Richard Swinburne; Eleonore Stump)
A theodicy, in Swinburne's definition, undertakes to show "that evils of the kind and quantity we find on Earth are neither incompatible with nor render improbable the existence of God." His own view is that this can be done without leaning on independent evidence for God to outweigh the counterevidence.

**Canonical argument** (source: Swinburne, "Some Major Strands of Theodicy," book 027, ch. 3 — a compressed reconstruction of the chapter's stated strategy, labelled as such):
- P1. A perfectly good being may allow an evil only if he has the *right* to do so and there is some good brought about by allowing it which could not be brought about in any better way — for an omnipotent being, it must be logically impossible to obtain that good without the evil (or an equally bad one).
- P2. With a sufficiently wide conception of good — wider than pleasure and pain — many actual goods meet that condition: the satisfaction of *persisting* desire requires its temporary frustration; compassion and kindred involvements require suffering to respond to; significant free choice between doing great good and great evil requires knowledge of how to harm, which observation of natural evil supplies (a strand Swinburne names but sets aside as too long to prove here).
- ∴ C. Evils of the kind (and, the chapter begins to argue, the quantity) we find serve greater goods in the way P1 requires.

Swinburne is candid about the limit: that the goods are *so good that it is worth* allowing the evils "I shall also begin to show but could not within the compass of a philosophical paper of normal length possibly show conclusively."

Stump's chapter (book 027, ch. 4) changes the question before answering it. Aquinas, reading Job, does not treat innocent suffering as a threat to God's existence but as a puzzle about *providence*: Job's friends assume providence pays out rewards and punishments in this life; Aquinas holds that what happens in this life "can be justified only by reference to [one's] state in the afterlife," suffering functioning as medicine for a condition that blocks eternal communion with God. Stump's conclusion is methodological: assessments of theodicy wait on settling what human happiness is and what the goal of human life is.

**Objections** — Gale (ch. 11) charges theodicies with epistemic laxness (accepting justifying stories on too little) and axiological laxness (miscounting the values at stake); Howard-Snyder's Introduction notes the recurring gap between goods that might justify God in permitting evil *in general* and the *particular* evils Rowe's argument cites.

### Skeptical theism: we are in no position to judge (Alston, Wykstra, van Inwagen, Plantinga)
The volume's most developed reply does not offer a theodicy. It attacks the inference from "no good *we know of* justifies God" to "probably *no good* justifies God" — what Howard-Snyder calls "the inference from inscrutable to pointless evil."

**Alston's agnostic thesis** (source: Alston, "The Inductive Argument from Evil and the Human Cognitive Condition," book 027, ch. 6):
- P1. To be justified in accepting Rowe's P1, we would have to justifiably judge, of some particular suffering, that God could have no sufficient reason to permit it.
- P2. Our grasp of the goods there are, of their value, and of the conditions under which they could be realized is radically insufficient for that judgment — the magnitude and complexity of the question outrun our powers and access to data.
- ∴ C. We are not justified in accepting P1, and the inductive argument collapses. (Alston stresses this is *agnosticism* about P1, not a theodicy claiming P1 is false.)

**Wykstra's CORNEA** (source: Wykstra, "Rowe's Noseeum Arguments from Evil," book 027, ch. 7):
- P1. An inference from "we see no X" to "there is no X" is justified only if X has reasonable *seeability* in the situation — only if, were X present, we could reasonably expect to see it. (Seeing no dog in the garage licenses "no dog"; seeing no flea does not license "no flea.")
- P2. Given the disparity between a creator's vision and ours, God-purposed goods — if there are any — are not the sort of thing we could reasonably expect to see.
- ∴ C. Rowe's "noseeum" inference (so named for the tiny biting flies you "no see 'um") fails the seeability condition; the move from "appears pointless" to "pointless" is not justified.

**Van Inwagen** (ch. 8) presses the parallel point against Draper-style arguments: we cannot judge what to expect on theism, so we cannot judge that a rival hypothesis explains the suffering better — a diagnosis he supports with two deliberately parallel, obviously faulty arguments (an "evidential argument from air" for an ancient atomist; an argument from God's silence), technicalized in ch. 12. **Plantinga** (chs. 5 and 13) adds two moves: the *total evidence* point (even a successful evidential argument shows at most that evil disconfirms theism relative to part of our evidence), and the claim that the real question is *nonpropositional* — the theist may have nonpropositional grounds for belief (of the sort classical theism itself predicts) better than the propositional grounds any argument from evil gives against it, so whether the argument renders her belief unreasonable depends on whether theism is true.

**Objections to skeptical theism** — the volume gives the other side the next word, three times over:
- **Draper (ch. 9, "The Skeptical Theist")**: the skeptical thesis (ST1 — we cannot judge directly that God would be unlikely to have a sufficient reason) may be true, but it only touches arguments that *need* that judgment. His own ch. 2 argument does not: it compares Pr(O/HI) with Pr(O/theism) without claiming any evil is probably unjustified. Inferring the failure of *all* probabilistic arguments from ST1 is "a considerable leap of faith." Skeptical theists "have not yet solved the evidential problem of evil."
- **Russell (ch. 10, "Defenseless")**: the unrepresentative-sample charge cuts only if there is reason to believe there *are* goods beyond our ken; merely possible hidden goods are like hypothetical crows that hide whenever anyone looks — they do not by themselves defeat the inference. (He concedes the limiting case: if we had no idea whether our sample was representative, suspension of judgment would be right.)
- **Gale (ch. 11)**: defensive skepticism must also reckon with the judgments that if some good justified God we would very likely know of it, and that it is "improbable in the extreme" that there are goods outside our ken of the required kind.
- **Howard-Snyder (ch. 15)** turns the specificity screw from the theist's side: the atheist's appeal to *particular* horrors presupposes that God must have a reason to permit *that* evil — that child, then and there, in that way — and a threshold thesis that exactly this much horrific evil, and no less, serves God's purposes. Both presuppositions, he argues, are questionable — so the argument must be run at the level of amounts and kinds, not individual cases. (His title distinction: *inscrutable* evil — we cannot grasp a reason — does not by itself justify belief in *pointless* evil.)

Alston's closing chapter (ch. 16) candidly re-works his own position: his ch. 6, he says, tried to do two things that "did not smoothly blend" — neutralize Rowe/Russell and mine theodicies for unexcludable possibilities. He drops the second project and rests on "the live possibility of divine reasons unenvisaged by us," which he believes sufficient to take up whatever slack the arguments leave.

## Thought experiments
- **The fawn** (Rowe, chs. 1 and 14). Lightning strikes a dead tree in a distant forest; a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before death relieves it. No one sees it; nothing, so far as we can tell, comes of it. It is the argument's engine: an omnipotent being could trivially have prevented it, so if any suffering is pointless, this looks like it — and Rowe's whole case is that "apparently pointless," multiplied across the world's suffering, rationally supports "pointless." Pressures P1's critics to say why appearance should not be trusted here.
- **Sue** (Rowe, ch. 14, case E2). The rape, beating, and strangling murder of a five-year-old girl — paired with the fawn so the argument cannot be deflected as concerning only animal or only natural evil. Ivan Karamazov's tortured children (the boy thrown to the general's hounds; the girl beaten "until her body was one bruise") play the same role in Howard-Snyder's ch. 15, which is why his specificity challenge matters: must God's reason attach to *this* child, *this* way?
- **The garage: dog and flea** (Wykstra, ch. 7). Looking around my garage and seeing no dog entitles me to conclude none is present; seeing no flea does not. The difference is *seeability*. Meant to show that "we see no justifying good" is evidence only under a condition Rowe has not established for divine purposes.
- **The zagaloops and the blue crows** (Russell, ch. 10). Aliens show us a wholly new kind of animal: from none of the many seen being red, we may not infer none are red — we have no idea whether the sample is representative. But hypothetical crows that hide whenever observed do not defeat "there are no blue crows": unobserved possibilities matter only given reason to think they are actual. Together the pair locate the real dispute — whether we have reason to believe our sample of goods is representative of all goods.

## Where the debate stands
Exactly where the volume leaves it: unresolved, and unresolved at a well-mapped point. No contributor defends the logical argument (see [The Logical Problem of Evil](logical-problem-of-evil.md)); the evidential argument's fate turns on one inference — from *inscrutable* to *pointless* — and on whether Draper's comparative, explanation-based version avoids needing that inference at all. Rowe concedes his original induction does not by itself make its conclusion more likely than not, and rebuilds it in Bayesian form; Alston concedes his first defence was two projects imperfectly blended, and rebuilds it on unenvisaged reasons alone; Draper concedes the skeptical thesis may be true and argues it does not reach his argument; Plantinga relocates the question to nonpropositional warrant, which by his own account makes the argument's force depend on whether theism is true. The editor's preface already knew the shape of this ending: the problem is "hardly 'old hat'," and the book was built as a stage for dialogue rather than a verdict. Anyone who reports this debate as settled — in either direction — is going beyond these sources.
