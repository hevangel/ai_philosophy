# The Logical Problem of Evil
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: the deductive / a priori argument from evil; Mackie's logical problem*

## The question
Can the existence of evil and the existence of the God of classical theism both be true — or do the two claims contradict each other, so that believing both is not merely improbable but *positively irrational*? The logical problem of evil is the strongest form the argument from evil can take: a purported disproof, showing theism inconsistent with a fact nobody denies, that evil exists. Its history in our sources is mostly the history of its failure — and of what that failure does and does not settle.

## The 90-second version
Line up four claims: God is all-knowing; God is all-good; God is all-powerful; and evil exists. If God knows about the evil, wants to stop it, and can stop it, why is it still there? The logical problem says the four cannot all be true — something has to go. You can give up God's knowledge, or his goodness, or his power; you can deny that evil is real; or you can take the fifth exit and argue the four were never inconsistent in the first place. The most famous version of that fifth exit is the free-will defence: a world where creatures genuinely choose, and sometimes choose evil, may be a greater good than a world of guaranteed good behaviour — and not even an all-powerful God can *force* a free choice to come out right, because a forced free choice is a contradiction, and omnipotence was never supposed to include doing contradictions. The lecture's verdict is the consensus one: the defence does not prove God exists; it defends the *possibility* of God against the charge of impossibility, and leaves the question open — with natural evil (earthquakes, hurricanes) and the weaker, evidential form of the problem still on the table. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [The Problem of Evil](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CYEg3SgfEDc) and [The Free Will Defense](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F1Kd_l-XzUU).)

## Positions

### Mackie's incompatibility claim (J. L. Mackie, as book 027 presents him)
Mackie's aim, in the words Howard-Snyder's Introduction quotes, was to show that religious beliefs "are positively irrational, that the several parts of the essential theological doctrine are inconsistent with one another." The three beliefs: "God is omnipotent; God is wholly good; and yet evil exists."

**Canonical argument** (source: Howard-Snyder, Introduction to book 027, §2, reporting Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955)):
- P1. G: God is omnipotent and God is wholly good.
- P2. E: Evil exists.
- P3. MP1: A good thing always eliminates evil as far as it can.
- P4. L: There are no limits to what an omnipotent thing can do.
- ∴ C. G and E are incompatible: a good omnipotent thing eliminates evil completely, so if such a being existed, evil would not.

Mackie himself saw that the bare trio is not inconsistent on its face; the argument runs entirely through the "additional premises, or perhaps some quasi-logical rules" MP1 and L.

**Objections** — Howard-Snyder's diagnosis, which the volume treats as decisive: G, MP1, and L rule out E only if MP1 and L are *necessary truths*. Properly understood, L is one. MP1 is not, because a wholly good being might have a morally justifying reason to permit an evil it could prevent. Substitute the plausible MP2 — a wholly good thing eliminates evil as far as it can, *unless it has a morally justifying reason not to* — and the argument then needs it to be a necessary truth that there is no such reason for God. Nothing we know rules that out: the proposition J (there is a morally justifying reason for God to permit evil, one we could not know of, and he permits evil for that reason) is, for all we know, possible — and G and J together are compatible with E. "Like logical positivism, Mackie's argument has found its way to the dustbin of philosophical fashions."

Howard-Snyder adds a *definitional* residue worth keeping, because it organizes the whole subfield: call an argument from evil **logical** when it has a premise saying theism is incompatible with some *known* fact about evil (Mackie's E is known with certainty), and **evidential** when it lacks such a premise — its key fact about evil being at most probable or reasonable to believe. Even Rowe's evidential argument has an incompatibility premise (its P2), but the fact it is incompatible with — that pointless intense suffering exists — is not *known*, only rationally believed. On that test the names, though "badly named," sort the debate correctly.

### The free-will defence (Alvin Plantinga, as the lectures and book 027 present him)
A *defence* is weaker than a theodicy: it does not claim to state God's actual reasons, only to describe a possible state of affairs in which God and evil coexist — which is all that defeating a *logical* incompatibility claim requires. Rowe's own note in ch. 1 concedes the point from the other side: "No one, I think, has succeeded in establishing such an extravagant claim [of logical inconsistency]. Indeed … there is a fairly compelling argument for the view that the existence of evil is logically consistent with the existence of the theistic God," citing Plantinga's *God, Freedom, and Evil*.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [The Free Will Defense](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F1Kd_l-XzUU), presenting Plantinga's response to Mackie — a formalization of the lecture's account, labelled as such):
- P1. Omnipotence does not include the power to do contradictions (the lecture's warm-up case: a rock so heavy God cannot lift it is a limit on our conception of omnipotence, not on God).
- P2. A creature's choice is free only if no one — God included — determines which way it goes; a world in which God forces free creatures always to choose the good is a contradiction ("Leibniz's lapse": Leibniz wrongly supposed an omnipotent being could actualize just any possible world).
- P3. Significant free will is a great good, and God may choose the best feasible world containing it even though some evil necessarily remains in such a world, because the evil is created by the creatures, not by God.
- ∴ C. The four propositions (God omniscient, omnibenevolent, omnipotent; evil exists) are consistent: the evil that exists is the price-structure of the greater good of free choice, not a contradiction in theism.

**Objections** — the lecture itself raises the two standing limits. First, *natural evil*: earthquakes, hurricanes, and tornadoes kill many people but "don't seem to really be a product of human choice or human free will," and it seems God could have made a world without them that still allowed free will. Second, *scope*: the defence does not touch the evidential problem, which is "more the job for a theodicy." The lecture's summary of the general consensus: Plantinga "has defended theism against the problem of evil but he's done so at a cost — this is not a proof of God's existence; this is a defense of God's possibility."

A Carneades-original counter presses the defence's picture of freedom itself. The **partial free will argument** ([The Partial Free Will Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=znh8-N6QYXE)) arrays figures in a hospital room — Amy in a coma (no choices at all), paralyzed Bob (choices only in thought), Cam with sixty seconds of agency, chained Don, ordinary Ed, the angel Fae, and God — to argue that free will lies on a *spectrum*: partial free will, the ability to choose some things freely but not others, is a coherent and familiar condition. If freedom comes in degrees, the defence's all-or-nothing framing (full freedom with its evil, or none) needs further defence: why could God not grant the degree of freedom that excludes the worst choices? The lecture presents this as a challenge to Plantinga's framing, not as a refutation of the consistency claim strictly understood.

Within book 027, Gale (ch. 11) records the majority verdict and his dissent from it in the same breath: "Almost everyone now believes that adequate defenses have been devised to neutralize this challenge … In particular, it is claimed that the free-will defense, in at least one of its many versions, succeeds in reconciling God's existence with moral evil," while noting his own book argues no version works and "the logical problem posed by moral evil is still with us." That dissent is flagged as *not* the chapter's concern, and the chapter does not argue it — so within this corpus the dissent is registered, not established.

Draper's ch. 9 gives the structural reason the logical form died, in terms of the proposition M (an omnipotent, omniscient being would have a morally sufficient reason for allowing E): to disprove theism one would have to prove M false or necessarily false, but "our knowledge of goods and evils and the logical relations they bear to each other is much too limited to prove that this could not be the case" — an omnipotent being could produce goods that logically entail the existence (or possibility) of E only by allowing E.

### The exits (the lecture's taxonomy)
The Problem of Evil lecture lays out the logical space the incompatibility claim creates — five "escape routes," four of which save consistency by surrendering part of the classical picture:
1. **Deny omniscience** — God does the best he can but does not know about all the evil ("it doesn't give us the God we're looking for").
2. **Deny omnibenevolence** — God knows and can prevent evil but does not care to, or is vengeful. (The dystheist/maltheist development of this exit — God exists but is not wholly good — is treated in the lectures [What is Dystheism?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NCpPaJQyS6g) and [Is God Evil? (Four Arguments)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xxccnitqT_Y), the latter arguing from scriptural acts, from the evidentiary pattern of religious people's suffering, from the "evil God challenge" (the evidence is symmetric between a good and an evil God), and from a trickster-duplication argument.)
3. **Deny omnipotence** — God knows and cares but cannot stop evil.
4. **Deny that evil exists** — the lecture calls this route "a little sketchy": it sits badly with taking God to be all-*good*, a notion that needs evil to contrast with.
5. **Deny the inconsistency** — hold all four propositions together, via a defence (free will) or, for the evidential residue, a theodicy.

## Thought experiments
- **The murder and the hand of God** (The Problem of Evil lecture). A murderer raises a knife over a victim: exactly the sort of evil an all-knowing, all-good, all-powerful God would stop — the hand of God comes down and the evil doesn't happen. But it does happen, daily. The vignette is the incompatibility intuition in its purest form, before the additional premises MP1/L are made explicit — and therefore also a demonstration of why the argument needs them.
- **The bookcase of possible worlds** (The Free Will Defense lecture). God at the beginning, browsing a vast bookcase in which each book describes a complete possible world, narrowed to two finalists: the best world with free will (in which some evil necessarily remains, because people are free) and a world with no free will in which everyone always does good. Plantinga's choice, on the lecture's telling, is clear — and the story's work is to make P2 vivid: the second book is not a *freer* world minus evil, it is a different kind of world altogether.
- **The hospital room** (The Partial Free Will Argument). Amy, Bob, Cam, Don, Ed, Fae, God — a ladder of agents with strictly increasing ranges of choice. Its point is taxonomic: if each figure plainly has *more* free will than the last without any of them being omnipotent, "free will" does not name an on/off property, and defences built on the binary inherit the burden of saying why the relevant degree could not have been set differently.

## Where the debate stands
In these sources, the logical problem is the closest thing the problem of evil has to a closed file — closed by the defence strategy, not by agreement that evil is explained. Howard-Snyder: Mackie's argument is in "the dustbin of philosophical fashions." Alston (ch. 6): "It is now acknowledged on (almost) all sides that the logical argument is bankrupt." Rowe, the evidential argument's own author, calls the inconsistency claim "extravagant" and unestablished. The "(almost)" is doing real work: Gale registers a dissent he does not argue in this volume, and the lecture tradition keeps pressing at the defence's edges (natural evil; degrees of freedom). What the failure of the logical problem settles is only the modality: evil does not *contradict* theism. Whether it *disconfirms* it is the separate, live question of [The Evidential Argument from Evil](evidential-argument-from-evil.md) — the lecture's own closing verdict on the combined problems is deliberately modest: suspend judgment, since the evidential problem "hasn't given us an argument against all gods," only against a specific kind of God.
