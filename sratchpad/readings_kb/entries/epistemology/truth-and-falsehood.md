# Truth and Falsehood
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the theory of truth; correspondence vs coherence*

## The question
Beliefs and statements can be true or false; tables and colours cannot. What does the difference consist in? "Charles I died on the scaffold" is true and "Charles I died in his bed" is false, and nothing about the beliefs themselves — their vividness, their coherence with what else the believer holds, the care taken in arriving at them — settles which is which: an event of two and a half centuries ago does. So truth seems to be a relation between a belief and something outside it. But saying exactly what the relation is, and to what, without either smuggling truth back into the definition or positing queer entities for false beliefs to relate to, is the problem of truth. Note what the problem is not: it is not "which of our beliefs are true?" — Russell insists the questions be kept entirely separate, on pain of answering neither.

## The 90-second version
Take a homely case: is today a holiday — really? Say "today is New Year's Day." What would make that true? On the *correspondence* theory, something in the world: but a holiday is not a physical object like a cup or a piano, and if the term corresponded to the objects people celebrate with, the holiday would cease to exist when the fireworks stopped, and different celebrations in different places would splinter one holiday into many. On the *coherence* theory, a statement is true if it is consistent with, and fits into, a set of other beliefs: "today is Halloween" is true because it coheres with the beliefs of your culture. But then whose set decides, when Europeans, Ethiopians, Chinese, and Muslims keep different New Years? The lecture's suggestion is that holiday-truths sit between the objective and the subjective — culturally relative — while noting that both theories carry further problems of their own. The homely case is a probe: whatever truth is, it must handle statements whose truth-makers are not obviously sitting in the physical world. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [Are Holidays Real? (Theories of Truth)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ei_bbIO8L1Y).)

## Positions

### Preliminary: truth belongs to propositions, not to ideas (Locke)
Locke opens his chapter "Of True and False Ideas" by withdrawing the terms from ideas altogether. Truth and falsehood belong, in propriety of speech, only to propositions — to affirmations and negations. An idea, a bare appearance in the mind, can no more be false than a single name can: the idea of a centaur has no more falsehood in it than the word "centaur" pronounced aloud. Ideas get *called* true or false only when the mind tacitly refers them to something outside them — to other men's ideas under the same name, to real existence, or to a thing's real essence — thereby making a secret proposition about their conformity, which proposition is what is actually true or false. Russell begins from the same boundary (below, requisite (2)): truth and falsehood are properties of beliefs and statements, and a world of mere matter would contain facts but no truths.

### Russell's three requisites, and the rejection of coherence as a definition
Russell (ch. XII) sets three conditions any theory of truth must meet: (1) it must admit an opposite, falsehood — a theory under which all our thinking ought to have been true has failed before it starts; (2) truth and falsehood are properties of beliefs and statements — no beliefs, no truth, in the sense correlative to falsehood; (3) the truth of a belief depends on something outside the belief itself, as the Charles I example shows. Requisite (3) pushes toward correspondence; but before following it, Russell examines the most important theory that tries to do without an outside relatum.

**The coherence theory, as Russell states it** — the mark of falsehood is failure to cohere in the body of our beliefs, and the essence of a truth is to form part of the completely rounded system which is The Truth.

**Canonical argument against it** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XII — a formalization):
- P1. If coherence defined truth, there could be at most one coherent body of beliefs. But there is no reason to suppose only one is possible: a novelist might invent a past for the world that fits everything we know and differs entirely from the real past, and in science two or more hypotheses routinely account for all the known facts.
- P2. Coherence itself presupposes truth: two propositions are coherent when both *may be true*, incoherent when one at least *must be false*; whether "this tree is a beech" and "this tree is not a beech" can both be true is known only via the law of contradiction — and if that law were itself subjected to the coherence test, nothing would be incoherent with anything. The laws of logic supply the framework within which the test applies and cannot be established by it.
- ∴ C. "Coherence cannot be accepted as giving the meaning of truth, though it is often a most important test of truth after a certain amount of truth has become known."

### Correspondence, defined: the multiple-relation theory (Russell)
The obstacle to a correspondence definition is falsehood. If belief were a two-term relation between a mind and a single object — what is believed — then Othello's false belief that Desdemona loves Cassio would require an object, "Desdemona's love for Cassio," which does not exist; and positing objective falsehoods as subsistent entities is a theory "to be avoided if possible." Russell's solution: believing is a *multiple* relation, knitting the subject and the several objects into one complex in a definite order (the "sense" or direction of the relation does the ordering: Desdemona–loving–Cassio is a different judgement from Cassio–loving–Desdemona with the same constituents).

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XII — a formalization of the chapter's constructive argument):
- P1. In every judgement there is a subject (the mind) and objects (the terms judged about, one of which is a relation — here, *loving* — occurring in the belief as a brick in the structure, not as the cement; the cement is the relation *believing*).
- P2. When the belief is true, there is a second complex unity composed exclusively of the objects, in the same order, with the object-relation now doing the uniting: "Desdemona's love for Cassio." When the belief is false, there is no such complex.
- ∴ C. A belief is true when there is a corresponding *fact* — such a complex unity of its object-terms united by its object-relation — and false when there is no corresponding fact. Hence truth depends on minds for the existence of beliefs, but not for their truth: "minds do not create truth or falsehood. They create beliefs… What makes a belief true is a fact," and the fact (catching-trains-type exceptions aside) does not involve the believer's mind at all.

**Where Russell takes it next** — ch. XIII demotes the defeated rival to an instrument: coherence, rejected as the *definition* of truth, returns as a *criterion* among probable opinions. A body of individually probable beliefs that are mutually coherent become more probable than any alone; it is the coherence test that "condemns the dreams and confirms the waking life." But the test "never gives absolute certainty, unless there is certainty already at some point in the coherent system" — organization of probable opinion cannot by itself manufacture indubitable knowledge.

## Thought experiments
- **Charles I: scaffold or bed** (Russell, ch. XII). Two beliefs, alike in every intrinsic respect a believer could inspect; one true, one false, in virtue of an event two and a half centuries old. Meant to isolate requisite (3): truth is extrinsic to the belief. Pressures every internalist definition — vividness, felt certainty, and (the chapter argues next) coherence among the believer's other beliefs.
- **Othello's belief** (Russell, ch. XII). Othello falsely believes Desdemona loves Cassio. There is no such thing as Desdemona's love for Cassio for his belief to be *of*. Meant to show belief cannot be a relation to a single object, on pain of objective falsehoods. Pressures simple correspondence pictures ("a belief pictures a fact") into Russell's multiple-relation refinement.
- **The novelist's alternative past** (Russell, ch. XII). A sufficiently imaginative novelist invents a past that fits everything we know and is wholly different from the real one; the resulting system coheres as well as ours does. Meant to show coherence cannot be the *meaning* of truth. Pressures coherence theories to explain what singles out one coherent system — and connects this entry to the skepticism entry's dream hypothesis, which Russell gives as the philosophical case of the same construction.
- **New Year's Day, in four calendars** (the Theories of Truth lecture). Europeans, Ethiopians, Chinese, and Muslims celebrate different New Years; "today is New Year's Day" coheres with each community's beliefs on different days. Meant to press the coherence theory's unanswered question — whose belief-set is the standard? — and correspondence's mirror-image question, what worldly item makes any of them true. Pressures both theories at once, which is the lecture's point: holiday statements are where each theory's bookkeeping gets hard.

## Where the debate stands
In these sources, correspondence wins the definitional contest by elimination and construction: coherence fails Russell's two tests (possible rival systems; presupposition of logical truth), and his multiple-relation account is offered as satisfying all three requisites at once. But the victory is carefully bounded. Russell does not claim the definition tells us which beliefs are true — that harder question is deferred to ch. XIII and answered there only with degrees of self-evidence and probability. The lecture's holiday case marks a class of statements where correspondence's truth-makers are genuinely obscure and coherence's standard-set is genuinely ambiguous, and it leaves the case — and with it the contest between the theories — open, gesturing at a cultural-relativity middle position it does not develop. Locke's contribution stands apart from the contest: whichever theory wins, its subject matter is propositions and beliefs, not ideas or things. Unsettled: whether Russell's "fact" (a complex unity of the belief's objects) can be specified without circularity for all belief-types, and what to say about domains, like holidays, where the putative facts are themselves partly constituted by belief.
