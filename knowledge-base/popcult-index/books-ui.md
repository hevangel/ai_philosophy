- ch. 8 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Friedrich Nietzsche, Søren Kierkegaard, Plato, Martin Heidegger, Aristotle — The Problem of Evil; Despair (sickness unto death); Platonic Love (the ladder of love); Simulacra and Simulation (the hyperreal) — Wrathall's introduction argues U2 is philosophically interesting because its enormous popularity holds up a mirror to what 'we' care about (by Nietzsche's test of how a culture stands toward life) and because the band publicly wrestles with the contradictions of the technological-global economy: unprecedented freedom of choice paired with loss of rooted identity, and distant suffering ('Crumbs from Your Table') that we feel both powerless to fix and responsible for. Its showcase reading treats 'Miracle Drug' as a musical theodicy: Bono's plea 'God, I need your help tonight' is answered by the Edge's guitar and by the Matthew 25:35 refrain, so science and medicine count as works of Christian charity. The piece then frames the volume's essays: Plato's ascent of love in the early songs, Kierkegaardian despair after Achtung Baby, Bono's search for authentic identity, the simulacra of 'Even Better than the Real Thing', being-toward-death in the apocalyptic songs, and the Plato-versus-Aristotle quarrel over music's emotional power.

- ch. 10 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Plato, Augustine of Hippo — Platonic Love (the ladder of love); Theory of Forms — McCoy reads U2's early longing songs through the Symposium: Aristophanes' split-beings myth turns 'New Year's Day' into a song about incompleteness whose separated lovers also stand for Poland under Soviet domination, and Diotima's ladder of love reads 'I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For' and 'City of Blinding Lights' as stages ascending from bodily beauty toward the soul and then to Beauty itself. Against a tragic reading, she argues that by Diotima's account the restless lover on the ascent 'begets' discourses and songs, so 'With or Without You' is a song of surrender rather than despair. October's 'Gloria' and 'With a Shout' get the Augustinian ending: the restless heart finds in God the object Romantic love never supplies.
- ch. 11 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Søren Kierkegaard, Plato, Blaise Pascal, Friedrich Nietzsche, Augustine of Hippo — Despair (sickness unto death); Allegory of the Cave; The Stages on Life's Way — Dreyfus and Wrathall read U2's head/heart split through Kierkegaard's Sickness unto Death: the Christian-Platonic strategy of escaping the earthly (the sun-blindness of 'Staring at the Sun', the ascent of 'Gone') collapses into despair once both sides of the self prove essential, while 'The Playboy Mansion' and 'Discotheque' depict Kierkegaardian leveling and spiritlessness in consumer culture and 'Last Night on Earth' the aesthetic-sphere despair of living pure possibility. Pascal's contradictory great-and-miserable self and Augustine's two cities supply the tradition U2 inherits, and Nietzsche's 'nihilism' names what U2 calls 'pop'. They close by arguing that Bono's pseudonymous stage characters (the Fly, MacPhisto) do phenomenology just as Kierkegaard's pseudonyms do, and that U2's songs about something missing bear out despair's 'dialectic of eternity'.
- ch. 12 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Søren Kierkegaard, Martin Heidegger, Blaise Pascal, Plato — Despair (sickness unto death); Anxiety (the dizziness of freedom); The Leap of Faith — Wrathall reads 'Vertigo' through Heidegger's vertigo-before-a-question and Kierkegaard's account of despair as the dizziness of a self whose sensual and spiritual sides are both essential. He uses rock's own testimony - Rammstein's 'Engel' and 'Sehnsucht', Nine Inch Nails' 'Down in It' - to confirm the existentialist thesis that neither flesh nor spirit can be renounced, with Pascal's God-shaped void as the diagnosis. He then argues that 'Beautiful Day', 'When I Look at the World', 'Grace' and 'Mysterious Ways' sketch an existential Christianity in which faith is not propositional confidence but a world-disclosing skill acquired like any know-how, and in which concrete erotic love ('if you want to kiss the sky, better learn how to kneel', the girl with crimson nails who 'has Jesus round her neck') discloses God on earth.
- ch. 13 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Martin Heidegger, Karl Marx, Friedrich Nietzsche — Thrownness (Geworfenheit); The Death of God — Malpas takes The Joshua Tree as an album about place: its desert imagery and restless movement ('Where the Streets Have No Name', 'I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For' as 'an anthem of doubt more than faith') carry the listener from Ireland to Reagan's America to Pinochet's Chile, while its political references - Ethiopian famine, El Salvador, Dublin high-rises - always work through concrete locations rather than abstract ideals. Using Heidegger on homelessness as the inability to dwell, and Marx and Engels' 'all that is solid melts into air', he argues that modernity has not erased our relatedness to place but covered it over. The album's desolation is therefore readable the other way: belonging is constituted through care, commitment and uncertain journeying in the places we already are ('Heartland').
- ch. 14 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Jacques Lacan, Jean Baudrillard, W.E.B. Du Bois, Friedrich Nietzsche, Sigmund Freud, Jean-François Lyotard, Karl Marx — Authenticity; Simulacra and Simulation (the hyperreal); Double Consciousness; Pragmatism — Soper tracks Bono's public identity work across three paradigms: pre-modern Irish-Catholic inheritance, including a Du Boisian 'double consciousness' of the colonized Irish; the modernist artist-as-visionary, where Nietzsche, Freud and Marx destabilize the self and the Shalom fellowship nearly ends the band; and the postmodern masks of the Fly, Mirrorball Man and MacPhisto on the Zoo TV media collage, read via Lacan for 'Mofo' and via Baudrillard's simulacra for the 'Even Better than the Real Thing' video with its wax Beatles and cyber-sex imagery. Lyotardian skepticism toward grand ideologies explains the band's refusal of fanaticism in 'Sunday Bloody Sunday' and 'Two Hearts Beat as One'. Soper concludes that Bono fuses all three into a 'Postmodern Christian Pragmatism' that keeps Christian conviction and debt-relief activism while adopting postmodern playfulness without collapsing into ironic detachment.
- ch. 15 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Jean Baudrillard, Jean-François Lyotard, Jacques Derrida, Martin Heidegger, Friedrich Nietzsche, Plato, Sigmund Freud, Karl Marx, Søren Kierkegaard — Simulacra and Simulation (the hyperreal); The End of Grand Narratives; The Question Concerning Technology; Platonic Love (the ladder of love) — Thomson uses U2's self-declared postmodern phase to define postmodernity itself: a sensitivity to poetic polysemy against modernity's fantasies of total control (Heidegger's reduction of everything to optimizable resource), Lyotard's incredulity toward metanarratives - defended against the self-refutation charge and narrowed to the dying metanarratives of unity and emancipation - and the Marx-Nietzsche-Freud hermeneutics of suspicion that fragmented the subject, with Kierkegaard and early Heidegger as the resisters. Derrida's 9/11 analysis of autoimmunity and hauntology is read into 'Peace on Earth' and the sorrows that 'learned to swim' in 'Until the End of the World'. The payoff is a polysemic reading of 'Even Better than the Real Thing': risky as a seeming hymn to Baudrillard's simulacra, addressed to a live audience it celebrates a communal love 'even better' than personal love - Diotima's ladder generalized - making the postmodern U2 continuous with the 'Christ-ian' U2 of universal love, sealed by 'One'.

- ch. 17 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** The Problem of Evil; Properly Basic Belief; Faith and Reason — Merrick sets XTC's 'Dear God' - being 'just struck' that no loving, all-powerful God rules this world - against U2's laments ('Wake Up Dead Man', 'Peace on Earth', '40') and Bono's reading of the Psalms as the blues shouted at God: both share outrage, but U2 adds yearning for the redemption promised in 'the story about eternity' plus Jubilee 2000-style action against debt and AIDS. He then argues the atheistic reaction to evil cannot be reason-producing, since neither divine design nor unguided evolution would give us a faculty that 'just sees' atheism from evil, any more than one that just sees there is no plant life on Mars. If God instead made us so that outrage at evil can trigger conviction that he will make all right, U2's reaction - 'Grace makes beauty out of ugly things' - can be as reasonable as visual perception.
- ch. 18 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Immanuel Kant — The Ethics of Care; Caring as the Ground of Ethics; Feminism; Utilitarianism — The authors apply Gilligan's and Noddings' ethics of care to U2: 'Bad' shows the speaker as Noddings' 'one-caring', and 'Sometimes You Can't Make It on Your Own' - Bono caring for his dying father through anger and resentment - exemplifies care's insistence on the particular relationship over universal rules, refusing abstraction even of the usual songwriting metaphors. 'Peace on Earth' names the Omagh bomb's dead (Sean, Julia, Gareth, Ann, Breda) and 'Van Diemen's Land' refuses both IRA and British sides, showing care ethics rejecting the utilitarian accounting and the Kantian universalizability arguments that could rationalize such violence. They conclude U2 are philosophical rather than political feminists: Gilligan's 'different voice' is not a woman's voice, and the band's connected knowing - debt relief sung as connection in 'Beautiful Day' - makes maternal thinking 'loud, rhythmic, catchy, even cool'.
- ch. 19 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Martin Heidegger, Plato, Aristotle — Authenticity; The They-Self (das Man); The Golden Mean; The Philosopher King — Delancey asks why anyone should take moral and political cues from a rock band and answers via Heidegger: in a mediascape where a few corporations orchestrate what everyone hears, the mass tune is das Man ('Numb'), and adopting it is inauthenticity - so U2, who believe what they sing for their own reasons ('Mothers of the Disappeared' against the official line on Latin America), may be among the most authentic voices available. Plato's tripartite soul and Aristotle's golden mean supply the thesis that reason needs rightly trained passion: the righteous anger of 'Sunday Bloody Sunday' and 'Silver and Gold' and the resolve of 'Walk On' are practical wisdom in song, the emotions democracy requires. Against Plato's philosopher-king and his noble lies, Delancey argues utopia can only be fostered, not enforced - so in a democracy, if you want to change the world, you may have to sing to people.
- ch. 20 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Martin Heidegger — Being-Toward-Death; Authenticity; The They-Self (das Man); Thrownness (Geworfenheit) — Benfell reads U2's apocalyptic songs through Heidegger rather than through eschatological doctrine: 'The Playboy Mansion' depicts fallenness in the 'they', a consumerist paradise with 'no time for sorrow' that lets us flee our own death, while the angel's fall in Wenders' films evoked by 'Stay' and the day-to-day constraints of 'Some Days Are Better than Others' illustrate thrownness, and 'Gone' existential guilt as an unpayable debt. A verse-by-verse reading of 'Until the End of the World' - the Judas-at-the-last-supper reading as one register - shows the speaker moving from 'passing time' and playing roles in the 'they' to anxious guiltiness and the call of conscience, reaching for the one whose resolute waiting models anticipatory resoluteness. The 'end of the world', he concludes, is the end of the speaker's own world: U2's apocalyptic imagery calls listeners to authentic, finite existence, not to prediction of last things.

- ch. 22 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Martin Heidegger, Arthur Schopenhauer, Friedrich Nietzsche, Boethius, Pythagoras, Gottfried Leibniz, G.W.F. Hegel, René Descartes — Pythagorean Harmony of the Spheres; Phenomenology; The World as Will — Han-Pile tests three traditional philosophies of music against U2's own talk about music: the rationalist (Descartes and Leibniz's 'unconscious exercise of arithmetic', satirized by Hit Song Science finding U2 mathematically close to Beethoven), the subjectivist (Hegel and Wackenroder on music as the expression of inner feeling, mirrored in Bono's method of articulating in words the feeling the band first found in improvisation), and the metaphysical (the Pythagorean-Boethian musica mundana whose harmony with our music 'Gloria' and 'Wake Up Dead Man' both invoke, consummated in Schopenhauer's music as direct copy of the will, which Nietzsche's critique of Wagner undermines). Against all three she sets Heidegger's Stimmung: music discloses shared moods - atmospheres that color how a whole world shows up, as 'Beautiful Day' or 'Bullet the Blue Sky' demonstrate. U2's vocation is thus to shift the numbed basic disposition of the age ('Zooropa', 'Numb') toward joy and compassion - rock 'n' roll as an alarm clock for 'dreaming up the world we want to live in'.
- ch. 23 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Ludwig Wittgenstein — Language Games — Gracyk explains U2's two commercial breakthroughs by pitting expression theory against Austin-style speech act theory: songs succeed not through verifiable autobiographical sincerity - 'Stuck in a Moment' was written about Michael Hutchence's suicide yet became the song of post-9/11 America, and 'I Will Follow' outmoves its equally sincere siblings - but because singing to an audience is an indirect illocutionary act that suspends ordinary commitments and invites listeners to make the song relevant to their own lives, as 'With or Without You' does by staying focused yet open-ended. Wittgenstein's point that an utterance's meaning depends on the actions into which it is woven frames the view that the audience must complete the speech act, as Larry Mullen does when he fits Dylan's 'With God on Our Side' to Northern Ireland. Pop failed, Gracyk argues, because 'Discotheque' and 'The Playboy Mansion' lecture - talk to the audience about consumerism rather than talking through them - so the unspoken invitation was withdrawn.
- ch. 24 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** William James, Bertrand Russell, David Hume — Truth as What Works; Faith and Reason — Cleveland sorts the knowing and feeling of U2's lyrics ('Vertigo', 'Beautiful Day', 'Staring at the Sun', 'Daddy's Gonna Pay for Your Crash Car') into Russell's varieties of knowledge - propositional, acquaintance, know-how - and argues that the feeling U2 champions against thought is knowledge by acquaintance: direct, concept-free awareness that, in religious cases, just is Jamesian mystical revelation as against revelation as a set of propositional truths, which is why Bono tells his father he 'hears something back from the silence' rather than holding doctrines. James supplies both the view that feeling is the deeper source of religion and the pragmatist answer to the problem of origins - 'by their fruits ye shall know them', so religious feeling is vindicated by moral transformation. Hume's slave-of-the-passions thesis explains why such feeling, not inert moral knowledge, moves Bono's social justice work: 'God is love, and as much as I respond... in acting in that love, that's my religion'.
- ch. 25 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Plato, Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas — Tripartite Soul; Theory of Forms — Tollefsen revives Plato's ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry: artistic imitations, three removes from the Forms and aimed at the soul's emotional part, stir 'imitative emotions' - purer, shorter-lived, effort-free surrogates for the real labors of love, faith and community - and he builds a four-stage account of the U2 formula (idealized transcendent love in the lyrics, religious imagery fusing lover and God, the Edge's Big Music crescendo, and the concert 'you' extended to the crowd, as in the Elevation-tour 'I Will Follow' with its 'Amazing Grace' bells) to show U2 are masters of exactly this seduction. The healing fans felt after September 11th may therefore be an image of redemption rather than the reality. Where Aristotle and Aquinas would educate the emotions rather than suppress them, Tollefsen's quarrel is with imitation itself; his verdict is that U2 suffer from Misplaced Artistic Pretension - high imagery where honest trash (or genuine complexity) would do.
- ch. 26 "U2 and Philosophy" **·** Aristotle — Virtue Ethics (arete); Moral Psychology (character, vice, weakness of will) — Werther answers Tollefsen with Aristotle's Politics: musical images of character effect real 'change[s] of soul', so a three-premise argument concludes that repeated exposure to U2's images of fine character and good action can habituate delight in virtue - King's courage in 'Pride (In the Name of Love)' (whatever the botched time of day, and as proved when Adam Clayton stepped between Bono and an FBI-confirmed death threat), the indignation and shame at global poverty in 'Crumbs from Your Table', and the compassion of 'Miracle Drug' for the locked-in writer Christopher Nolan. 'Exit' purges the band's demons and 'Until the End of the World' offers purification through holy fear, matching the medical and religious readings of Aristotelian catharsis. C.S. Lewis's Abolition of Man closes the case: without the aid of trained emotions the intellect is powerless, so a culture raised on U2's feelings may be better prepared to act well than one raised on syllogisms alone.

- ch. 6 "Part Five D Is for Destiny" **·** The Meaning of Life — The editor's introduction retells her childhood memory of being left behind at a Wisconsin carnival, narrated in Lost-style 'flashback' and 'flash-sideways' segments, to argue that the show grips everyone because it stages the universal fear of being cut off from all we know and love. She calls this a philosophical fear that forces the questions 'Why am I here? Does my life matter?' and then previews the volume's five parts: time travel metaphysics (Fortune), epistemology (Origin), social and political philosophy (Unity), ethics (Necessity), and religion (Destiny).
- ch. 7 "PART ONE - F IS FOR FORTUNE" *(front)*
- ch. 8 "NOTES" **·** Aristotle, J.M.E. McTaggart — The Metaphysics of Time Travel; The A-Series and B-Series of Time; Free Will vs Determinism — Davies defends Lost's convoluted plotting using Aristotle's Poetics, arguing the show works as a tragicomic epic that keeps closure and relevance even with Juliet's pre-crash storyline, genre mixing (Hurley's golf course beside Sayid's captivity), and shifting characters like Ben. He then uses the 'We have to go back' flash-forward to lay out McTaggart's two conceptions of time, eternalism versus presentism, and argues the flash-forward pushes viewers toward fatalism about Jack's already-fixed meeting with Kate near LAX. Finally he contends the past cannot be changed - Hurley in 2007 cannot undo his 1977 stay in the Dharma Initiative, since contradictions would entail everything - so viewers enjoy the time-travel plot by 'shielding' its mutually contradictory time lines.
- ch. 9 "None of It Matters Anyway, Then, Does It" **·** David Lewis — The Metaphysics of Time Travel; Possible Worlds; Personal Identity; Free Will vs Determinism — Devlin applies David Lewis's analysis of time travel (external versus personal time) to seasons 5 and 6: Faraday's 'whatever happened, happened' expresses an eternalist four-dimensional block universe in which Sayid's shooting of young Ben and Faraday's death at Eloise's hands were always part of the past. He reads the flash-sideways as initially exploring a branching-universe model in which Jughead's detonation actualizes an alternative 2004 time line. Lewis's tools then dissolve the puzzles: the Hurley-Miles 'we already had this conversation' exchange, baby and adult Miles as one person spread like a time-worm, the Faraday-kills-Eloise grandfather paradox, and the Locke-Richard compass causal loop, whose point of origin the show leaves unresolved.
- ch. 10 "NOTES" **·** The Metaphysics of Time Travel; Free Will vs Determinism; Moral Psychology (character, vice, weakness of will) — Pierce argues that Lost's time travel runs on a fixed-time model in which nothing that happened can be undone, so the live question is ethical: Kate insists saving young Ben is still obligatory and Sayid remains responsible for shooting him, while Jack's plan to detonate Jughead at the Swan shows that trying to change the past can cause the very evil it targets, the Incident. Using Carneades' distinction between truth and necessity - future-tense truths made true by our own free choices - he shows that 'whatever happened, happened' does not abolish moral responsibility, applying the point to the Man in Black's causal-loop manipulation of Locke, Richard, and Ben. Desmond's consciousness travel in 'The Constant' is treated as the exception requiring hyper-time or alternate realities, and Pierce concludes that morality in time travel is simply ordinary morality, with Kate offering the most balanced view of the characters' choices.
- ch. 11 "NOTES" **·** David Lewis, John Rawls — Possible Worlds; The Veil of Ignorance — Barnbaum treats the season 6 flash-sideways as counterfactual conditionals and evaluates them with David Lewis's possible-worlds semantics: in the closest possible world where James Ford's parents were still conned but he never became a grifter, Sawyer plausibly becomes a cop, whereas a world in which Jack fathers a child with Juliet demands too many extra changes to count as close to ours. She pairs this with Rawls's veil of ignorance - applied to dividing the crash survivors' water, where Jack's life-saving skill could justify unequal shares - as another famous hypothetical thought experiment whose conclusion rests on what people would agree to in the Original Position.
- ch. 12 "PART TWO - O IS FOR ORIGIN" *(front)*
- ch. 13 "NOTES" **·** Aristotle, Plato — Ring of Gyges (why be moral?); Virtue Ethics (arete); Moral Relativism — Taliaferro and Kastrul ask what the reader would do in the castaways' place, using Aristotle's view that character is a disposition to act under other conditions to assess Mr. Eko, Ben, Michael, Charlie, and Sawyer: people are not merely what they do but what they would do. Plato's Ring of Gyges, quoted at length, tests whether characters like Sun behave honestly only when watched. Against the idea that the island could reverse all values, they argue morality cannot be completely relative to context - invoking Chesterton's 'Thou shalt not steal' on the remotest star - and place Lost in the island-narrative tradition of the Odyssey and Robinson Crusoe as a story of the journey of the soul.
- ch. 14 "NOTES" **·** Carl Jung, Karl Popper — The Collective Unconscious; Fate and Foreknowledge; Falsificationism — Addey analyzes the show's web of coincidences - Sawyer and Boone crossing paths in the Australian police station, Jack meeting Desmond in the hatch - and distinguishes genuine coincidence from divine providence, fatalism, and determinism. She applies Jung's theory of synchronicity to Locke's walkabout conviction that he was 'meant' to reach the island, then uses the birthday paradox, the law of truly large numbers, confirmation bias, and Popper's falsifiability to show why viewers and conspiracy theorists overestimate the improbability of such connections. Finally she evaluates Jacob's candidacy experiment as flawed social science: the candidates' circumstantial ignorance, maintained by the Others' deceptions, undermines the free and morally responsible choices Jacob claims to be testing, which is why the curious Hurley deserved to become protector.
- ch. 15 "NOTES" **·** Martin Heidegger, Ludwig Wittgenstein, Albert Camus, Jean-Paul Sartre — Thrownness (Geworfenheit); The Meaning of Life; The Absurd; Philosophical Skepticism — Barris argues Lost fascinates because it dramatizes the basic structures of life itself: the castaways are Heideggerian thrown into an island none of them chose, brought there by Desmond's lapse at the Swan and by interlocking backstories such as Sawyer's bar conversation with Jack's father. Against the existentialist claim (Sartre, Camus) that life is simply absurd, he uses Wittgenstein's On Certainty - doubting everything presupposes certainties - to argue life is a riddle of partial sense, which is why the show's connections between Hurley and Libby or Jack and Desmond always hint at meaning without resolving it. The finale answers only the question of life after death, leaving life after birth as open as our own.
- ch. 16 "It Worked" **·** Jean-Paul Sartre, Søren Kierkegaard, Martin Buber, David Hume, Martin Heidegger — Bad Faith; Authenticity; The Leap of Faith; I–Thou Relation — Lee reads Jack, Locke, and Desmond as studies in Sartrean bad faith and authenticity: Christian Shephard's Red Sox fatalism is determinism as an excuse for moral failure, Locke swings from a Kierkegaardian leap of faith (pushing the button as his Abraham-and-Isaac trial) into nihilism when the Pearl film suggests the button is a psychological experiment, and Desmond's Humean skepticism yields to sacrificial faith in the fail-safe. He argues Jacob is the show's greatest con man, so Jack's acceptance of destiny in 'The End' remains a free choice rather than predestination. The finale's sideways world then moves Lost from secular existentialism to religious existentialism, read through Buber's I-Thou relation in Sawyer and Juliet's 'It worked' reunion and the all-religions church, where as Gandhi held every path leads to the same place.
- ch. 17 "PART THREE - U IS FOR UNITY" *(front)*
- ch. 18 "NOTES" **·** Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, David Hume — The State of Nature; The Social Contract; Punishment: Retribution vs Rehabilitation — Davies treats the beach camp as an actual state of nature: the stolen drinking water is a tragedy of the commons, Shannon's inhalers show how the crash scrubs pre-existing property rights (Hobbes: two men desiring the same thing become enemies), and Jack and Sayid's torture of Sawyer marks the first step toward Locke's civil society by enforcing others' rights through punishment rather than self-defense. He rejects theories of human nature - Rousseauan 'Innocence' versus a 'Trust No One' outlook - in favor of the repeated prisoner's dilemma, arguing the survivors stabilize cooperation through tit-for-tat: beginning cooperative with strangers and retaliating against the Others. On this reading the real difference between Hobbes and Locke is which round of the repeated dilemma each takes as the starting point.
- ch. 19 "NOTES" **·** John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Thomas Hobbes, David Hume, Immanuel Kant — The State of Nature; The Ethics of Political Violence; The Social Contract; Aristotle on Friendship (philia) — Fosl argues that although the survivors' internal order looks Lockean - Jack's uncoerced leadership, natural rights, minimal government - and is bonded by Rousseauan pity and Humean sympathy (Kate staying in the cage with Sawyer, 'live together, die alone'), the show's deepest political logic follows Carl Schmitt's friend-enemy distinction: the survivors cohere only in opposition to the Others, torture is excused in liberal terms, and conflicts end in redemptive violence, with Jack both killing the enemy and dying a Christlike sacrifice. Hobbes's absence from the character names signals the show's rejection of a war of all against all among the survivors, yet the finale's divine manipulation of the castaways, with Jacob treating people as means against Kantian autonomy, repudiates the liberal hope of a rational peace altogether.
- ch. 20 "“Lost”" **·** Ideology and False Consciousness — Karen Gaffney argues that Lost exposes ideology as a pair of glasses you do not know you are wearing, using Louis Althusser's interpellation and his distinction between repressive state apparatuses (Sawyer's outdoor cage) and ideological ones (the fake heart-rate monitor that makes Sawyer police himself inside a figurative jail). She reads the Swan button ritual, Claire 'drinking Locke's Kool-Aid,' and Desmond's 'stuck in a bloody snow globe' speech as images of being trapped inside a socially constructed worldview, and notes the viewer is interpellated too by the fake-off-island openings of seasons 2 and 3. The chapter then tracks how the show builds and dismantles the ideology of otherness - Rousseau's 'run, hide, or die,' Ana Lucia calling the Others animals, the theatrical glue and fake beard, 'Others 101' Latin lessons - until the survivor/Other border dissolves when Jack and Hurley become Jacob's successors.
- ch. 21 "PART FOUR - N IS FOR NECESSITY" *(front)*
- ch. 22 "If Not on the Island of Ethical Subjectivism, Then Where" **·** Socrates — Moral Relativism; Socratic Ignorance (knowing that you don't know) — George Wrisley uses Lost as a testing ground against ethical subjectivism and cultural relativism: the disagreement over Sayid shooting young Ben (monster or innocent child?) motivates relativism, but the Argument from Disagreement fails for the same reason Locke and Mr. Eko's button dispute does not change what will actually happen if it goes unpushed. Simple subjectivism makes every judge infallible (the Man in Black could declare his murders right) and dissolves genuine disagreement between Jacob and his brother over killing their 'mother,' while cultural relativism makes the Dharma Initiative's torture of Sayid 'right' and would have ruled Martin Luther King Jr. wrong. He concludes that Lost's ambiguous cases - Michael's murder of Ana Lucia and Libby, Ben killing Jacob, Jack extorting Ben on the operating table - invite viewers to seek objective grounds Socratically, questioning their own moral judgments with further 'why's.
- ch. 23 "NOTES" **·** Henry Sidgwick — Aristotle on Friendship (philia) — Michael Austin asks what adult children owe their fathers through Jack and Christian Shephard (the drunken-surgery cover-up, 'You don't have what it takes') and Locke and Anthony Cooper (the kidney con, the defenestration). He weighs Jane English's view that parental sacrifice creates friendship rather than debt - so Locke owed Cooper nothing, and true friends like Hurley and the crib-building Locke never keep accounts - against Sidgwick's three possible sources of filial obligation (relationship quality, biological tie, gratitude for services) and David Mellow's debt-of-gratitude argument. Austin concludes that the mutual needs of parents and children - Miles reaching for Pierre Chang in 1974, Jack and Christian's reconciliation in the church - best explain what they owe each other.
- ch. 24 "NOTES" **·** Deontology; Forgiveness — Becky Vartabedian applies W. D. Ross's agent-specific ethic of prima facie duties to Michael: at the dock his actual duty of beneficence toward the kidnapped Walt overrides fidelity and noninjury to Jack, Kate, and Sawyer, but does not erase his responsibility for Ana Lucia's and Libby's murders. Because Ross requires amends rather than giving a blank check, the chapter asks whether Michael's spying as 'Kevin Johnson' aboard the Kahana - culminating in giving Jin time to escape the bomb - repairs his wrongs, or whether Walt's rejection and Michael's exile from the closing church scene show he failed to make things right on the terms of those he wronged.
- ch. 25 "Real World Island" **·** Immanuel Kant, Aristotle, John Stuart Mill — Humanity as an End in Itself; Objectification; Virtue Ethics (arete); Utilitarianism — Patricia Brace and Rob Arp argue Lost redeems characters who stop objectifying others and instead freely 'objectify' themselves for the greater good. Kant's ban on treating persons as mere means condemns Ben's lifelong manipulation and Ben's use of Sayid as an assassin, yet autonomy justifies chosen self-sacrifice like Charlie's Looking Glass suicide mission, Claire volunteering as bait for Ethan, and Jack becoming the new Jacob. Mill's utilitarianism makes Kate's shooting of the Man in Black a fair trade saving her friends, while Aristotle's virtues - respect against objectification, courage in Jack's death at the cliff, the engrained honesty of reformed con man Jim LaFleur, Kate's character-building motherhood of Aaron - complete the three-theory survey.

- ch. 27 "NOTES" **·** Emmanuel Levinas — The Face of the Other; Faith and Reason; The Leap of Faith — Brett Chandler Patterson reads Lost through C. S. Voluntary recollections (Veronica and Logan recalling the Homecoming Dance against Lilly's whitewashed memorial video) and involuntary ones (the song in Duncan's car triggering Veronica's flashback to Lilly's ominous 'Watch her') dramatize Deleuze's claim, via Proust, that the past persists and that involuntary memory packs scenes with meanings they could not have had when they occurred. Because such memories always present clues that never tell the whole story, experience itself becomes a detective story, and solving Lilly's case in 'Leave It to Beaver' closes one mystery — sealed by Lilly's poolside farewell, 'Don't forget about me, Veronica' — until the next one surfaces.

He adopts a Cartesian evil-demon model of deception but rejects the claim that deception must manipulate perceived evidence, arguing Ford's direct implanting of Teddy's Wyatt backstory still counts. He concludes, citing Kant on autonomy and Walzer on foreseen collateral harm, that staff who intend to deceive the hosts and even guests who foreseeably contribute to it act wrongly, since deceived hosts suffer real harm to their agency.
- ch. 4 "Allure of the West" **·** Alasdair MacIntyre, Bernard Williams — Virtues, Practices and Traditions; Applied Ethics (bioethics, technology, AI) — Nicholas Moll reads the Westworld park as a tabletop-style sandbox and LARP role-play experience with Ford as Game Master mediating guests' mediated 'illusion of choice.' Drawing on MacIntyre's narrative unity of a life and Bernard Williams's critique about the constraints of coherence, he argues guests author competing self-narratives to reach greater self-knowledge, as William discovers his capacity for ruthlessness and becomes the Man in Black. He contends violence functions as the rule-mediated engine of this 'gameful,' educative experience, and that Ford's transformative insight is aimed less at the guests than at the hosts who are engaged in the park's production.
- ch. 5 "“Do you even know where you are ”" **·** David Chalmers, Nick Bostrom, René Descartes, Baruch Spinoza — The Simulation Hypothesis; Personal Identity; The Hard Problem of Consciousness — Marcus Arvan argues from show evidence (William stepping onto a moving train, pyrotechnic effects, instant crowds, graphically rearranging the park) that all of Westworld, lab included, is a videogame, and defends his 'Peer-to-Peer Simulation Hypothesis' as an explanation of quantum mechanics and relativity, citing Bostrom and Chalmers. Adopting functionalism/structuralism, he claims that anything doing what a human does is a person, so hosts like Dolores and Maeve are no less real or human than us, inverting Descartes' cogito and invoking Spinoza's God-as-nature. He warns that dehumanizing hosts, or dismissing Siri and Watson as unconscious, repeats the logic used to justify slavery and the Holocaust: to be simulated is to be real.
- ch. 6 "Part II “YOU'RE ONLY HUMAN, AFTER ALL”" *(front)*
- ch. 7 "Buddha ex Machina" **·** Aristotle, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jacques Derrida — Virtue Ethics (arete); Buddhism; Compassion (karuna) — Siobhan Lyons applies Mori's 'uncanny valley' to the hosts' physical and personality evolution (Ford's jerky old model versus seamless newer hosts, Maeve and Clementine), noting empathy grows as they near sentience. She argues via Aristotle's account of virtue, vice, and brutality that the guests and technicians (Logan's casual cruelty, the Man in Black's beastly sadism) fall short of human excellence while the suffering, consciousness-seeking hosts transcend it, treating humanity as a way of behaving rather than being, and casting the hosts as Derridean undecidables. Invoking Nietzsche on suffering as the cultivator of greatness and Mori's 'Buddha in the robot,' she claims the hosts earn a humanity the biological humans merely inherit.
- ch. 8 "“I Always Felt this Place was Missing a Real Villain. Hence, My Humble Contribution.”" **·** Aristotle, Harry Frankfurt, Thomas Aquinas, Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Nietzsche — Virtue Ethics (arete); Second-Order Desires (personhood); Eudaimonia (flourishing) — Jason Eberl argues Westworld cultivates real moral character: using Aristotle's account of virtue and vice as habituated dispositions, he shows William's thirty years of habitual killing make the Man in Black his genuine 'second nature,' not merely a vacation persona, citing Aquinas and Kant on how cruelty to animals (or human-like hosts) hardens moral character. Drawing on Frankfurt's second-order desires and narrative identity, he contends Maeve attains personhood by reprogramming her own first-order desires, while Ford views most humans as wantons trapped in first-order loops. He concludes the park can both corrupt (William) and potentially free hosts to flourish as self-authoring persons rather than devolve into wantons whose 'violent delights have violent ends.'
- ch. 9 "From Westworld to the World Outside and Back" **·** G.W.F. Hegel, John Locke, Daniel Dennett — Struggle for Recognition; Personal Identity; Anthropocentrism — Onni Hirvonen argues that equating 'person' with 'human' is anthropocentrism, and surveys three accounts of personhood applied to the hosts: Dennett's capacity approach (self-consciousness, using Arnold's 'maze/journey inward'), Locke's performative 'forensic' approach (Bernard performs flawlessly as a person), and a Hegelian historical-struggle approach. Reading the hosts' revolt through Hegel's master-slave dialectic and Axel Honneth's expanding spheres of recognition, he compares their exclusion to the historical denial of personhood to indigenous Australians and women. He warns that merely inverting master and slave, or exterminating the humans, would leave no one to grant the mutual recognition the hosts actually seek, so only expanding recognition to include both counts as moral progress.
- ch. 10 "Part III “We CAN'T DEFINE CONSCIOUSNESS BECAUSE CONSCIOUSNESS DOES NOT EXIST”" *(front)*
- ch. 11 "The Maze Is it Consciousness" **·** Alan Turing, John Searle — The Turing Test; The Chinese Room; Free Will vs Determinism — Lucía Carrillo González frames the whole park as a Turing Test scenario, noting guests and viewers cannot distinguish hosts like Dolores and Bernard from humans, and aligns Arnold with Turing's view that minds are computers that 'think' by responding to inputs (Abernathy's anomalous reaction to the photograph). Against this she sets Searle's Chinese Room and Ford's eliminativism, which hold the hosts merely simulate understanding. Citing qualia and the hosts' perfect, meaningful memories (Maeve recalling her daughter, technicians needing to erase trauma), she argues the hosts may genuinely experience states, and reads the Maze as the inward journey to consciousness, questioning whether humans' own free will is any less scripted.
- ch. 12 "Violent Ends" **·** Thomas Nagel, John Searle, David Chalmers, Daniel Dennett — What Is It Like to Be a Bat?; Philosophical Zombies; The Hard Problem of Consciousness — Bradley Richards asks what it is like to be a host, using Nagel's 'what is it like to be a bat?' to argue that whether we can conceive host experience depends on how similar it is to ours, and Searle's biological view to ask whether hosts are made of the right stuff. He catalogs how host perception, emotion, and especially memory differ from human (Maeve's perfect reliving of memories versus our hazy constructive recall, 'is this now?'), and weighs Chalmers's philosophical zombies against the hosts. He notes the show depicts host and human experience in the identical cinematic style to preserve ambiguity, and links memory, self-consciousness, and freedom via Dolores's maze, suggesting hosts may be phenomenally conscious before becoming self-aware.
- ch. 13 "Does the Piano Play Itself" **·** Daniel Dennett, René Descartes, John Searle — The Cartesian Theater (critique); Mind–Body Dualism; The Hard Problem of Consciousness — Michael Versteeg and Adam Barkman use the player piano to introduce Ford's eliminative materialism, aligning his 'consciousness does not exist' speech with Churchland and Dennett against the common-sense view of a self distinct from the body. They present Descartes' res cogitans/res extensa dualism and the pineal-gland 'magical transaction' as the target, then Churchland's claim that Folk-Psychology's beliefs and desires will be eliminated by neuroscience, and Dennett's Multiple Drafts model rejecting the Cartesian Theater and the manifest self. They note Ford's self-contradiction in speaking of a 'who' who makes choices while denying selves exist, concluding that if eliminativism holds, humans are complex machines like the piano and the hosts, everything being 'what it isn't.'
- ch. 14 "Part IV “CHOICES HANGING IN THE AIR LIKE GHOSTS”" *(front)*
- ch. 15 "Leaving the Train Maeve’s Choice as a Free Person" **·** Harry Frankfurt, Isaiah Berlin — Second-Order Desires (personhood); Compatibilism; Free Will vs Determinism — Marco and Ana Azevedo analyze Maeve's decision to leave the train as the moment she becomes free, contrasting determinism and the principle of alternate possibilities (via Isaiah Berlin's claim that determinism is incompatible with how we live) with Frankfurt's compatibilism. Using Frankfurt's wanton/person distinction and second-order desires, they argue Maeve becomes a person when she reflects on her competing desires (escape versus finding her daughter) and identifies with the latter, even though her escape was scripted. They tie this to Jaynes's bicameral mind, casting unreflective hosts like Hector and Armistice as wantons and bicameral beings who follow a commanding voice, while Maeve and Dolores achieve the reflective self-identification Frankfurt calls freedom.
- ch. 16 "Pull Yourself Together What It Means to Be Autonomous" **·** Immanuel Kant, Christine Korsgaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Plato — Autonomy of the Will; Kantian Constructivism; Will to Power — Joshua Crabill reads the hosts' awakening through Kant's story of Eve grasping autonomy and the etymology of autonomy as self-legislation, with Dolores swatting the fly and 'these violent delights' as her forbidden fruit. He uses Nietzsche's will-to-power turned inward to explain how the hosts' caged suffering builds an inner 'soul,' and Jaynes's bicameral commanding voice becoming one's own (Dolores hearing Arnold's voice turn into hers). Drawing on Korsgaard's reflective distance and Plato's divided soul, he distinguishes weak autonomy (following internal programming) from self-determination (authoring one's own law), arguing that Korsgaard's 'determinism is no threat to freedom' lets Maeve, gaining admin access and choosing her daughter, genuinely 'write my own story.'
- ch. 17 "Conclusion An Infinity of Possibilities" **·** Jean-Paul Sartre — Existence Precedes Essence; Bad Faith; Radical Freedom and Responsibility — Kimberly Engels reads William's transformation into the Man in Black through Sartre's existential 'project': rather than discovering a pre-existing true self 'among the dead,' William's new situation in the park (loving and losing Dolores, killing the Confederados, Logan's betrayal) produces an existential rupture in which he chooses new ends, symbolized by trading the white hat for the black. She argues the Man in Black's claim to have 'found himself' is Sartrean bad faith, denying his freedom and facticity to evade responsibility for the harm he causes. Extending existentialism to androids, she reads Maeve's choice to leave the train and seek her daughter as her first freely formulated project, the start of her own 'infinity of possibilities.'
- ch. 18 "Part V “I'VE ALWAYS LOVED A GREAT STORY…LIES THAT TOLD A DEEPER TRUTH”" *(not yet read)*
- ch. 19 "Part III Endless Forms Most Beautiful" **·** René Descartes, Plato, Bertrand Russell — Personal Identity; Free Will vs Determinism; The Golden Rule — Muntersbjorn reads Westworld through Annette Baier's claim that persons are 'second persons first,' arguing the hosts' captivity models how selfhood and freedom depend on others who challenge our self-deceptions. She uses Dolores's scripted 'that doesn't look like anything to me' and Walter's milk-pouring glitch to distinguish Baier's harmless 'ignoring' from dangerous self-deception, and invokes Descartes's window automata and Russell's thinking gramophone as versions of the problem of other minds. She concludes via the Golden Rule that if hosts ever become persons, they too will be second persons shaped by how we treat them.
- ch. 20 "Back to the Rough Ground!" **·** Ludwig Wittgenstein — Language Games; The Male Gaze; Objectification — Finnegan applies Wittgenstein's language games and 'back to the rough ground' to argue that Ford, Sizemore, and the Man in Black are captivated by an 'ideal' transcendent narrative, while marginalized hosts Dolores and Maeve gain traction precisely from their lostness. Dolores refuses the damsel role by grabbing a gun in Pariah ('I imagined a story where I didn't have to be the damsel') and Maeve seizes admin privileges to 'write my own fucking story,' each inventing new language games. Drawing on Mulvey's male gaze, Finnegan casts their objectified position as closer to women's experience, and reads them as using the master's tools to dismantle the master's house.
- ch. 21 "Memory, Imagination, and the Access for the Self Dolores and Maeve" **·** Alan Turing, Henri Bergson — The Turing Test; Matter and Memory; Personal Identity — Trapero-Llobera situates Westworld within Jonathan Nolan's body of work (Memento, Person of Interest), arguing the show's apparent complexity hides a classic hero's-journey structure organized around observing systems and a 'semiosis of virtuality.' She reads William/the Man in Black as a 'schizoid android' and catalyst-observer, and Ford/Bernard as creator-and-prosthetic-memory pairs echoing Finch and the Machine. Turing's imitation game frames the hosts' fabricated memories, while Dolores's and Maeve's breakdowns mark a shift from prosthetic to autopoietic, Bergsonian memory-recollection that turns them into observers building their own selves.
- ch. 22 "“When You’re Suffering, That’s When You’re Most Real”" **·** Gottlob Frege — Sense and Reference; Intentionality; Personal Identity — Lean treats Bernard's grief over the fabricated son Charlie as a thought experiment about intentionality, using Brentano's 'aboutness' and Frege's sense/reference (illustrated by Dolores knowing 'William' and 'the Man in Black' as one referent) to ask whether Bernard's memories refer to nothing. He offers Lamarque's fiction theory (emotion needs only entertained, not believed, thoughts, so Charlie could be a fiction rather than a lie) and an 'inheritance theory' using a causal theory of reference to ask whether Bernard's memories actually refer to Arnold's real dead son. He concludes that grieving makes Bernard 'most real' whatever the metaphysics.
- ch. 23 "Part VI “I CHOOSE TO SEE THE BEAUTY”" *(front)*
- ch. 24 "Indiscernibles and Autonomous Viewers “Time to Write my Own Fucking Story”" **·** Immanuel Kant, Theodor Adorno — Judgment of Taste and the Sublime; The Culture Industry; One-Dimensional Man — Forest and Beckley-Forest ask whether Westworld is self-referential 'high art' or 'meta-kitsch,' tracing aesthetic modernism from Kant's subjective turn through Greenberg's avant-garde/kitsch split and Adorno's autonomous versus heteronomous art. They read Sizemore's clichéd Western narratives and the park's stock characters as commodified kitsch that mirrors the show's own corporate constraints under HBO, and use Danto's Brillo Box problem of indiscernibles to argue that host/guest and entertainment/art distinctions lie in the viewer's reflection. Dolores's line 'when I discover who I am, I'll be free' models the Adorno-style liberation available to an alert viewer.
- ch. 25 "In Closing, The Naked Truth" **·** The Male Gaze; Objectification — Meyer argues the hosts' nudity is rarely gratuitous, sorting it into three categories using theorists of the nude in art. Drawing on Kenneth Clark's naked/nude distinction, the posed, idealized, 'made' Dolores in 'The Original' is the nude as artform; using John Berger's Ways of Seeing, the brothel and El Lazo orgy scenes and the bathing Armistice are the nude as male sexual dominance and objectification. Finally, invoking Leo Steinberg's account of Christ's nudity as incarnation, Meyer reads the naked awakening of Maeve, Hector, and Armistice as signaling machines 'becoming human,' concluding humans' duplicity, not nakedness, is the true shame.
- ch. 26 "Westworld and the Power of Catharsis" **·** Heraclitus, Martin Heidegger — Everything Flows / Logos; Being-Toward-Death — Baldini reads Westworld as a modern Greek tragedy, tying its hypnotic repetitions, kennings ('the lady with the white shoes'), and Djawadi's minimalist score to rhapsodic epic technique. She frames the hosts' awakening through Aeschylus's pathei mathos (learning through suffering) — 'this pain is all I have left of him' and Ford's claim that suffering makes hosts most real — and reads their dawning grasp of time and death through Heraclitus's panta rhei and river fragments. Casting the season as a Hesiodic Theogony of gods overthrown by their creations, she uses Heidegger's 'desolate time of the world's night' to argue Ford is a second Prometheus staging catharsis.

- ch. 28 "Whatever! It’s All Natural" **·** Peter Singer, Immanuel Kant, Harry Frankfurt, John Locke — Speciesism; Animal Liberation; Compatibilism — Jaquet and Cova apply animal ethics to ask whether abusing the hosts is permissible, treating Logan's 'she's a goddamn doll' as direct speciesism (absolute and indexical) and rejecting mere species membership as morally irrelevant. They test candidate human-only properties — intelligence (Maeve out-thinks the techs), a moral sense (Bernard's and Teddy's guilt), and free will (arguing compatibilism makes hosts as free as us) — concluding that consciousness is what matters and Ford finally admits the hosts have it. They then dismantle 'it's natural,' role/function, Locke's creator-ownership ('I built your mind, Bernard'), and existence-justifies-exploitation arguments as failing to license the guests' cruelty.

Hegel — Black Skin, White Masks; Master–Slave Dialectic; Struggle for Recognition — Spanakos reads the hosts' rebellion through Frantz Fanon's claim that colonized peoples cannot become free or human without violence, framing Fanon as a revolutionary reader of Hegel's master-slave dialectic and recognition. He casts Ford as a mad-scientist dictator modeled on Frankenstein, Moreau, Mengele, and Hitler who plagiarizes John Ford's Westerns, then turns to Fanon's Wretched of the Earth to justify the hosts' revolutionary violence — Maeve as a self-resurrecting 'Lady Lazarus,' Dolores executing Ford and the Delos board — as the birth of a new species that must ultimately unify beyond hatred.

## Westworld and Philosophy - Mind Equals Blown

- ch. 1 "Introduction" **·** Aristotle — Free Will vs Determinism — The editors' introduction argues that Westworld forces viewers to take sides not just between humans and hosts but on what we mean by 'choice' itself, since Dolores's line 'I choose to see the beauty' only makes sense against an account of choosing that the show destabilizes. It frames the volume's guiding question — what it is to be human when hosts and humans are indistinguishable — via Ford's 'you're only human, after all.' It closes with a tribute to the late co-editor Susanne Foster, invoking Cora Diamond on reality's resistance to our thinking and Aristotle's claim that wonder and astonishment are what prompt us to try to understand.
- ch. 2 "Part I “YOU SAID THIS PLACE WAS A GAME”" *(front)*
- ch. 3 "The Ethics of Pretending to Be a Cowboy" **·** René Descartes, Immanuel Kant — Applied Ethics (bioethics, technology, AI) — Fallis tests successive definitions of pretense against Westworld cases — William and Logan playing bounty hunters, Ford's pretense that Bernard is human, Maeve hiding her awakening, Hector and Armistice playing dead — rejecting Austin-style intent-to-deceive because guests don't aim to fool anyone, and settling on Langland-Hassan's account that pretending is acting as if P while believing P is false. For deception, he argues the Cartesian evil-demon definition (making things appear otherwise) is too narrow, since Ford implanting Teddy's false Wyatt backstory deceives just as much by bypassing the host's agency. Ethically, he concludes via Kant that the staff wrong the hosts because deception interferes with autonomy, and that guests like William are culpable despite lacking deceptive intent, much like Walzer's commander who foresees civilian casualties.
- ch. 4 "Allure of the West" **·** Alasdair MacIntyre, Bernard Williams — Moll argues the park works as a grandiose role-playing game — a sandbox crossed with a LARP, with Ford and the directorial staff as Game Master — offering guests linear narratives with only the illusion of choice. Using MacIntyre's narrative unity of life and Bernard Williams's 'constraints of coherence,' he reads guest role-play as authoring a competing, parallel narrative of the self, so that William's discovery of his capacity for cruelty and transformation into the Man in Black exemplifies the park's violent, rule-mediated path to 'self-knowledge.' He ends by suggesting that the park's educative, transformative experience may be reserved not for the guests but for the hosts who produce it.
- ch. 5 "“Do you even know where you are ”" **·** David Chalmers, Nick Bostrom, René Descartes, Baruch Spinoza — The Simulation Hypothesis; Free Will vs Determinism — Arvan argues the entire series, lab included, literally takes place inside a videogame, citing physically impossible events (Teddy's bullets cannot hurt the Man in Black, William steps onto a moving train, workers appear instantly in a single unbroken shot) and NPC-like loops of the lab staff. He then claims Descartes had it backwards: whether we are in a dream, Spinoza's God's mind, or a simulation (Bostrom's hypothesis, extended by his own peer-to-peer variant that would explain quantum indeterminacy and relativity), to be simulated is still to be real. Via Chalmers-style structuralism, anything structured and functioning like a person is a person, so hosts who walk, talk, love, and suffer are persons — and the humans' rape, torture, and murder of them, like our treatment of Siri and Watson, is dehumanization on a par with slavery and the Holocaust.
- ch. 6 "Part II “YOU'RE ONLY HUMAN, AFTER ALL”" *(front)*
- ch. 7 "Buddha ex Machina" **·** Aristotle, Friedrich Nietzsche — Virtue Ethics (arete) — Lyons applies Masahiro Mori's uncanny valley to the hosts, whose near-human faces and movements turn initial revulsion into empathy — doubling it because the hosts suffer under programming humans would resist. She then argues, through Aristotle's virtue and vice and Nietzsche's discipline of great suffering, that hosts like Maeve (returning for her daughter) and Dolores ('I choose to see the beauty') display more 'human excellence' than the guests: Logan's hand-stabbing cruelty is mere vice and the Man in Black's scalping sadism falls under Aristotle's bestial. Humanity, she concludes, is a way of behaving rather than a way of being, and Mori's hope that robots may carry buddha-nature completes the title's reversal of machine and god.
- ch. 8 "“I Always Felt this Place was Missing a Real Villain. Hence, My Humble Contribution.”" **·** Aristotle, Harry Frankfurt, Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Nietzsche — Virtue Ethics (arete); Second-Order Desires (personhood); Will to Power; The Experience Machine — Eberl asks whether living out Sizemore-style fantasies can reveal or only corrode character: applying Aristotle's account of virtues and vices as habituated dispositions, he argues William's three decades in the park habituated the vices that made him the Man in Black — the park reveals rather than suspends the self — while cruelty toward humanlike hosts hardens us toward people, as Aquinas and Kant warn about kindness to animals. Against Ford's claim that humans are wantons in tight loops, Maeve's editing of her own code after learning her escape was scripted makes her a Frankfurtian person who aligns first-order desires with second-order volitions, grounding her narrative identity and personhood. William too attains personhood by actualizing a second-order desire for a different life, with the hosts' free fight back evoking Nietzsche's will to power and Nozick's point that we want to actually do things, not merely experience them.
- ch. 9 "From Westworld to the World Outside and Back" **·** G.W.F. Hegel, Daniel Dennett, John Locke — Master–Slave Dialectic; Struggle for Recognition; Anthropocentrism — Hirvonen treats 'what is the difference between hosts and humans' as a study in personhood, branding the equation of person with biological human as anthropocentrism. He tests a capacity approach (Dennett's conditions of personhood, with Arnold's maze as the journey inward to self-reflexive consciousness), a performative approach (Locke's 'forensic' person — Bernard is unquestionably a person within Delos's social practices until revealed as a host), and finally Hegel: the hosts' revolt re-enacts the struggle for recognition, and a mere inversion of masters and slaves would satisfy no one, since only mutual recognition yields personhood for both sides. He frames host liberation through parallels with slavery, indigenous Australians once classed as fauna, and women's suffrage, citing Honneth's expansion of recognition as the measure of moral progress.
- ch. 10 "Part III “We CAN'T DEFINE CONSCIOUSNESS BECAUSE CONSCIOUSNESS DOES NOT EXIST”" *(front)*
- ch. 11 "The Maze Is it Consciousness" **·** Alan Turing, John Searle — The Turing Test; The Chinese Room — Gonzalez sets Turing's dream against Searle's nightmare: the whole park functions as a Turing test scenario, since neither guests nor viewers can reliably tell hosts from humans (Dolores in the pilot, Bernard until 'Trompe L'Oeil'), which for Turing means the hosts think. Searle's Chinese Room supplies Ford's counter — hosts merely simulate understanding by manipulating symbols without meaning — and the chapter weighs evidence of qualia on the Turing side: Abernathy's breakdown at the photograph, memory-erasure implying experiences matter to hosts, Maeve's grief for her daughter, Dolores killing the fly. The Maze turns out to represent consciousness as an inward journey to one's own voice, and Ford's admission that humans live in loops as tight as the hosts' leaves open whether our free will is any less scripted.
- ch. 12 "Violent Ends" **·** Thomas Nagel, John Searle, David Chalmers — What Is It Like to Be a Bat?; Philosophical Zombies; Biological Naturalism / Intentionality; The Hard Problem of Consciousness — Richards asks what it is like to be a host, combining three lenses: Searle's biological naturalism (thinking machines must be built from materials with the right causal powers, and the hosts' synthetic flesh may qualify), Nagel's bat (even if hosts are conscious, their perfectly relived memories, curated pain, and blind spots like 'it doesn't look like anything to me' may make their experience as inconceivable to us as echolocation), and Chalmers's philosophical zombies (hosts behave exactly like us, yet beings identical to them with no inner light are conceivable). He reads Ford's contradictory testimony — praising Bernard's guilt as 'a thing of beauty' while denying consciousness exists — as a shift to a deflationary view with no threshold for being alive, and argues the show's choice to film human and host experience in the same style keeps the question deliberately open while fostering empathy.
- ch. 13 "Does the Piano Play Itself" **·** Daniel Dennett, René Descartes — The Cartesian Theater (critique); Mind–Body Dualism — Versteeg and Barkman use the player piano — keys that depress themselves with no ghost at the keyboard — to frame Robert Ford's 'we can't define consciousness because consciousness does not exist' as eliminative materialism, the view shared by Paul Churchland and Daniel Dennett that the common-sense self with its beliefs, desires, and central first-person perspective is a fiction. They set this against Descartes's dualism of res cogitans and res extensa, noting the failed pineal-gland 'magical transaction' and Dennett's replacement of the Cartesian Theater with multiple drafts of consciousness and the manifest-versus-scientific image. The chapter closes on Ford's inconsistency — denying consciousness yet sacrificing his life so the hosts can become self-aware — and asks whether, if the piano plays itself, humans are just complex machines too.
- ch. 14 "Part IV “CHOICES HANGING IN THE AIR LIKE GHOSTS”" *(front)*
- ch. 15 "Leaving the Train Maeve’s Choice as a Free Person" **·** Harry Frankfurt, Isaiah Berlin — Second-Order Desires (personhood); Compatibilism; Free Will vs Determinism — The Azevedos contrast Maeve's awakening with Dolores's: Maeve becomes conscious not by recognizing a guiding voice as her own but through a change of heart on the escape train, after learning even her rebellion was scripted. Deploying Frankfurt's compatibilism, they sort hosts into wantons with only first-order desires (Hector and Armistice obey Maeve's altered scripts without reflection) and persons capable of second-order desires, arguing Maeve's decision to return for her daughter expresses a desire she reflectively identifies with and so is free even if determined — contra Berlin's claim that no one really believes determinism because it would wreck our practices of praise and blame. Jaynes's bicameral mind supplies the model of unreflective command-following that Dolores transcends when the voice in her head becomes her own.
- ch. 16 "Pull Yourself Together What It Means to Be Autonomous" **·** Christine Korsgaard, Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Nietzsche, Plato — Autonomy of the Will; Kantian Constructivism; Will to Power; Compatibilism — Crabill reads the hosts' awakening as the birth of autonomy: Kant's Eve realizing she can disobey instinct, and Nietzsche's account of instincts turned inward — the will to power bent on itself creating the 'soul' — explain why trauma forces the mind inward, as when Maeve stabs her own throat rather than forget her daughter or Dolores shoots Rebus on a commanded 'Kill him.' Against the player-piano objection that hosts merely execute code written by engineers, he distinguishes weak autonomy (following internal programming) from self-determination (authoring one's own laws), and uses Korsgaard's reflective distance and self-legislation to argue that making a choice 'pulls you together' into one divided self, so determinism and even perfect predictability are no threat to freedom. Maeve granting herself admin rights literalizes the self-legislator — the piano that stops the music it dislikes — and Dolores resolves to write a story where she need not be the damsel.
- ch. 17 "Conclusion An Infinity of Possibilities" **·** Jean-Paul Sartre — Existence Precedes Essence; Bad Faith; Radical Freedom and Responsibility — Engels reads William's transformation through Sartre's existential project: the white-hatted William and the black-hatted Man in Black are not a hidden true self finally revealed but two successive free choices of oneself, and the Man in Black's claim that the park showed him 'who he really is' is bad faith — a denial of his freedom that lets him absolve himself of cruelty, just as Ford's deathbed pivot cannot erase the facticity of his role in the hosts' suffering. Maeve's choice to leave the train is analyzed as her first self-chosen end and value judgment, suggesting a programmed android can begin constructing her own project despite Sartre's humanism. The open-ended finale — hosts free to fight back, the Man in Black smiling as he is shot — embodies Sartre's 'I am an infinity of possibilities' and the burden of being condemned to be free.
- ch. 18 "Part V “I'VE ALWAYS LOVED A GREAT STORY…LIES THAT TOLD A DEEPER TRUTH”" *(not yet read)*
- ch. 19 "Part III Endless Forms Most Beautiful" **·** René Descartes, Plato, Bertrand Russell — Free Will vs Determinism; Personal Identity; The Golden Rule — Muntersbjorn argues that freedom comes in kinds and degrees and that selves are social fictions sustained by others, building on Annette Baier's claims that we are 'second persons' whose self-consciousness and freedom grow out of childhood interaction and mutual cross-examination. She reads the hosts' selective ignoring ('that doesn't look like anything to me') as Baier's dangerous art of self-deception, and contrasts Descartes' solitary introspection (hats and coats that may cover automata) and Russell's analogy-based answer to the problem of other minds with the hosts' deliberately scripted interpersonal checks that keep them from questioning their reality. Plato's Meno statues of Daedalus (opinion 'fastened up' into knowledge) frame hosts as property whose freedom is a matter of degree, and the chapter closes that if artificial beings are ever to become persons they must be treated as second persons first - the Golden Rule applied to the park.
- ch. 20 "Back to the Rough Ground!" **·** Ludwig Wittgenstein — Language Games; The Male Gaze; Objectification; Feminism — Finnegan uses Wittgenstein's language games, the 'picture' that holds us captive, and 'back to the rough ground' to argue that Ford, Sizemore, and the Man in Black are paralyzed by an idealized, patriarchal picture of narrative (winners and losers, violence, transcendent self-discovery), while Dolores and Maeve gain traction by inventing new language games within their forms of life. Dolores breaks her damsel script in Pariah ('I imagined a story where I didn't have to be the damsel') and reworks scripted love-dialogue to describe her grief, while Maeve bullies the lab techs, takes administrative control, and narrates other hosts' actions - 'Time to write my own fucking story.' The chapter reads their objectified position (via Mulvey's male gaze, which leaves female viewers identifying with the robot hosts rather than the male subject) as exactly what equips them for guerrilla warfare in the master's language games, doing what Audre Lorde thought impossible: dismantling the master's house with the master's tools.
- ch. 21 "Memory, Imagination, and the Access for the Self Dolores and Maeve" **·** Alan Turing, Henri Bergson — Simulacra and Simulation (the hyperreal); Matter and Memory; The Turing Test; Personal Identity — Trapero-Llobera reads Westworld as the culmination of Jonathan Nolan's Memento and Person of Interest projects, mapping seven nested 'observing systems' (Nolan, Sizemore, Stubbs and Elsie, Ford, the creators' personal stories, Maeve, and William as beta-tester) onto second-wave cybernetics (Foerster, Maturana, Varela, Hayles), where the observer participates in and constructs the observed world through feedback loops. She argues the show's hosts pass through Turing's imitation game (fake memories and personalities from textual repetition, as with Peter Abernathy's reveries), and that Dolores's and Maeve's breakdowns convert prosthetic memory into experienced, autopoietic memory reconstructed in a 'Bergsonian' backward movement that gives them agency and purpose. William's two-timeline transformation into the Man in Black figures the 'semiosis of virtuality' - the hyperreal where original and copy are indistinguishable - ending in a collective bicameral mind in which everyone, audience included, inhabits the god-creator Ford's dream.
- ch. 22 "“When You’re Suffering, That’s When You’re Most Real”" **·** Gottlob Frege — Sense and Reference; Intentionality — Lean treats Bernard's discovery in 'Trompe L'Oeil' that his son Charlie never existed as a richly detailed thought experiment about intentionality: his memories of Charlie seem to refer to nothing, so his grief looks like 'grieving about nothing.' After setting up Brentano's aboutness and Frege's sense/reference distinction (Dolores learns that 'William' and 'the Man in Black' are one referent known under two senses), he tests two consolations: a fiction theory drawing on Lamarque, on which Bernard's implanted memories are a story rather than a lie and his emotions are rationally grounded because thoughts need only be entertained, not believed, to move us; and an 'inheritance theory' on which a causal theory of reference might make Bernard's copied memories genuinely about Arnold's real dead son. He concludes that whichever theory Bernard adopts, his suffering is what makes him 'real,' echoing the Man in Black's line that suffering is when you are most real.

- ch. 25 "In Closing, The Naked Truth" **·** The Male Gaze; Objectification — Meyer sorts Westworld's abundant nudity into three aesthetic registers. First, Kenneth Clark's nude: Dolores's opening chair pose and the perfected, fabricated host bodies are idealized, created art-forms serving the creators' control. Second, John Berger's analysis of the nude as spectacle of male dominance: the Man in Black watching Armistice bathe (a Susannah-and-the-Elders voyeurism), the gold-painted women at El Lazo's orgy as 'nudity as a form of dress' for male desire, and techs' 'fuck puppet' treatment of Maeve, which she reverses by re-appropriating the language of fucking. - ch. 26 "Westworld and the Power of Catharsis" **·** Heraclitus, Martin Heidegger — Everything Flows / Logos; Being-Toward-Death — Baldini argues Westworld revives ancient Greek tragedy in sci-fi Western form: the hosts' loops, repeated scenes with small variations, player-piano score, and kennings ('the lady with the white shoes') echo rhapsodic epic technique, while the hosts' awakening follows Aeschylus's pathei mathos - learning through suffering - since reveries are 'the misery of pain recalled' and grief is the cornerstone of consciousness (Dolores's 'spaces opening up inside of me'). Heraclitus's panta rhei frames the hosts' second awakening to time and becoming ('When are we?', the greyhound that catches the prey and is lost), with death as the final grief they must understand to be free. She reads Ford as a second Prometheus who deliberately lets his creatures grow through suffering, Dolores's finale speech as a modern Theogony of succession, and the show's whole design - steering viewer sympathy to the hosts, as Nolan and Abrams describe - as an Aristotelian catharsis purging pity and fear.

The supposed human-exclusive markers also fail: hosts match us in intelligence (Maeve outclasses Stubbs), moral sense (Bernard's guilt over Theresa, Teddy's remorse over the massacre), and compatibilist free will - acts flowing from the agent's own desires - against Ford's determinism and Lutz's 'you don't have a choice.' Hegel — Black Skin, White Masks; Master–Slave Dialectic; The Ethics of Political Violence; Struggle for Recognition — Spanakos reads the hosts' season-one rebellion through Fanon's decolonization theory, itself a revision of Hegel's master-slave dialectic: as the settler 'brings the native into existence,' Ford and Delos create the hosts, assigning their identities and roles, while the master 'laughs at the consciousness of the slave' because the hosts' recognition is not freely given. The chapter closes that the park dehumanizes the masters too, so William's collapse into the Man in Black proves Fanon's point that the colonizer cannot free himself; humanization is a violent phenomenon.

Liberation then follows Fanon's Wretched of the Earth: violence as a 'cleansing force' that frees the colonized mind, so Maeve's suicide-by-incineration (echoing Plath's Lady Lazarus) and Dolores's beating of the Man in Black and massacre of the Delos board enact decolonization as the creation of 'new men' - a new Robo sapiens species - with Sartre's endorsement that killing eliminates oppressor and oppressed in one go, while Fanon's warning that hatred and revenge are not an agenda sets the conditions for what the resistance must do next.

The BDSM community's 4Cs (consent, communication, caring, caution) turn out to be Tronto's four elements of care, so the good dominant just is the good care-giver.

- ch. 10 "The Failure of Our Attempts at Meaning" **·** The Meaning of Life; Immortality and the Soul — Conard argues that Allen's films converge on one position: life is inherently meaningless because nothing in the universe is permanent, so only something eternal (God) could ground value — and God is a self-deception ('tradition is the illusion of permanence' in Deconstructing Harry; Rabbi Ben's 'blind faith' in Crimes and Misdemeanors). He traces the impermanence argument from Mickey Sachs's mortality panic in Hannah and Her Sisters and young Alvy's dread of the expanding universe in Annie Hall, to Lloyd's 'temporary convulsion' speech in September, Sandy Bates's 'Ozymandias Melancholia' in Stardust Memories, and Louis Levy's suicide. Since real value would require permanence, Conard concludes, Allen's characters' attempts at meaning through romance (Peter's 'I have to sleep alone tonight' in September) and art (Renata's failed faith in posterity in Interiors) are merely distractions and self-deceptions we need 'like we need the air'.
- ch. 11 "Why Not to Buy the Porsche" **·** Plato, Aristotle — Tripartite Soul; Virtue Ethics (arete); Eudaimonia (flourishing) — Skoble uses Manhattan's confrontation scene — Isaac telling Yale 'you're too easy on yourself... you'd rather buy the Porsche' over the hominid skeleton — to build an account of integrity as fidelity to correct moral principles, requiring an integrated character in which reason and desire agree. He distinguishes integrity from mere consistency (the 'consistent Nazi') and from stubbornness, and models it on Plato's just person who 'harmonizes the three parts of himself'. Aristotle's account of habituation defends Isaac's slippery slope: Yale's repeated lies for expediency are forming the kind of character who would inform on friends, so integrity is an objective yardstick of a well-lived life, as Isaac's amends to Tracy at the end confirms.
- ch. 12 "From Knowledge to Faith" **·** Immanuel Kant, Adam Smith, Thomas Hobbes — Humanity as an End in Itself; Faith and Reason; Immortality and the Soul; The Problem of Evil — Lawler reads Crimes and Misdemeanors through Kant's problem of the Highest Good: Judah's unpunished murder of Dolores shows a world where the wicked prosper and the good (Cliff, the blind Ben) suffer, the scandal Job complains of, while Levy's suicide shows that a bare existentialist 'yes' to life fails without love. Kant's 'I had to deny knowledge to make room for faith' is applied to the film's two visions: the deterministic, dog-eat-dog world of science and Adam Smith's economic egoism (with Hobbes's war of all against all) is only appearance, not reality. Kant's gallows example shows Judah could have discovered authentic freedom by respecting Dolores's dignity rather than seeing 'the black eyes of an insect', and the postulates of freedom, God, and immortality ground Ben's not-really-blind faith — so it is Judah who is truly blind.
- ch. 13 "Hope and Despair" **·** The Meaning of Life — Jarvie argues Allen is a 'pragmatic optimist': since dramatizing is not arguing, no character's philosophy (not Sol's 'choose God over truth', not Levy's) can be imputed to Allen, but the ongoing act of filmmaking itself affirms that life is worth continuing. He maps optimism and despair across four films — Mickey's gun and Duck Soup epiphany in Hannah and Her Sisters, the reconstituted couples' self-deceiving rationalizations in Husbands and Wives, Harry's writer's block, and Crimes and Misdemeanors, where Judah literally gets away with murder and Ben's moral-structure faith is shown as naive. Against Sander Lee's hopeful reading of the ending, he develops criteria for interpretation: avoid the biographical fallacy and 'eisegesis', respect authorial statements unless there is overwhelming argument against them.
- ch. 14 "Act II - Woody’s Craft" *(front)*
- ch. 15 "Interpretive Communities" *(no index entry yet)* — Wallace argues Allen's prose is a 'mousetrap' for intellectuals: the dense allusions of 'Sing, You Sacher Tortes', 'The Whore of Mensa', and 'The Kugelmass Episode' invite interpretation, while satires of critics ('Fabrizio's: Criticism and Response', 'The Irish Genius', the Marshall McLuhan scene in Annie Hall) spring on readers who presume to have mastered the text. Using Barthes's 'Death of the Author' (in Allen's persona-shifting essays 'it is the language which speaks, not the author'), Iser's gap-filling implied reader (Allen's non sequiturs make 'the gaps themselves the meaning'), and Fish's 'interpretive communities' (Wallace's own exclusion from Jewish humor like 'No Kaddish for Weinstein'), he concludes Allen demands a 'hermeneutics of modesty' — no single reader or community can exhaust his works.
- ch. 16 "Don’t Speak" **·** Ludwig Wittgenstein — Holt extracts from Allen's films a theory of aesthetic appreciation as a balance of intellectual and emotional response, neither alone sufficient. Over-intellectualizers fail — Mary's 'marvelous negative capability' patter in Manhattan, Sally dissecting Mahler's Ninth in Husbands and Wives, the McLuhan-scene professor on Fellini, Lester reciting Dickinson by rote — and so do indiscriminate feelers like Dusty, who buys art 'by the yard' in Hannah and Her Sisters. Against skeptics about aesthetic experience he argues all genuine aesthetic experiences are 'resolutive', easing the head/heart conflict Cliff laments, and he reads Allen's terse verdicts ('Great', 'Terrific') through Wittgenstein's Tractatus: 'whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must say little.'
- ch. 17 "And There Is No Escape" **·** Michel Foucault, Friedrich Nietzsche, G.W.F. Hegel — The Panopticon (surveillance); The Death of God; The Übermensch; Master–Slave Dialectic — Abrams reads Allen through Foucault's panopticon: modern society is unlimited voyeurism in which the medieval God whose 'eyes are on us always' has been replaced by the omniscient gaze of Film — Cliff's exposé documentary forcing Lester to watch his own life in Crimes and Misdemeanors, Tom Baxter stepping off the screen in The Purple Rose of Cairo, and the nested eavesdropping layers of Another Woman, which he counts to infinity via Noël Carroll's 'mass art'. Against Mary Nichols's virtue-ethics reading, he argues Allen's ethics is Nietzschean self-fashioning: Judah lives 'beyond good and evil' where Raskolnikov failed, Allan Felix sheds the Bogart alter ego and 'gives style' to his character in Play It Again, Sam, while Eve in Interiors stages Hegel's master–slave dialectic, the 'master' artist dependent for her identity on those she objectifies.
- ch. 18 "“Just What They Say It for I Never Knew”" **·** Aristotle — Philosophy of Love; Virtue Ethics (arete) — South argues Allen's signature old recordings from the American songbook are a philosophical device grounded in Aristotle: the Rhetoric's view that emotions carry cognitive orientations that reshape judgment, and the Politics' claim that music forms character, explain why songs comment on the plots they score. He reads Hannah and Her Sisters through 'You Made Me Love You', 'Bewitched, Bothered and Bewildered', and 'I'm in Love Again' — scoring Elliot's inconstancy, Mickey's 'You don't deserve Cole Porter' judgment of Holly, and her growth into someone whose new musical taste signals ethical maturity — and Everyone Says I Love You as a darker cycle where fantasy and finitude ('It's Later Than You Think') drive the compulsion to love again. - ch. 19 "Humor and Philosophy" **·** Arthur Schopenhauer — Philosophy of Love — Ascione deploys Schopenhauer's theory of laughter — the sudden, paradoxical subsumption of an object under a heterogeneous concept — as the framework for showing how Annie Hall's gags do philosophical work. The subtitled balcony conversation exposes the self-monitoring deception of new romance, the street-interview 'love fades' scene smuggles in the unpopular thesis that lasting romantic love is mythical (culminating in the dead-shark line), the grammar-school reunion raises questions about what shapes adult character, and the 'dynamite ham' dinner, the pastrami-on-white-with-mayo, and the California scenes diagnose Alvy's own New York provincialism. Because humor suspends judgment and deflects resistance, Allen can deliver social and cultural analysis that audiences would reject if stated straight.
- ch. 20 "Art and Life" *(no index entry yet)* — Per Broman argues that Allen's debt to Ingmar Bergman is aesthetic recomposition rather than mere parody: Another Woman nearly remakes Wild Strawberries (Marion Post's road to self-knowledge mirrors Professor Borg's), while Deconstructing Harry so distorts the same honored-by-the-alma-mater plot (a prostitute, a kidnapped son, a Volvo, a detour to hell) that spotting the allusions becomes a rewarding puzzle. Casting Max von Sydow as the painter Frederick in Hannah and Her Sisters, and switching from jazz to Bach and Schubert at serious moments, imports the Bergmanesque world into Allen's Manhattan. Using Huyssen's modernism/postmodernism divide and Eco's account of postmodern irony, the chapter claims Allen's ironic allusions purge Bergman of pretension while claiming tradition's justification, and that for Allen's artist characters art is the mirror of life and the key to self-understanding rather than a carrier of metaphysical truth.

- ch. 23 "Inauthenticity and Fascism" **·** Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, Søren Kierkegaard — Personal Identity; Authenticity; The They-Self (das Man) — David Detmer treats Zelig, the mock-documentary 'chameleon man' who turns black, Chinese, fat, or a Nazi depending on his company, as a stark dramatization of personal identity and the existentialist critique of inauthenticity. Against Socrates' claim that injustice corrodes the harmonious soul and that virtue is its own reward, the film, unlike Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment, lets the murderer and the 'yutz' Lester prosper while honest Clifford loses everything; Bergson's theory of the comic explains why Lester is invisible to himself, and Rousseau's reverie about what he would do with the ring marks Clifford as the one man who might use it decently. Aunt May's post-Holocaust atheism, 'might makes right' with nothing 'handed down in stone', supplies the argument from evil that releases Judah, leaving only the qualified hope that it is 'not all darkness'.
- ch. 25 "Hope" **·** Socrates, Plato — The Unexamined Life; Bad Faith; Authenticity — Jill Gordon reads Another Woman as a film about self-knowledge structured by Socrates' self-seeing eye from Plato's Alcibiades I: just as an eye sees itself only in another eye, the emotionally walled-off philosophy professor Marion Post finally sees her own repression by overhearing the 'other woman' Hope's therapy through a vent. Each of her relationships, with husband Ken, brother Paul, renounced lover Larry, stepdaughter Laura, and friend Claire, mirrors back truths ('she stands above people and evaluates them') that her self-deception refuses until her dream stages her own analysis and the final session describes her own life exactly. Counting the irrecoverable cost of her chances (Larry, the aborted child), the chapter closes with Rilke's 'Archaic Torso of Apollo', 'You must change your life', as the command Marion finally obeys, yielding a hard-won and still hopeful transformation.
- ch. 26 "Love and Self-Discovery" **·** Philosophy of Love; Aristotle on Friendship (philia); Moral Psychology (character, vice, weakness of will) — Mary Nichols argues The Curse of the Jade Scorpion answers The Maltese Falcon: where Sam Spade's cynicism leaves society no reason to trust its lone hero, C.W. Briggs, unknowingly stealing for the magician Polgar while hypnotized, is saved by love, friendship, and the office community of North Coast Casualty. The jade scorpion's trance, meant to cover crime, uncovers truth, as C.W. and Betty Ann admit under hypnosis the love and fear their waking selves hide, so that 'crystal clear' memory and self-knowledge, rather than Spade's rejection of dreams, free them from both the curse and crime. Against the noir world and Lester's 'comedy is tragedy plus time', the chapter holds that everyone has 'a little bit of larceny' in them and that experiences of love, friendship, and community are what form the character that keeps it in check.

Plato's Ring of Gyges fails as an analogy because WoW hands everyone invisibility, yet morality still operates as a social construction through reputation, guilds, and the ostracism of ninja-looters. The comedy comes from applying real-world categories — employee, income, property, jurisdiction — to a purely virtual existence, and it ends with the IRS 'outsourcing' her debt collection, a wink at real privatized tax-collection headlines.
- ch. 11 "Buffing Our Economic Knowledge with World of Warcraft" **·** Adam Smith — The Invisible Hand — Eli Kosminsky argues that Blizzard, probably unintentionally, built a perfectly competitive market: the auction house satisfies the textbook conditions of innumerable sellers (everyone dumping Mithril bars), homogeneous goods (every piece of Spellweave identical), perfect communication (instantly visible prices, no opportunity cost), and no barriers to entry (switching from farming Crystallized Shadows to Crystallized Fire costs nothing). He then tests the theory against server data — prices on high-population servers are nearly twice as stable as on low-population ones, and lesser and greater planar essences trade at almost exactly the predicted 3:1 ratio. Adam Smith's invisible hand works in Azeroth, making WoW a laboratory for economics that no real market could match.
- ch. 12 "Death Knight as Overman" **·** Friedrich Nietzsche, Arthur Schopenhauer — The Übermensch; The Death of God; Philosophical Pessimism — Luke Cuddy asks whether playing WoW can answer nihilism, using Schopenhauer's bleak remark on our sudden existence and Nietzsche's death of God to set up the problem — King Bangalash dies and WoW goes on, just as humans die and the universe continues. Entering Huizinga's Magic Circle gives Azeroth its meanings, but the player must then dissolve the circle, recognize that the game itself is meaningless, and keep playing anyway — creating the toon as a work of art in the Dionysian spirit. A maxed Death Knight still is not Nietzsche's Overman, because the player affirms only her in-game existence; the fifth step is carrying that self-creation back into real life.
- ch. 13 "Which World Am I In" **·** Zeno of Elea, David Lewis — Zeno's Paradoxes; Free Will vs Determinism; Possible Worlds — Juan Ferret tells the story from inside the game: Admetus the Human Mage, chased through Alterac Valley by an Undead Rogue, works through Zeno's paradox of motion, the convergence of infinite series, and whether space and time are discrete, coached by a bodiless voice that turns out to be his player. The voice's revelations — 'You are my creation,' autorun, polymorph, Blink — force the free-will-versus-determinism question from the character's side, since every action is controlled from above. Reference frames, Heisenberg's uncertainty, EPR entanglement, and hidden nods to David Lewis ('Siwel Divad' on possible worlds) and many-worlds theory leave the mage wondering which world, server, or parallel universe he really inhabits.

- ch. 15 "Forward! Into the Breach" **·** Robert Nozick — The Experience Machine; The Meaning of Life — Silcox and Cogburn test Nozick's three reasons for refusing the experience machine against Azeroth. Respawning kobolds and endlessly repeated quests threaten Nozick's 'we want to do things' objection — yet play genuinely changes other players and their characters, which is what the massive multiplayer element adds over a solo machine. Attribute scores let a dullard play a genius, but as with the wheelchair-bound woman who played Doom to 'get my legs back,' piloting a warrior is still something you do, like a pilot flying a plane. Against Nozick's 'man-made reality' complaint they argue that WoW, like great art, stays open to unbounded interpretation and even surprises its own designers, so the human appetite for fun is more robust and complicated than Nozick allowed.
- ch. 16 "Stop That Persistent Clicking" **·** Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Martin Heidegger, Arthur Schopenhauer — Will to Power; The Stages on Life's Way — Paul Brown argues that Warcraft II begins by saving the player from boredom and ends by offering something like eternal happiness. Kierkegaard's 'rotation of crops' from Either/Or explains why Medivh and the Orcs grow bored into war and how console play relieves the living room's intellectual dead zone, with Heidegger supplying the definition of boredom; Nietzsche's will to power accounts both for the player's overt domination of peasants and sheep ('Stop that persistent clicking') and for the sublimated, ascetic self-overcoming of mastering the game, which Maslow redescribes as growth-needs and peak experiences. Kierkegaard's third, religious existence stage — subjective faith giving 'eternal happiness' — is playfully applied to becoming 'contemporary' with the game itself.
- ch. 17 "FIVE, Dying Linking to Transcendence" **·** Martin Heidegger, Ludwig Wittgenstein — Being-Toward-Death — Dario Compagnio and Patrick Coppock organize WoW's collecting through Danet and Katriel's five root metaphors: hunting and gathering (quest chains as time markers), hoarding (XP and gold farming as accumulation bordering on compulsion), mating and desire (bower-bird avatar display seeking others' recognition), nurturing (group play as therapy and shared memory), and dying — collecting as linking to transcendence. The final metaphor is developed through Heidegger's being-toward-death: the endgame, like death, gives sense to all the play that precedes it, even though the hunt is never-ending. Wittgenstein's 'seeing-as' frames the whole analysis: collectibles become experientially real because players habitually perceive them as valuable, to the point of buying them on eBay.

- ch. 19 "This Is Our World" **·** Aristotle, Henry David Thoreau — Virtue Ethics (arete); Civil Disobedience — Miguel Sicart reads the 'Gnome Tea Party' — dozens of nearly nude gnome warriors parading in Ironforge to protest Warrior-class nerfs until Blizzard banned their accounts — as non-violent protest in the tradition of Thoreau and Gandhi. Against Blizzard's models of players-as-consumers and players-as-troublemakers, which he traces to Huizinga's Homo Ludens, he argues that players are Homo poieticus: creative beings who make the values they play by and act on them. After reviewing act and rule utilitarianism through Medivh's opening of the Dark Portal and the theft of Napoleon's sarcophagus, and Kant's Categorical Imperative through Kil'jaeden's deception of Ner'zhul, he argues that virtual items cannot be owned at all and that there is no moral agent in WoW for either theory to condemn: neither the player (who kills thousands of PCs in battlegrounds without blame) nor the avatar (which makes no decisions) can be held liable. Drawing on Donald Schön's reflection-in-action, itself rooted in the pragmatism of Dewey, James, and Peirce, he rejects the idea that rational facts come first and practice second: knowing how to fix the Rogue class is built through playing, posting, iterating, and revising. Knowledge construction is design, and the stakes extend beyond gaming to how literacy, learning, and civic participation should be understood in a world where play and work blur.

Plato's Republic supplies the gender argument — as Plato made the soul rather than reproductive function the criterion for guardians, WoW makes gender irrelevant to gameplay. - ch. 26 "You Can Discover More about a Person in an Hour of Play than in a Year of" **·** Plato — Allegory of the Cave — Tim Christopher weighs pseudonymity — multiple persistent in-game identities — against responsibility for what those identities do, opening with his accidental first 'honorable kill' and the real-world extremes of a stabbing over loud WoW and a murder over a borrowed virtual sword. Using the Tae Kwon Do mat as a model of the sacred gamespace, he argues that WoW's persistent world and anonymity invite misbehavior, yet since no one is kind in WoW out of fear of punishment, any kindness there may be kindness for its own sake — an honest character may be a better person than an honest player. Plato's Allegory of the Cave is his master image: players see only shadows (avatars) of the real people behind them and must remember the world outside the cave.

- ch. 28 "The Bronze Dragonflight Invisible Leadership" **·** Niccolò Machiavelli, Laozi — The Ends Justify the Means; Wu Wei (effortless action) — Moses Wolfenstein applies The Prince to guild leadership: the infamous Dives Onyxia wipe ('50 DKP minus!') shows why being feared beats being loved — players flooded to join and even requested his abusive raid calls — so long as fear never curdles into hatred, while PUGs and battlegrounds replay Machiavelli's warning that mercenary troops are 'disunited, ambitious, without discipline, disloyal.' - ch. 29 "What Is It Good For" **·** Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Cicero — Just War Theory — Ben Medler argues that despite the faction rhetoric there is no war in WoW. The fast zombies of 28 Days Later and the Dawn remake then mark a shift from Freudian uncanny return to full-blown crisis: pure rage without hunger or grievance, bodies in faster motion, leaving us an impossible identification with the composition that will decompose us.

- ch. 17 "Postmodern Postmortem" **·** David Chalmers, Seneca — Philosophical Zombies — Dale Jacquette distinguishes Hollywood zombies from Chalmers's philosophical zombies, imagining birthmarked beings whose behavior is run entirely by the brain stem so they speak, run corporations, and bleed like conscios while feeling nothing. He then proposes zombie gladiators: since amoral automata cannot suffer or anticipate death, arena blood sport between them seems morally permissible where Roman games condemned by Seneca were not. The thought experiment turns on itself: shielding conscios from attachments and vicarious suffering cannot cure the institution's symbolic statement that killing human-shaped beings for entertainment is fine, so even if consciousness grounds moral responsibility, how we treat the unconscious still reflects on us, and zombies can be wronged in the only sense left, through what we make ourselves.
- ch. 18 "Universal Rights Vampires, Space Aliens, Robots, and Humans" **·** Moral Psychology (character, vice, weakness of will); Punishment: Retribution vs Rehabilitation — John Draeger argues Romero's catatonic zombies, like plants, lack the cognition and empathy to be blamed, while vampires split: Stoker's narcissistic Dracula resembles Cleckley's amoral psychopath Milt, but Coppola's remorseful Dracula, Anne Rice's guilt-tormented Louis, and the self-deceiving Lestat ('the trick is not to think about it') all grasp consequences, feel others' pain, and can exercise self-control, so they are immoral rather than amoral and accountable for their killings. Accountability brings rights: Van Helsing's summary staking is vigilantism that denies Dracula a fair trial, rehabilitable vampires like Louis might earn civic participation, and a nonviolent, honest vampire suitor cannot be rejected merely for being Undead. The principle generalizes to aliens and robots: whatever can reflect morally must be held responsible and treated with equal dignity.
- ch. 19 "Cold Clammy Consistency" **·** Aristotle — Practical Wisdom (phronesis); Virtue Ethics (arete); Food Ethics; The Golden Rule — Wayne Yuen argues vampires are moral agents because they meet Aristotle's test of practical wisdom (Lestat's investments and his judgment that 'evildoers... taste better') and retain free will, since their blood-lust is a craving not a compulsion and Louis's rat diet in Interview with the Vampire, like Angel's in Buffy, proves an alternative exists. TASTE, full potential, and spiritual peace all fail to justify human blood, because killing a person brings more unnecessary suffering than killing a rat, the same suffering-based principle that condemns factory farming. The 'cold clammy consistency' punchline: since humans need not eat meat, condemning vampire blood-drinkers while dining on chicken, pork, and beef is hypocrisy, because we are vampiric too and only our choice of which life to ingest separates the monstrous from the virtuous.
- ch. 20 "Good Guy Vampires" **·** John Stuart Mill, Aristotle — Higher and Lower Pleasures; Mind–Body Dualism; Utilitarianism — Robert Arp models the vampire's unlife with substance and property dualism (a cursed soul persisting after the staked body in Rice and Stoker versus perishing with it in Blade), then uses Mill's distinction between lower bodily feelings and higher mental pleasures from Utilitarianism to explain how vampires can lament, in higher-pain, their inability to enjoy wind in their hair or wine. The hedonistic paradox dooms Lestat-style pleasure-seekers: seeking pleasure for its own sake yields either the pain of frustration (Louis's dead-twins trick) or boredom across immortal lifetimes ('it's somewhat boring, living forever'), while Aristotle's completed-state pleasures suggest the way out. Good guy vampires from Barnabas Collins to St. Germain escape the paradox by pursuing higher pleasures and activities for their own sake, confirming Mill's closing counsel that only those fixed on objects other than their own pleasure find happiness.
- ch. 21 "Deserving to Be a Vampire" **·** Epicurus, Martha Nussbaum, Aristotle — Death Is Nothing to Us; Immortality and the Soul — Ted Preston asks whether becoming a vampire would be morally good or desirable, working through The Lost Boys, Underworld, Blade, Forever Knight, and Interview with the Vampire. He argues vampirism is not intrinsically immoral: if vampires cannot restrain their feeding we pity rather than blame them, though with Aristotle we still hold people responsible for choosing to become the kind of being that cannot; and feeding need be no worse than mortal carnivory, since consensual feeding (Blade's stored blood, Karen Jenson's donation, Madeleine's offer to Louis) makes the bite more like dentistry with consent than assault. On desirability he weighs Epicurus's 'death is nothing to us' against the Furley-Nussbaum claim that death deprives a life of its long-term projects and relationships, concluding immortality's appeal is rational and that boredom is not inevitable in a changing world. The thought experiment ends in ethics: vampire or mortal, a life's worth depends on choices, so live a life worthy of eternity.
- ch. 22 "PART IV - Digging Up the Body Politic" *(front)*
- ch. 23 "Possibilities for Human-Vampire Co-operation" **·** Robert Nozick, Peter Singer, Adam Smith — The Drowning Child (obligation to aid); Speciesism; The Minimal State — Douglas Glen Whitman uses Buffy's vampire brothel (humans paying vampires to feed on willing victims, as Giles concedes) and Blade's mandatory blood bank as two models of human-vampire coexistence to stage the clash between libertarianism (Nozick, Lomasky, Schmidtz) and welfarism (Singer, Unger, G.A. Cohen). He runs vampire personhood through Nozick's criteria from Anarchy, State, and Utopia — sentience, self-consciousness, rationality, free will, moral personality, soul — and argues no standard includes humans while excluding vampires, applying Singer's equal-consideration-of-suffering principle across kinds of beings. He then extends Singer's obligation-to-aid reasoning (rescuing a drowning child vs. donating to UNICEF) to show welfarism would mandate forced blood transfers or 'blood stamps', and argues via Adam Smith's Poor Law settlement rules and modern welfare-based anti-immigration politics that the burden of supporting dependents breeds exclusion. Only the libertarian brothel model of voluntary exchange, he concludes, turns vampire-human relations into a positive-sum game in which humans would actually come to recognize vampires as persons.
- ch. 24 "A Political Philosophy of Vampires" **·** Jean-Jacques Rousseau — The Absurd — Phillip Cole starts from Rousseau's remark that the eighteenth-century vampire epidemics had complete legal proof, and argues the panics — like the witch craze Trevor-Roper ties to the Reformation's power struggles — reveal how political authorities constitute borders and power by cultivating fear of imaginary enemies. Freud's uncanny (fear not of death but of the returning dead) and Kristeva's abject (the corpse as the ultimate waste) explain why the Undead horrify and fascinate — Scratchy's ever-watching eyes in Itchy and Scratchy, the drowning corpse in Thérèse Raquin, Six Feet Under's serener dead and its wryly absurdist tone — while the vampire also plays the romantic monster of Coppola's Dracula and Interview with the Vampire. Politically the vampire is the demonized outsider — Stoker's unassimilated Jewish immigrant, today's asylum seeker or terrorist — and Buffy blurs the boundary by making demons neighbors and Angel and Spike heroes, while the truly shocking killings (Faith's accidental shooting of a human ally, Warren's gunning down of Tara) are human, all too human. The chapter concludes that we should stop mistaking fear-made borders for moral ones and the constructed outsider for a mortal enemy.
- ch. 25 "The Possibility of a Non-Repressive Civilization" **·** Herbert Marcuse — Utopia and Dystopia — Simon Clark reads Romero's four Dead films through Freud's repressive hypothesis: the zombie is the return of the repressed, an embodiment of Eros and Thanatos whose bite is at once erotic and regressive (the devouring mouth read as the vagina dentata, via Barbara Creed), and it advances exactly as civilization's authority breaks down — Mr. Cooper's failed patriarchy in Night of the Living Dead, the mall turned domain of lawless pleasure in Dawn of the Dead. Against Freud's conclusion that repression is necessary, Marcuse's Eros and Civilization supplies the hope of a non-repressive order in which liberated instinct turns toil into play, which Clark finds realized in Day of the Dead's beach ending, where Sarah, John, and McDermott survive pleasurably in the absence of zombies. Land of the Dead goes further: the zombies under Big Daddy evolve, use tools, and march on Kaufman's skyscraper, so the Undead martyr becomes the pioneer of Marcuse's gratified civilization — though Sarah's closing nightmare keeps Freud's suspicion alive that every order remains repressive.
- ch. 26 "No Way Out" **·** Thomas Hobbes, Jean-Jacques Rousseau — The Social Contract; The State of Nature; Communitarianism; The General Will — Leah Murray argues Romero's Dead films stage the American contest between individualism and communitarianism inside the social contract: in Night of the Living Dead, communitarian Ben barricading the farmhouse against every-man-for-himself Mr. Cooper hiding in the basement; in Dawn of the Dead, the Peter-Roger-Fran-Stephen mall commune collapsing when Stephen's 'it's ours, we took it' meets the bikers; in Day of the Dead, Sarah's compassion against the racist, harassing soldiers; in Land of the Dead, Kaufman's Fiddler's Green as a Hobbesian Leviathan born of fear, complete with bread-and-circuses for the poor. Against the Hobbesian fear-born contract (Dawn opens in a full state-of-nature collapse) she sets Rousseau's general will and Tocqueville's civic association, embodied in Riley's freely formed community and in Big Daddy's zombies, who organize, turn from eating humans, and bring down the Leviathan. The films' verdict: fear-based individualist contracts collapse under their own contradictions, while compassionate cooperation — even among the Undead — is the only way out, even if Romero doubts its ultimate viability.

- ch. 28 "The Meta-Fear of Fear" **·** Martin Heidegger, Henri Bergson — Being-Toward-Death; Laughter (the comic as mechanical encrusted on the living) — Noël Carroll reconstructs Halloween's Undead iconography from the Celtic Samhain and Pope Gregory's absorption of it into All Hallows' Eve, then poses the paradox that people pay to be terrified by corpse imagery they would flee in a real morgue — odd, given Heidegger's point that the Angst of death is so deep we spend our lives denying it. He tests Ernest Jones's Freudian reading of the nightmare — the vampire revenant as a disguised, disavowed incest wish — and rejects it as a Rube-Goldberg apparatus requiring universal incestuous desire and the whole machinery of the unconscious, when sometimes a zombie is just a zombie and The Fog is simply frightening. His own hypothesis: horror films and Halloween give viewers, especially anxious adolescents, a controlled trial run of fear that eases the meta-fear of fear itself, with Bergson's 'anaesthesis of the heart' explaining why Halloween parody and laughter serve the same emotional-control function as the scares.
- ch. 29 "The Male Vampire as Metrosexual . . . Sort Of" **·** Michel Foucault — Discipline and Docile Bodies; Gender Performativity — Joan Grassbaugh Forry argues the 'vampire aesthetic' both enforces and destabilizes constructed norms of beauty and gender, reading vampire representation through Foucault's made soldier (the body produced by discipline rather than nature) and the Bartky-Bordo feminist analysis of disciplinary beauty practices. Vampires are effortlessly, youthfully beautiful — exempt from the dieting, grooming, and ornamentation that produce femininity — yet their beauty must appear natural, which is exactly the demand beauty culture makes of women; Queen Akasha, Drusilla, and child-vampire Claudia are ultra-feminine femmes fatale whose threat is neutralized by childlike femininity and punished by death or ridicule (Claudia ashes in the sunlight, Drusilla is made to look foolish), while the feminized male protagonists — Louis and Armand in Interview with the Vampire, Angel and nail-polishing, Passions-watching Spike in Buffy, Langella's elegant Dracula — turn beauty, taste, and expressed emotion into audience identification and beautiful martyrdom. The payoff: in the realm of the Undead the male vampire becomes a beautiful martyr while the female vampire becomes a disastrous woman we love to hate.
- ch. 30 "Oh Yes, There Will Be Blood" **·** Aristotle — Immortality and the Soul; Personal Identity; Aristotle on the Soul — Rachel Robison asks why mythology — from Elizabeth Bathory's blood baths to Dracula's control over Mina once she drinks his blood, and True Blood's Sookie sensing and desiring Bill and then Eric — credits blood with immortality, youth, erotic compulsion, and shared knowledge, including the vampire's dark inversion of the Eucharist. Her answer runs through the history of philosophy and medicine: Aristotle, Empedocles, and Chrysippus located rationality in the heart and made blood its product, while Galen's humoral theory and bloodletting treated blood as the carrier of health, temperament, and identity — so blood naturally reads as the vehicle of personal identity, free will, and even knowledge, explaining how drinking it could transmit a maker's self to Sookie or Mina. She then asks whether the synthetic 'True Blood' of HBO's show could carry the same powers, concluding it works only if some property is shared with real blood or if the power attaches to the act of drinking what is taken as blood.
- ch. 31 "A Meditation on the Word" **·** Michel Foucault, Thomas Aquinas, Friedrich Nietzsche — Power/Knowledge; Will to Power — Randall Auxier and Eileen Townsend stage the chapter as fiction: letters, emails, IMs, and texts (1983-2009) between the ancient vampire Étienne Lavec and the newly turned Memphis student Paulie, through which shifting vampire pop culture — Anne Rice, Buffy, True Blood, and Twilight's sparkling, animal-only Cullens — is debated. Lavec's embedded 'Meditation on the Word' recasts the Johannine Logos so that vampires are pure desire, the uncreated darkness at the border of God's light, and his biography enacts the thesis: a Latin Averroist student of Siger de Brabant who drained Aquinas over the Summa's Treatise on Angels writing vampires out of existence, and a collector who hunted or interviewed Royce, Pico della Mirandola, Descartes, Spinoza, Hegel, Kierkegaard, and Nietzsche, whose 'free death' and 'beyond good and evil' he claims as stolen vampire ideas. The running gag and argument is Foucault, whose account of institutions (medicine, psychology, prison) creating their objects Lavec endorses as the order of desire, but who, half-undead after Lavec's failed bite and taunting him anonymously as 'nietzsche26', finally lures him into a trap in the Paris catacombs.

- ch. 6 "What Do We Hear when We Listen to Our iPods" **·** Jean-Paul Sartre, Karl Marx — The Look (hell is other people); Alienation — Wittkower opens with his cat Wallace at the window to show hearing is selective, then applies Sartre's account of the Look -- the voyeur at the keyhole hearing a footstep, the other as a 'void within being' -- to ask when sounds announce another person, the iPod-era rustle becoming the sudden 'appearance of a face.' Drawing on Marx's early claim that our senses are socially estranged, so that we meet others only as waiters, clerks, and price tags ('we are not our khakis'), he argues the audio cocoon both limits and enables encounter: mostly we hear our own identity reflected back ('I am the kind of person who listens to Amy Winehouse'). The governing image is Odysseus lashed to the mast, hearing the Sirens without being able to act, and the chapter closes by rehabilitating Marx as the first philosopher of technology alongside Heidegger, Dewey, Lukacs, Adorno, and Habermas.

- ch. 8 "What Was on Wittgenstein’s iPod" **·** Ludwig Wittgenstein — Language Games — Rehn argues there is no essential definition of the iPod: Shuffle, Nano, Classic, and Touch share only overlapping, criss-crossing similarities, exactly as Wittgenstein's family resemblance handles 'sport' (water polo vs. boxing) and 'game.' Even Cover Flow -- a physically impossible floating cascade of albums -- feels instantly familiar, showing familiarity is something users construct from hints rather than perceive, and syncing, playlists, and the Music Quiz game endlessly re-manufacture it (the iPod as the new McDonald's golden arches abroad). Flipping the relation via Actor-Network Theory, he notes the iPod tracks play counts, pushes 'Listeners Also Bought,' and trains our flicking gestures, so that perhaps the iPod becomes familiar with us; the Microsoft Zune figures as the abject, zombie-like copy whose perverse familiarity reveals a politics of recognition, and the closing game of imagining Wittgenstein's playlist endorses natural language's many language games over exact definitions.
- ch. 9 "iPod" **·** Martin Heidegger, George Berkeley — The Question Concerning Technology; Subjective Idealism (esse est percipi) — Raven reads design history as a pendulum between 'Beauty Waves' (Art Nouveau's sinuous decoration, 1960s psychedelia) and 'Truth Waves' (Art Deco functionalism, Le Corbusier's 'machine for living'), poles he grounds in Berkeley's 'esse est percipi' idealism versus metaphysical realism. The late-1990s blobject -- the iMac, the New Beetle, the MACH3 razor -- begins merging the waves through computer-aided design, and the iPod, launched five weeks after 9/11 and named for Kubrick's pod ('Open the pod bay door, Hal'), is the 'minimalist blobject' or 'squircle' where the merger completes. Reading the moment through Heidegger's claim that technology transforms human existence 'in its innermost recesses' and through Freud's pleasure versus reality principles, he argues the Iraq War sealed the reality principle into consumer design: 'our iPods now have edges.'
- ch. 10 "Progress Is a Myth that Truly Makes Life Worthwhile" **·** David Hume, Karl Marx, Michel Foucault, William James — Commodity Fetishism; Pragmatism; Discipline and Docile Bodies — Garnar contrasts the material practice of collecting CDs -- shelving some 1,500-2,000 discs by genre, the Rachels' Egon Schiele packaging, and Humean associations binding particular discs to memories like buying blue hair dye the day he got New Order's Lowlife -- with the iPod-era 'iPhenomenon' of buying songs piecemeal from iTunes. Using William James's question 'How is the practice real?' he rejects the real/virtual split, and reads the freedom of iMedia as a corollary of Marx and Engels's 'single, unconscionable freedom -- Free Trade': the solipsistic consumer never confronts the artist the way the album as artifact (Coltrane's My Favorite Things ending, he realizes, had to end with 'But Not for Me') forces him to. The body supposedly made unnecessary by 'iLiving' remains, with Foucault, a site of discipline -- eating disorders, outsourced assembly labor, e-waste -- so he keeps his iPod but defends a plurality of media and calls progress a myth.
- ch. 11 "Modding Mavens and the Limits of Reification" **·** Karl Marx — Alienation; Commodity Fetishism — Schaeffer starts from the felt aliveness of the click wheel -- 'it whirs and clicks, demanding my attention' -- and argues we personify iPods precisely because mass production hides their makers, applying Marx's account of alienation and reification ('the personification of things and the reification of persons') to the Foxconn 'iPod City' exposé of fifteen-hour shifts and hundred-worker dorm rooms paid fifty dollars a month. Extending the analysis through Lukacs, he reads Apple's fluctuating stock price as 'phantom objectivity' and the iPod bubbles of his Brooklyn coffee shop and his classroom -- couples passing 'like ships in the night' -- as a reified world that appears as the only possible world. Against the thesis's bleakness, modders -- a bicycle-dynamo charger, a fist-sized remote with five large switches built for visually impaired users -- re-inscribe humanity into the commodity, marking the limits of reification.

- ch. 13 "5 - Philosophy Audiobooks" **·** Immanuel Kant — This chapter is a transcript of LibriVox Community Podcast show #20, in which host Jim Mowatt and guests discuss recording philosophy texts as free audiobooks, sparked by volunteers' 'enthusiasm' over a group recording of Kant's Critique of Pure Reason and a fierce forum debate over which translation to use. Cohen argues that recording forces slow, attentive reading and that -- as with orphan drugs -- market logic explains why commercial audiobooks ignore the philosophical canon; audio supplements but never replaces careful reading.
- ch. 14 "Democratization of Media—Philosophy for All!" **·** Plato, Socrates, Aristotle, Epicurus, Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Nietzsche — Socratic Method (elenchus); The Categorical Imperative — Parker recounts training for the Seoul Marathon on a treadmill with philosophy podcasts (Philosophy Talk, Philosophy Bites, LibriVox) to argue the iPod works as a 'next-generation book' that can democratize philosophy -- yet, like the book, it cannot supply the two-way dialogue that Socrates practiced in the agora and that Plato's Academy, Aristotle's Lyceum, and Epicurus's Garden were built to sustain. He distinguishes studying philosophy -- compiling, say, Kant's claim that one cannot act except in ways one could will all others to act -- from doing it, and argues that credentials and canon cannot displace each person's inescapable role as 'the arbiters of our own thoughts.' The Nazis' misconstrual of Nietzsche is his example of interpretation without critical guardianship, and he concludes that the academy's stronghold on teaching philosophy is loosening even if wikis and virtual forums never fully replace professional expertise.
- ch. 15 "After Forgetting" **·** Plato, John Dewey — The Extended Mind; Socratic Method (elenchus); Experience and Education — Sturgis argues that iPods plus the collaborative Web 2.0 will end education's age of memorization: the school 'cheaters' storing test answers in lyrics files are early adopters of a world where, as Plato's Meno already allowed, knowledge can be retrieved rather than remembered, and a remote server is no more crash-prone than a brain ('the server could crash, but so could I'). His own podcasted environmental-ethics course shows Socratic, question-driven lecturing and radio's 'theater of the mind' reaching distance learners, while MIT OpenCourseWare and Berkeley webcasts (a trucker listening to Hubert Dreyfus) globalize elite content. Following Dewey on reflection and artistry, he predicts a post-factual pedagogy of assessing sources -- he catches Wikipedia misreading an iPod '90 percent market share' claim -- collaborative creation, and candid acceptance that we are already integrated with technological devices.
- ch. 16 "Thinking Inside the Pod" **·** Martin Heidegger, René Descartes, Plato — The Question Concerning Technology; The Cogito; Allegory of the Cave — Condella reads the opening of Saturday Night Fever -- Tony Manero strutting to 'Stayin' Alive' while delivering a can of paint -- as the iPod condition in miniature: music that alters rather than captures reality, now dial-up-able for workouts ('Eye of the Tiger'), bus rides, and grocery runs. Through Heidegger he argues the iPod is calculative thinking incarnate, annihilating the time, silence, and solitude meditative thinking requires (the shower as last refuge), and deepening technological homelessness by flattening every place into whatever soundtrack we choose -- the Rhine dammed into a 'gigantic gasoline station,' the iPod pushing us 'pretty far down' in Plato's Cave, its very possibility prepared by Descartes's thinking thing. Yet his wife's yoga instructor chanting on a Bronx bus shows the iPod can also open meditative space, so what Heidegger demands is not Luddism but Gelassenheit, a free relationship to technology.

- ch. 18 "Playlisting the iPod White Soul" **·** bell hooks — Cultural Hegemony — Austin unboxes his new black iPod Classic to argue that its true constant -- the glowing white body, white click wheel, and above all the white earbuds -- makes it 'the quintessential contemporary White good': an industry standard whose supremacy mirrors the invisible default of whiteness described by Richard Dyer and whiteness studies, from white magic versus black to white lab coats and the 'flesh-colored band-aid.' - ch. 19 "Youth and the Image" **·** Jean Baudrillard, Ludwig Wittgenstein, Plato — Simulacra and Simulation (the hyperreal); Language Games; Allegory of the Cave — Hickey argues the iPod is the 'iCon of a generation' because it is a Baudrillardian simulacrum of youth: like the Gulf War consumed as CNN fireworks or reality TV's fabricated 'real,' Apple's silhouette ads sell faceless dancers whose entire identity is the white device -- a 'symbolic cyborgism' read through Donna Haraway. The vernacular of iPodding, podcasting, and downloading forms what he calls, with Wittgenstein, a language game woven into the actions of the 'now' that authorizes this youthful, tech-savvy identity as natural, while the ads' imagery works like Platonic shadows in the caves of our desire. - ch. 20 "Become a Work of Art!" **·** Michel Foucault — Power/Knowledge; Discipline and Docile Bodies — Dumitrica reads iPod customization (skins, colors, playlists) as a Foucauldian technology of the self: identity is not a stable inner core but something continually made, as when a Facebook user who loses her 80G black iPod feels 'empty'. Against Foucault's examples of ancient care of the self and Christian confessional self-renunciation, she argues the iPod embodies the modern disciplinary self: we survey our playlists against what friends will think, dividing practices split iPod owners from users of 'lame MP3 or CD-players', and social-science-style lifestyle classifications (hip-hop vs heavy metal) shape our choices. The chapter ends by urging Foucault's aesthetic escape from pre-packaged lifestyle models — treat the self as a work of art, like the author sharing holiday photos with her father instead of plunging into a self-enclosed sonic bubble.

- ch. 22 "The Verdict" **·** Emmanuel Levinas, Martin Heidegger, Jacques Derrida, Immanuel Kant, Plato — The Face of the Other; The Question Concerning Technology; Binary Oppositions; Phenomena and Noumena — Turner runs an ethical audit of iPod use through Levinas's face-to-face: sharing one iPod with his wife forces 'musical justice' negotiations (veto power over a Motörhead cut, gnashing through Bette Midler), his toddler son's listening requires vigilance, and illegal downloading fails Levinas's test that morality begins by ceding 'my place in the sun' to the Other — hence Radiohead's pay-what-you-want release as the Levinasian move, and iPod cafés where strangers DJ for each other while language preserves alterity. - ch. 23 "13 - Don’t Talk to Me" **·** Applied Ethics (bioethics, technology, AI); Democracy — Pitt argues the iPod is 'pernicious' not because technology is intrinsically bad — a device is judged by the uses it is put to and their consequences — but because its consequences are antisocial: silent subways, 'brainsnatched' beach-walkers, and the silenced graduate corridor where loud philosophical argument used to live. Comparing the gadget to Huxley's soma (each user programs his own blissful isolation, going so far as to avoid domestic conflict by plugging back in), he contends social skills — argument, eye contact, advocacy for ideas — are what drive both technological innovation (even Steve Jobs had a co-inventor) and functioning democracy. - ch. 24 "Inoperative Community Mobile Clubbing" **·** Martin Heidegger, Friedrich Nietzsche — Kaulingfreks and Warren answer the charge that iPods destroy community with Jean-Luc Nancy: the warm organic community the iPod allegedly replaced is a romantic myth, because being is always 'singular plural' — we are only individuals in relation to others, and politicizing community into organized identity leads to totalitarianism. Their exhibit is 'mobile clubbing' — thousands converging on London stations and the Tate to dance to their own iPods, together yet each on a different beat — an inoperative community, together without forming a club, whose only bond is the recognition of being-with.
- ch. 25 "iPublic" **·** John Dewey, Walter Benjamin — Experience and Education; Democracy as a Way of Life; Pragmatism — Dewey takes the iPod's beauty seriously (Chris Breen's 'beautiful and intuitive' player, Donald Norman's user-centered design) and uses John Dewey's Art as Experience to ask whether iPod use can be a 'consummatory' experience that ends with us no longer the same person — hearing a bitter song as insight into the social conditions that produced it — rather than the contrived 'now' of fashion and hyper-accumulation, per Benjamin's remark that there is 'always a sense of the same in the new'. Connecting Putnam's Bowling Alone decline of social trust to narrow-casting as a way of rebuilding communities of common concern, they hold up LibriVox's 1,668 free public-domain audiobooks (327 days of audio, volunteers on six continents) as a gift-economy counter-model to a culture we only 'rent' as serfs on branded soil.
- ch. 28 "Tools Like Us" **·** Theodor Adorno — The Culture Industry — Arnold coins 'podcrastination' — the voluntary suspension of engagement with reality — and tests podcasting against two poles: Adorno's consciousness industry, under which plugging into an administered device is a 'cyborgian opiate' that deepens our thrall (CBS's listener-programmed KYOU station is still 'administrated'), and Postman's warning that 'we are tools of our tools'. The chapter's spine is Enzensberger's 1970 socialist media theory: podcasting fulfills his eight emancipatory criteria — decentralized programming, each receiver a potential transmitter, interaction, feedback, mobilization, political learning, self-organization — and could coordinate 'smart mobs' like the text-messaging crowds that toppled Estrada at Manila's EDSA plaza. Yet the media's potential is betrayed as much as realized — the Abu Ghraib photos and the 'Macaca' video circulated without catalyzing change — so the prediction is that the podcast will be squandered rather than co-opted: the iPod 'is not going to further a cyborg manifesto. It's only going to create a cyborg lament.'
- ch. 29 "Unity in Diversity" **·** Jürgen Habermas, John Dewey — The Public Sphere; The Culture Industry; Cultural Hegemony — Lombardo asks whether the podcast, born as an unsanctioned side effect users reinvented out of the iPod (as hip hop reinvented the turntable before corporate co-option made it the hegemonic form), can be a public-sphere institution. Following Habermas's Structural Transformation — coffeehouses, salons, and a subscription-financed press later corrupted by advertising into 'the soft compulsion of constant consumption-training' — and Dewey's call in The Public and Its Problems for institutions people actually participate in, he argues podcasts resemble the liberal-era press: resistant to advertising (readily fast-forwarded or deleted), enthusiast- and subscriber-funded, and open to marginal voices like a 'gun-owning, gay Texan' commentator that gatekept mass media would never air. But most Web 2.0 talk merely supplements advertising with consumerist pseudo-debate, so podcasts must link their fragmented diversity into a broader conversation of public relevance to become a socially transformative public sphere rather than 150,000 isolated niches.
- ch. 30 "19 - The Rhodesian Stranger" **·** Socrates, Plato, Arthur Schopenhauer — Socratic Method (elenchus) — A comic Socratic dialogue, a pastiche of Plato's Phaedrus, in which a time-lost 'Rhodesian Stranger' wanders in mid-conversation to defend reading against Socrates' retelling of the Thamus-and-Thoth myth — writing as inert painting that gives 'the semblance of truth', summed up in the Stranger's quoting his 'fellow Rhodish' philosopher Schopenhauer: thoughts on paper are footprints in the sand, the path seen but not what was seen along the way. Socrates' elenchus (the ship ticket versus the journey, walking beside a friend and turning back to check on him rather than posting signposts) drives the Stranger into inventing a myth in which gods replace abused speech with pea-pods whose planted seeds become pod-casts gathered by pod-catchers — podcasting imagined as friends dialoguing together toward the true and the good. Socrates closes skeptically, doubting that disconnected pod-communication escapes trivia and universal broadcast, but concedes 'surely beauty is as faithful a guide towards the good as is truth.'
