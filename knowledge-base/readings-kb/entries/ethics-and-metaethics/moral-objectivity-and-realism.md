# Moral Objectivity and Realism
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics · Also known as: the objectivity of moral value; moral realism vs anti-realism*

## The question
When we say that cruelty is wrong, are we stating a truth about the world — something that would be so whether or not anyone felt, believed, or decided it — or are we doing something else: expressing a feeling, projecting an attitude, reporting a convention? And if moral value *is* objective, what kind of objectivity is it? The candidates on offer in our textbook do not agree with each other: value as a mind-independent reality, value as rational necessity, value constructed by rational procedure, value as objective reasons, value grounded in empirical facts about desire, value grounded in human nature. The problem is therefore double: *whether* moral judgments answer to anything independent of us, and *what* they could be answering to.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture is devoted to this problem; the following is distilled from the textbook (Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 1) and is marked accordingly, per the build rules.* "Objective" can mean several different things, and moral objectivists have meant most of them. Plato and Moore held that good is a real feature of reality that we know rather than make — we can be wrong about it as we can be wrong about geometry. Kant relocated the objectivity: moral claims are objective not because they describe a value-realm but because they pass a formal test any rational agent can apply, the test of universalizability. Rawls's constructivism and Nagel's "normative realism" soften the metaphysics further — constructed moral facts, or simply true propositions about what we have reason to do. Mill went the other way and grounded value in a plain empirical fact, that pleasure is the one thing desired for its own sake; Aristotle grounded it in what a human being, given its nature, needs in order to flourish. Against all of these stands John Mackie's challenge: objective values, if they existed, would be entities "of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe," detectable only by a special faculty — and, worse, a bare fact about the world could not by itself move anyone to act. Moral considerations are supposed to be action-guiding; objective facts, as such, are inert. That pair of charges — queerness, and inertness — is what every objectivist account in this entry has to answer.

## Positions

### Platonic–Moorean realism: value as a reality we know
Good is an impersonal, objective reality, independent of desire, feeling, custom, and convention. For Plato it is a Form: particular good things are good by approximating it, and knowledge of it is the most important knowledge there is. Moore's version keeps the realism but drops the separate realm: good is a real, irreducible property — its presence depends on the presence of other things, as texture depends on a surface, but it is identical with none of them — and we are aware of it directly, by what Moore called intuition. On both versions, moral judgments are cognitive: literally true or false, made true by the value they answer to.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 1, presenting Plato's *Republic* and Moore's *Principia Ethica* — a formalization):
- P1. When we truly judge that a man, an action, and a social arrangement are each *good*, there is some one property they share in virtue of which each is good.
- P2. That property is not constituted by our desires, feelings, or conventions: we can be correct or incorrect in our judgments of it, as in geometry, and correctness is fixed by the property itself, not by our valuing.
- ∴ C. Goodness is an objective constituent of reality — something to be known, not stipulated, chosen, or invented.

**Objections** — Mackie's queerness charge (below) is aimed first of all here: a non-natural property, apprehended by no sense and no ordinary faculty, is metaphysically and epistemologically exotic. Jacobs also notes the standing question whether realism *must* be committed to value as an item in the inventory of the world — the dispute that Nagel's version (below) is designed to avoid.

### Kantian objectivity: the objectivity of rational necessity
Moral claims are objective, but not because they refer to a mind-independent reality. Their objectivity is the "universally valid necessity" of principles that any rational agent can endorse: a maxim is morally permissible if it can be universalized — willed as a law for all rational agents — without being conditioned by the agent's particular desires, passions, or interests. Moral judgments are therefore objectively true or false (Kant is a cognitivist), but their truth-maker is the formal structure of practical reason, not a value-realm.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 1, presenting Kant's *Foundations of the Metaphysics of Morals* — a formalization):
- P1. A principle conditioned by an agent's desires or interests commands only hypothetically: it binds only those who happen to have that desire or interest.
- P2. Moral requirements bind categorically — they have authority for an agent simply as a rational agent, independently of what the agent happens to want.
- ∴ C. The criterion of moral rightness must be a formal, desire-independent test — universalizability — and objectivity in morals is the objectivity of rational necessity, not of a normative realm "out there."

**Development** — Rawls's *constructivism*: the search for moral truth "fixed by a prior and independent order of objects and relations" is replaced by grounds "rooted in our conception of ourselves"; moral facts are *constructed* by principles of moral reasoning rather than discovered. Whether Kant's own view counts as *realism* is, Jacobs notes, arguable — it is objectivist without being committed to moral value as an object of reason.

### Nagel's normative realism: objective reasons without value-entities
Realism need not posit occult entities. What is real is that "propositions about what gives us reasons for action can be true or false independently of how things appear to us" — discoverable by adopting an objective standpoint and subjecting appearances to critical assessment. Korsgaard's gloss, quoted by Jacobs: "We need only believe that reasons themselves exist, or that there are truths about what we have reason to do."

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 1, presenting Nagel, *The View from Nowhere* — a formalization):
- P1. For there to be objective values, what is required is true normative propositions — truths about what anyone has reason to do and want — holding independently of our beliefs and inclinations.
- P2. Such truths do not require, in addition, a further item in the world (a Form, a non-natural property) in virtue of which they hold.
- ∴ C. Moral realism can be true without commitment to curious metaphysical objects: "the view that values are real is not the view that they are real occult entities or properties, but that they are real values."

### Mill's naturalistic objectivity: value grounded in desire
Value is objective, and its objectivity is empirical. Pleasure is the one thing desired for its own sake and only for its own sake; that psychological fact is an objective basis for the normative conclusion that pleasure is good and to be promoted, and it is a further matter of fact which actions produce more of it. Moral judgments are thus factual judgments — a deliberate demystification of morality. (Moore's charge that this commits the naturalistic fallacy is taken up in [Naturalism, Non-naturalism, and the Open Question](naturalism-and-the-open-question.md).)

### Aristotelian objectivity: value grounded in human nature
Given the distinctive constitution of human nature, there are objective conditions of a flourishing life. Practical reason — perfected in the person of practical wisdom — grasps what is genuinely good for a human being; in the virtuous agent "what reason asserts is what desire pursues," so correct desire, not desire as such, is the standard. This is a naturalistic objectivity, but of a very different kind from Mill's: where Mill cites a general fact about what people *do* desire, Aristotle cites what a human being *is* — the proper function of its essential capacities.

### Mackie's attack: queerness, the unexplained "because," and inertness — the error theory
The anti-realist challenge in its strongest form in this corpus. Ordinary moral judgments *claim* objectivity — but the claim is false, because there are no objective values for them to report.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 1, presenting Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* — a formalization of three converging arguments):
- P1 (queerness). If there were objective values, they would be "entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe," and awareness of them would require "some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else."
- P2 (the supervenience puzzle). Take the natural fact that an action is deliberate cruelty — causing pain just for fun — and the alleged moral fact that it is wrong. The wrongness is said to hold *because* of the cruelty; but the connection "cannot be an entailment, a logical or semantic necessity," and is not mere co-occurrence either. Just what in the world the "because" signifies is unexplained — whereas replacing the moral quality with a *subjective response*, causally related to detecting the natural features, would make the situation "much simpler and more comprehensible."
- P3 (inertness). Moral considerations are action-guiding; but merely *knowing* that something is the case does not, by itself, motivate. Hume: conclusions of the understanding "discover truths: but where the truths which they discover are indifferent, and beget no desire or aversion, they can have no influence on conduct and behaviour."
- ∴ C. There are no objective values. Since ordinary moral judgments presuppose them, those judgments are systematically false — an **error theory**: cognitivist about what moral claims *mean*, nihilist about whether any of them is *true*.

**Replies available in the corpus** — the objectivist's counters are developed across the neighbouring entries: that the "because" can be constitution or identity rather than entailment ([Naturalism](naturalism-and-the-open-question.md)); that responses can be *merited* by objective properties, as colour judgments are (McDowell, same entry); that moral facts earn their keep by figuring in our best explanations (Sturgeon/Railton, same entry); and that motivation need not be external to judgment in the way P3 assumes ([Moral Motivation](moral-motivation.md)). Jacobs is careful to note what Mackie does *not* conclude: that morality is unimportant. Mackie held that we make moral judgments and that doing so "is of the first importance" — they are to be reinterpreted in subjectivist terms, not abandoned.

### Monism and pluralism (a cross-cutting division)
Objectivists divide again over how many values there are. *Monists* (Kant, Mill) posit one fundamental value and one criterion of right action. *Pluralists* hold that values are irreducibly many: Moore's several intrinsic goods (pre-eminently "the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects"); Nagel's distinct sources of value — utility or welfare, autonomy, perfectionist considerations, rights — which cannot be measured against a common standard, though they can sometimes be ordered (fairness before utility, one right before another). Pluralism's burden is to show how irreducibly different values can jointly figure in a single judgment without making judgment arbitrary; its motivation is that attempts to reduce all value to one dimension look forced against the reality of value.

## Thought experiments
This problem is not driven by a single thought experiment in the corpus; its working cases are smaller. **The cat that grows hungry** belongs to the epistemology slice (Russell), but ch. 1's own diagnostic cases are: **cruelty** (the standing test case — deliberate cruelty, causing pain just for fun: whatever one's theory, it must explain what *makes* the judgment "cruelty is wrong" true, false, or neither) and **the tomato** (deferred to the naturalism entry, where McDowell's colour analogy does its work).

## Where the debate stands
In this corpus, unsettled — and unsettled at two levels. First, whether moral value is objective at all: Mackie's three challenges are stated at full strength and are nowhere in ch. 1 *answered*; the answers come piecemeal, in ch. 4's naturalism debates and ch. 2's motivation debate, and Jacobs presents them as moves in a live dispute, not as a verdict. Second, among objectivists, *which* objectivity: Jacobs's own summary caution is that all objectivists agree moral judgments are literally true or false, and "disagree over what *makes* them true or false" — Plato, Moore, Kant, Rawls, Nagel, Mill, and Aristotle give seven different answers. A further question the chapter leaves open is whether objectivity requires any one of: truth-values, independence of beliefs, independence of desires and feelings, determination by natural properties, or distinctness from natural properties — different combinations yield different positions, and the merits are "in the details."
