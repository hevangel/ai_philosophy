# Emotivism and Expressivism
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics · Also known as: non-cognitivism; the "boo–hurrah" theory (of emotivism); universal prescriptivism (Hare's variant)*

## The question
What are we *doing* when we say "murder is wrong" or "charity is good"? The sentence looks like a statement of fact, fit to be true or false. Emotivism says the resemblance is superficial: a moral judgment does not state anything — it *expresses* an attitude, approval or disapproval, and typically tries to influence others' attitudes too. If that is right, moral judgments are not true or false at all, moral properties are not among the things that exist, and a whole set of questions — what makes moral judgments true? where in the world is value? — dissolve rather than get answered. The problem is whether this account can explain the features of moral discourse that make it *look* factual: that we argue, give reasons, demand consistency, and correct ourselves.

## The 90-second version
Emotivism is a metaethical theory: not a theory about which actions are good or bad, but about what "good" and "bad" mean. The emotivist answer is that statements about good and evil merely express the speaker's approval or disapproval. "Charity is good" amounts to "I approve of charity" — or simply "Hurray, charity!"; "Stealing is wrong" amounts to "I condemn stealing," or "Boo to theft!" — which is why the view is nicknamed the boo–yay (boo–hurrah) theory. Emotivists are non-cognitivists: moral statements do not express propositions that can be true or false, any more than "Hurray!" does. The lecture works through a refinement — thick terms like "brave": to call Kiki brave is at once to describe her (she is fearless) and to express approval of fearlessness; pick a differently-loaded word for the same trait ("brazen," if you disapprove of fearlessness) and the description stays while the expression flips. The standing objection the lecture ends on: when we say murder is wrong we seem to mean something more than "I don't like murder" — we seem to say *you must not* — and since emotivist statements report no facts, the emotivist seems to have no way of giving a reason to stop a murder that she lacks for stopping someone painting a living room bright green, beyond "eh, I don't like it." (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [What is Emotivism? The Boo-Yay Theory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=39uOXZVVHoA).)

## Positions

### Emotivism (Ayer)
Moral language is not fact-stating. Ayer's version, as Jacobs presents it, is driven by a translation test: can statements of ethical value be translated into statements of empirical fact? No — but not, as Moore thought, because ethical concepts are genuine yet unanalysable. For Ayer "they are mere pseudo-concepts. The presence of an ethical symbol in a proposition adds nothing to its factual content." In making ethical statements "we are merely expressing certain moral sentiments" — and, a point the simple "boo–hurrah" label hides, we are also concerned that others should share and be moved by them.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 4, presenting Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic* — a formalization):
- P1. A sentence states a fact only if its content can be cashed out in factual, truth-evaluable terms; an ethical symbol in a proposition "adds nothing to its factual content" — "Murder is wrong" and "Murder is a killing" do not differ by any further fact the first reports.
- P2. What the ethical symbol does contribute is expressive: it expresses the speaker's sentiment of approval or disapproval and is apt to arouse similar sentiment in others.
- ∴ C. Moral judgments are not true or false and do not refer to moral properties; their meaning is expressive, not cognitive. (Ch. 1's statement of the same view: if a sentence "makes no statement at all, there is obviously no sense in asking whether what it says is true or false.")

**Objections** — (i) The lecture's objection: moral condemnation presents itself as more than dislike — it claims authority over others, and bare expression supplies no reasons. (ii) Foot's objection (below): the descriptive and evaluative components cannot be prised apart in the way the theory requires. (iii) The standing worry from ch. 1: can *any* expressivism, however sophisticated, be "more than 'thinly' expressivist" — can it recover the genuineness of moral argument rather than merely its outward forms?

### Prescriptivism (Hare): emotivism plus universalizability
Hare accepts the non-cognitivist core — moral judgments do not report facts, and "what is wrong with naturalist theories is that they leave out the prescriptive or commendatory element in value-judgements, by seeking to make them derivable from statements of fact" — but adds the formal feature that distinguishes moral from merely expressive judgments: moral judgments are **prescriptions the speaker universalizes**. To judge that deceitful promising is wrong is to prescribe that deceitful promises are not to be made — by anyone, in relevantly identical circumstances, including oneself.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 4, presenting Hare, *The Language of Morals* and *Freedom and Reason* — a formalization):
- P1. Moral judgments are distinguished within the class of prescriptive judgments "by being universalizable": in making one, I commit myself to the same judgment for every exactly similar case.
- P2. This commitment is a matter of the judgment's prescriptive form, not of any fact it reports — contrast "this stuff is awful" (taste) or "you ought to get a calculus tutor" (conditional, person-specific), which carry no general prescriptive significance.
- ∴ C. Moral judgment is action-guiding and reason-structured (consistency across cases is built in) without being fact-stating: the universal imperative force of moral judgments is grounded in their prescriptivity, not — as Kant held — in requirements of pure practical reason.

### Expressivism sophisticated: consistency and subjective supervenience (Hare, Blackburn)
The developed expressivism of ch. 4 concedes that moral judgments are tightly constrained by facts, and builds the constraint in. **Consistency**: "it is not possible to hold an attitude to a thing because of its possessing certain properties and, at the same time, not hold that attitude to another thing that is believed to have the same properties" (Blackburn). Hare's version: of two pictures exactly alike in every respect, one cannot say "P is a good picture and Q not" — the challenge "But how can one be good and the other not, if they are exactly alike?" has no expressivist answer except retreat. Jacobs calls the resulting relation **subjective supervenience**: moral responses depend regularly on non-moral properties, but the dependence runs through *us* — as being *uncomfortably loud* depends on facts about sound waves *and* facts about our thresholds, and is a property of our response, not of the sound. Blackburn's defence of the whole approach: the expressivist, in saying there are real obligations, "affirms *all that could ever properly be meant* by saying that there are real obligations."

**Objections** — the critics' question (Jacobs): is consistency *enough*? On expressivist terms, responses must be uniform across like cases — but can the theory *disallow* the response itself? "Is that sufficient to disallow the possibility that on their terms, cruelty cannot be morally permissible?" If a consistent, fully informed agent prescribed cruelty universally, the formal machinery registers no defect; the defect the rest of us register seems to be a cognitive one — a failure to understand something.

### The fact–value challenge (Foot, McDowell): the reply from within this corpus
Philippa Foot, an early and important critic of both emotivism and prescriptivism, argues the descriptive/evaluative split is untenable: "there is no describing the evaluative meaning of 'good'… without fixing the object to which they are supposed to be attached." Can a person understand what fairness, benevolence, or honesty *is* without understanding that each is morally good? If not, evaluation is not a laminate applied to a neutral description; the concepts themselves carry their significance, and moral judgment is "a rational response to the normative significance of factual considerations" — cognitive in the way ordinary concept-use is cognitive, with sensibility playing a role *within* cognition rather than projecting quality onto a neutral world. (The full development — McDowell's secondary-quality analogy and "no-priority" view — is in [Naturalism, Non-naturalism, and the Open Question](naturalism-and-the-open-question.md).)

## Thought experiments
- **The gallery pictures** (Hare, via Jacobs ch. 4). P and Q are replicas, painted by the same artist at about the same time; you do not know which is the original. You cannot say "P is a good picture and Q not" — there must be some further difference to ground the difference in verdict. Meant to show: even pure expression is hostage to consistency across identical cases; it is the expressivist's own route to a supervenience-like structure. It pressures purely "thin" emotivism, which has no resources to explain why the challenge is unanswerable.
- **The bright-green living room** (lecture). If "murder is wrong" expresses only disapproval, the emotivist's ground for stopping a murder is the same kind as her ground for objecting to a neighbour's paint colour: "I don't like it." Meant to show the authority gap — moral language presents itself as giving *others* reasons, not as reporting the speaker's tastes.

## Where the debate stands
In this corpus the trajectory runs: Ayer's austere version (moral sentences say nothing) → Hare's repair (add universalizable prescription, recovering formal rationality) → Blackburn's further repair (consistency and quasi-realist truth-talk from inside the moral point of view) → the Foot/McDowell counter-attack (the fact/value split the whole family presupposes may itself be the mistake). Jacobs leaves the contest open, with the critics' question standing: expressivism can insist that stances respond to facts and must be consistent — "But is that sufficient?" The lecture's simpler version of the same pressure (the authority/reasons objection) is likewise posed, not answered. Note also the family agreement that survives every repair: emotivists, prescriptivists, and Moore all reject *naturalism* — the dispute is only over what follows from that rejection (see the naturalism entry).
