# Moral Luck
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics (moral psychology) · Also known as: the problem of luck in moral assessment*

## The question
How much of what we are morally judged for is up to us? Two drivers take the same icy road at the same careless speed; a child runs out in front of one of them. We blame the second driver vastly more than the first — yet the difference between them is pure luck: nothing in their choices, characters, or efforts differed. A would-be rescuer dives and misses; another dives and saves; the dives were identical. If moral assessment tracks what lies within the agent's control, outcome luck should make no difference to judgment. If it shouldn't matter but irresistibly *does* — in blame, in punishment, in the agent's own remorse — then either our practices are systematically unjust, or the control condition on moral judgment is false. The same problem recurs for *character*: nobody chooses the upbringing that formed their dispositions — "constitutive" luck — yet we judge people for the characters they have.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture is devoted to moral luck; the following is distilled from the textbook (Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 2, "What about Luck?") and is marked accordingly, per the build rules.* Kant saw the problem and built his theory to be immune to it: moral worth resides in rational volition alone — in the will's determination by duty — and the will is the one thing fortune cannot touch; a good will frustrated by every accident remains good, like a jewel shining by its own light. Bernard Williams, quoted in the chapter, names the attraction exactly: Kantianism promises "an ultimate form of justice" — assessment purified of luck — as "solace to a sense of the world's unfairness." Mill refuses the immunity: for him the *motive* bears on the worth of the agent and the *intention* on the morality of the act, and both are exposed to luck — what an agent manages to intend and effect depends, as Nagel puts it, on "what one did often depends on outcome." Aristotle sits uncomfortably between: upbringing is "all-important" for virtue, which no one chooses; yet virtuous activity, once formed, is something like up to us, and flourishing still needs external goods fortune can withhold. The chapter's question is whether Kant's immunity is purchased at too high a price — a moral worth so sealed off from outcomes, character, and circumstance that it stops resembling the judgments we actually make.

## Positions

### Kantian immunity: worth in the will alone
Moral worth is located in rational volition — the quality of the agent's willing — and volition is the one item wholly within the agent's control. Outcomes, talents, temperament, and upbringing may affect what an agent *achieves* but not what the agent is *worth*, morally. Luck is thereby excluded from moral assessment by design.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 2, presenting Kant — a formalization):
- P1. It is unjust to assess an agent for what was not up to the agent; genuine moral assessment must track only what lies within the agent's control.
- P2. The quality of an agent's volition — whether the will is determined by duty — is within the agent's control in a way that outcomes, native temperament, and formative circumstances are not.
- ∴ C. Moral worth resides in volition alone, and is therefore immune to luck: the frustrated good will and the successful one have equal worth.

**Objections** — (i) The cost objection: our actual judgments do track outcomes (the two drivers), intentions realised and unrealised, and formed character; a theory declaring all of that morally irrelevant may be consistent but describes a practice recognisably not ours. (ii) The depth objection: is volition itself really luck-free? The capacity and disposition to will well are themselves shaped by constitution and upbringing — the immunity may only push luck one level down. The chapter airs both worries without resolving them; Williams's "solace" remark cuts both ways — a consolation can be a motive for believing, not a reason.

### Mill: motive and intention, both luck-exposed
Mill splits the assessment Kant unifies. The *motive* — what the agent acted from — determines the worth of the *agent*; the *intention* — what the agent meant to bring about — determines the morality of the *act*. Neither is sealed against fortune: which intentions an agent forms and carries through depends on capacities, opportunities, and outcomes, and motives are themselves products of temperament and education. Moral assessment, on this view, honestly registers luck rather than legislating it away — and the divergence between "objective rightness" (what in fact produced the best outcome) and "subjective rightness" (what the agent could reasonably have aimed at) becomes a permanent feature of moral judgment, not a defect.

### Nagel: the control condition collides with practice
Nagel's formulation in the chapter is diagnostic rather than constructive: we cannot give up the control condition (it seems constitutive of fairness in judgment), and we cannot give up our luck-tracking judgments either (attempted and completed crimes, the two careless drivers). "What one did often depends on outcome" — and as the sphere of the genuinely controlled shrinks under scrutiny (outcomes, circumstances, constitution, character), the area of agency threatens to contract to a vanishing point. Jacobs presents this as the problem in its sharpest form: not a puzzle with a known solution but a tension between two commitments neither of which we will surrender.

### Aristotle: character luck admitted, activity defended
Aristotle concedes the deepest luck of all: virtue requires a good upbringing, which is "all-important" and wholly unchosen — the badly raised may be permanently disabled from excellence. Three mitigations structure his position in the chapter. (i) Once formed, virtue is *activity*, and activity is the agent's own: the virtuous person does not merely have dispositions but exercises them. (ii) Flourishing (as distinct from virtue) admittedly needs external goods — friends, resources, a city — so fortune retains real power over the *life*, though less over the *character*. (iii) The result is a two-level view: moral assessment proper tracks the formed character and its exercise (partly luck's gift, then the agent's), while the assessment of a whole life remains frankly luck-exposed. Whether this is a resolution or a relabelling of Nagel's tension is left open.

### A supporting lecture note: motivation-based virtue ethics and luck
One Carneades lecture in the forms cluster observes, in passing, that *motivation-based* (agent-centred) virtue theories have a structural advantage exactly here: by locating moral assessment in the agent's motives rather than in outcomes, they insulate evaluation from bad outcome luck — a Kantian benefit reached by an Aristotelian route. The remark is brief and the lecture's main business is elsewhere; it is recorded here because it is the only luck-specific observation in the lecture corpus for this slice. (Distilled from: [What is Agent Centered Virtue Ethics?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g1hSqpOISxg).)

## Thought experiments
The chapter's own discussion cases do the work of thought experiments here; they are Jacobs's, set as questions at the end of the section.
- **The equally careless pair** (Jacobs, ch. 2). Two drivers, identically careless on the same icy road; a child runs out before one. Our blame differs enormously; nothing about the agents does. The case isolates *outcome luck* in its purest form.
- **The failed rescue** (Jacobs, ch. 2). A rescuer dives, misses, and the swimmer drowns; a second rescuer, identically brave and identically skilled, succeeds. If worth is in the willing, the two are equals; if outcomes matter, they are not. The case is the Kant–Mill fork as a story.
- **Unacted evil intentions** (Jacobs, ch. 2). An agent fully intends a wrong, is prevented by sheer accident from ever attempting it, and is discovered. We judge him — but for what? An intention that luck kept causally inert. The case tests whether intention alone, with no outcome and no attempt, can bear the full weight of blame — and whether Kant's volition-centred view handles it *better* than its rivals, as it should if immunity is its virtue.

## Where the debate stands
Open, in the strong sense: the chapter presents the tension (control condition vs luck-tracking practice) and four historically great managements of it — Kant's exclusion, Mill's admission, Nagel's sharpening, Aristotle's partial concession — and endorses none. Two scope notes for this KB. First, the literature's familiar taxonomy of luck (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) is Nagel's, but the *textbook* does not lay it out under those labels; this entry has used the cases (outcome, character/upbringing) without importing the labels as if they were the book's. Second, the textbook's own discussion questions are left as questions — including whether there is *subjective* vs *objective* rightness — and the entry preserves them as such rather than reporting a resolution the text does not contain.
