# Moral Relativism and Subjectivism
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics · Also known as: subjectivism about moral value; cultural relativism (for the relativist variant)*

## The question
Suppose moral value is not an objective feature of the world. Two different deflations are possible, and they are often confused. The truth of a moral judgment might depend on *whose* standards are in play — the norms of a culture, community, or person — so that the same act is right here and wrong there, with no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate: that is **relativism**. Or moral judgments might be grounded in *mental states* — sentiment, feeling, approval — rather than in facts about the world: that is **subjectivism**. Either could hold without the other, and the interesting subjectivisms in our textbook (Hume's, Smith's) are explicitly *not* relativist: they ground morality in a sentiment common to all mankind. The problem is to say what, if anything, secures the authority and correctness of moral judgment once objectivity is given up — or whether nothing needs to.

## The 90-second version
Relativism and subjectivism are independent positions that are often held together and therefore confused. Relativism says the morality of an action differs from person to person or culture to culture; it is opposed to *universalism*, the claim that ethical statements apply the same to everyone. Subjectivism says the morality of actions depends on the content of someone's mental states — beliefs, desires, feelings — rather than on objective facts about the world; it is opposed to *objectivism*. You can be a relativist without being a subjectivist: someone who holds that you must do whatever *your* holy text (or your country's laws) prescribe is a relativist, because different texts and laws give different rules, but an objectivist, because what the text says is an objective fact, not a mental state. And you can be a subjectivist without being a relativist: a divine-command theorist who holds that right is whatever the one God wills is a universalist — the same rule binds everyone — but a subjectivist, because rightness depends on a mental state, namely God's. The lecture also distinguishes *descriptive* relativism (the empirical observation that people do disagree about morality) from *metaethical* relativism (the claim that the truth of a moral statement depends on who utters it), and voices a skeptic's worry about arguing from the first to the second: that we cannot prove which moral claim is correct does not seem to imply that all of them are correct, each for different people. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture: [Moral Relativism vs Moral Subjectivism (Meta-Ethics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UQdlu_spyk).)

## Positions

### Sentiment subjectivism (Hume and Smith): subjective ground, universal reach
Moral distinctions are grounded in sentiment, not detected by reason — but the sentiment in question is not private taste. Hume: "The notion of morals implies some sentiment common to all mankind, which recommends the same object to general approbation"; the affection of humanity, "being common to all men, can alone be the foundation of morals, or of any general system of blame or praise." To judge morally one must "depart from his private and particular situation, and must choose a point of view, common to him with others." Smith's version disciplines sentiment through the **impartial spectator** — "only by consulting this judge within… can we ever see what relates to ourselves in its proper shape and dimensions" — so sentiments can be proper or improper, felt in due measure or not.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, *Dimensions of Moral Theory*, ch. 1, presenting Hume's *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* and Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* — a formalization):
- P1. Moral judgment has its basis in sentiment: first perceptions of right and wrong are felt, not derived from reason — reason instructs us in the tendencies of actions, but a sentiment must "display itself" to give the useful the preference over the pernicious.
- P2. The relevant sentiments, suitably corrected (the common point of view; the impartial spectator), are common to mankind rather than idiosyncratic: within that corrected standpoint, someone who does not see that cruelty is wrong is *mistaken*, not merely possessed of a different taste.
- ∴ C. Morality can be fully genuine — with argument, criticism, correction, and authority — on a subjectivist basis, without objective values and without relativity to groups or persons.

**Objections** — the objectivist's standing suspicion (Jacobs): is this having it both ways? If there are no moral facts, in what sense are moral statements "true"? The subjectivist's answer in the text: truth-talk within morality needs no realm of objective values behind it — we accept certain statements as true, argue for them, and argue against their contradictories, and Hume's universal sentiments supply "an adequate basis for morality." The objectivist counters with the *games* analogy: within cricket's rules we can say a move was well played, but we cannot say cricket is a *better game* than baseball — evaluation inside a practice is not evaluation of the practice, and the subjectivist (the charge runs) mistakes the first for the second. Jacobs presents this dispute through an analogy with compatibilism about free will: subjectivism about moral truth, like compatibilism, claims we can make all the distinctions that matter (correct/incorrect, sound/unsound views) without the contested metaphysics; critics of both reply that the distinctions are unstable without it.

### Ayer's expressivism: subjectivism without truth at all
The uncompromising version. A moral sentence "makes no statement at all": "sentences which simply express moral judgements do not say anything. They are pure expressions of feeling and as such do not come under the category of truth and falsehood." Note the precision Jacobs insists on: Ayer does *not* say moral judgments are reports *about* the speaker's feelings (those would be true or false); "That's wrong" expresses feeling, the way an exclamation does, where "I strongly disapprove of that" reports it. This position is developed as [Emotivism and Expressivism](emotivism-and-expressivism.md); it appears here as the limiting case of subjectivism — the version Blackburn's quasi-realism then tries to soften (truth *internal* to the moral point of view: "the existence of the truth depends on nothing (externally), and on those features that make it wrong (internally)").

### Relativism: correctness relative to norms
The correctness of moral judgments is relative to the norms of different times, places, and communities. Within a set of norms, judgments can be true or false; but there is no objective or neutral standpoint from which the norms themselves can be assessed, and no universal basis for morality. A universally shared morality is not ruled out — but if it existed it would be "a historical, contingent matter," everyone's happening to agree, not their agreeing on something objective.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 1, presenting the relativist case including Mackie's argument from relativity — a formalization):
- P1. Moral codes vary persistently across cultures and across the history of single cultures, and the disagreements do not resolve the way disagreements about (say) the causes of illness do.
- P2. The variations are better explained without objective values: in Mackie's words, "the actual variations in moral codes are more readily explained by the hypothesis that they reflect ways of life than by the hypothesis that they express perceptions, most of them seriously inadequate and badly distorted, of objective values." Commonalities (e.g. prohibitions on murder) are likewise explicable by needs and interests almost any community has.
- P3. Claims to have ascertained objective values are always made from *inside* one or another culture, whose norms shape the alleged ascertainment.
- ∴ C. There are no objectively true or false moral claims; correctness is always correctness relative to customs, practices, and ideals.

**Objections** (the objectivist replies, as Jacobs presents them) — (1) Difference, even persistent difference, does not show there is no fact of the matter: a society that denied prime numbers would prompt questions about its mathematics, not doubts about primes; "objective" does not mean obvious, and disagreements may be resolvable in principle. (2) Explanations of diversity are *consistent with* objective values existing; that a society's morality has sociological causes does not show its judgments answer to nothing. (3) The aspiration objection: if slavery or causing pain for sport is wrong, it seems wrong "because of what *they* are, not just because of who *we* are" — "it's wrong for us, but not wrong for them" drains the judgment of the content we meant it to have. (4) **The group-specification problem**: relative to *which* group? If the whole of humanity, the view collapses into Humean subjectivism; if a smaller group — which, given that each of us belongs to many overlapping communities? Were Southern abolitionists in the early 1800s *wrong*, diverging from the prevailing system? Can a minority be one person — is an act "wrong for P" only if P accepts norms condemning it? Jacobs's verdict on the dialectic: the relativist's arguments against objectivity do not by themselves establish relativism, which "must have merits of its own."

**A caution the relativist must accept** — relativism is a thesis about the *status* of values, not a first-order value: it does not entail tolerance. Tolerance, if affirmed, is affirmed from inside some value system; relativism "is consistent with the most lethal dogmatism and closed-mindedness, as well as being consistent with valuing diversity." Arguing that relativism *objectively* supports tolerance would be inconsistent.

### The independence of the two positions
The lecture's logical map matches the textbook's taxonomy. Subjectivism need not be relativist (Hume, Smith: universal sentiment; the lecture: divine command, subjectivist about God's mental states, universalist in scope). Relativism need not be subjectivist (the lecture: personal holy texts and national laws — different rules for different people, but the rules themselves objective facts). Textbook and lecture also agree on the common case: the familiar view that a moral statement is true iff it matches the beliefs of one's culture is *both* — relativist (varies by culture) and subjectivist (depends on mental states) — which is why the two are so often conflated. One textbook refinement the lecture lacks: on some uses, "subjectivism" names the narrower report-view (to say "X is bad" is to state that I believe X is bad) or is used loosely for emotivism; Jacobs's Hume/Smith subjectivism is neither — sentiment is the *basis* of judgment, not its reported content, and not mere expression either ("not 'blind' expressivism by any means").

## Thought experiments
- **The abolitionist test** (Jacobs, ch. 1). Were Southern abolitionists in the early 1800s morally *mistaken*, because they diverged from the prevailing system to which rightness is relative? And are critics of mutilation punishments mistaken where tradition sanctions those punishments? The case pressures relativism's group-specification and its ability to make sense of moral reformers — people a culture's own norms condemn but whom we want to call right.
- **The holy-text relativist** (lecture). A person who holds that everyone must do what *their own* holy text says: relativist (contradictory rules for different readers) yet objectivist (the text's content is a physical fact). Constructed to prove the two distinctions cross-cut.

## Where the debate stands
Unsettled at every joint, in these sources. Against relativism, the objectivist objections (especially group-specification and the reformer's case) are presented as serious but not fatal — "it needs to be addressed in a careful manner." For subjectivism, the live question is whether the Hume/Smith/Blackburn strategy — full-strength morality on a subjective basis, with truth internal to the moral point of view — is a stable position or, as the objectivist suspects, an unstable halfway house that must either find a stronger ground or admit the genuineness it claims to preserve is only apparent. The lecture ends one step further back, with the skeptic's procedural doubt: even granting irresolvable disagreement, it does not follow that each side is right for its own people — ignorance of which claim is correct is not evidence that all are.
