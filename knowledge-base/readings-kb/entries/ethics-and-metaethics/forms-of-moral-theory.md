# Forms of Moral Theory
*Subfield: Ethics & Metaethics (normative ethics) · Also known as: the locus-of-value question — consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, intuitionism, contractarianism*

## The question
Moral theories disagree about many things, but the deepest disagreement is architectural: **where is moral value located?** In the *consequences* an act produces? In the act's conformity to *duty or principle*? In the *character* of the agent? In a plurality of self-evident *duties* we simply recognise? Or in what free and equal persons could *agree* to? Each answer makes a different item **basic** — the thing in terms of which everything else (acts, rules, traits, obligations) is assessed — and each generates a different theory of what it is to deliberate, to be justified, and to go wrong. The textbook chapter surveys five forms; the Carneades lecture series approaches the same map from a different direction, asking of each theory what it takes as **primary** — outcomes, rules, or virtue — and what objection (too permissive, too restrictive) it characteristically invites.

## The 90-second version
The lecture series organises normative ethics by a single question: *what is primary?* **Consequentialists** make outcomes primary: the morally right act is the one with the best consequences, full stop — and the standing objection is that this is *too permissive*, licensing horrors (kill one patient to distribute his organs to five) whenever the arithmetic favours them. **Deontologists** make rules primary: some act-types are required or forbidden regardless of outcome — the mirror objection is that this is *too restrictive*, commanding us to let terrible consequences happen rather than break a rule; divine command theory counts as a deontology here, its rules being God's. **Virtue ethicists** make character primary: right action is *built on top of* virtue — act as the practically wise, excellent person would — and the objection pressed is consistency and guidance: virtues are plural and can conflict, and "do what the virtuous person would do" underdetermines hard cases. A framing lecture sharpens the taxonomy into **deontic** theories (act/duty primary) versus **aretaic** theories (virtue primary), and further lectures split virtue ethics by structure: **target-centred** versions define the virtues by the target each aims at, in a way that leans consequentialist; **agent-centred** versions split again into motivation theories (right act = act from virtuous motives) and paragon theories (right act = what the virtuous exemplar would do). Utilitarianism gets its own lecture as consequentialism's most specified member — analysed as actual, hedonistic, act (direct), universal, evaluative, equal-consideration, and maximising — where each adjective is a choice the wider consequentialist family leaves open. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is Consequentialism?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aFEfoGLLWx0), [What is Deontology?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qMCeaXyrl7k), [What is Virtue Ethics?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JPV2KsWMRfc), [What is Utilitarianism?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cbQx-Cmtqt0), [Deontic vs Aretaic Ethical Theories](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uqPIWhe2WVQ), [What is Target Centered Virtue Ethics?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DJYNu2mQO2c), [What is Agent Centered Virtue Ethics?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g1hSqpOISxg), and [Agent Centered Deontology](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FyEV-izx4K0).)

## Positions

### Consequentialism: value located in outcomes
The rightness of an act is wholly a function of the value of its consequences; no act-type is intrinsically right or wrong. Mill's version makes **utility** the criterion — acts are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse — with *secondary principles* (the inherited rules of common morality) doing the everyday deliberative work, themselves justified by the criterion. Moore's variant keeps the consequentialist structure but pluralises the good (see [Moral Objectivity and Realism](moral-objectivity-and-realism.md)). The textbook's test case is **assisted suicide**: where a competent patient in irremediable suffering requests help to die, the consequentialist assessment turns entirely on outcomes — suffering ended, precedent effects, effects on trust in medicine — with no residue of intrinsic prohibition to weigh. The lecture series adds the family's internal variables: *which* consequences count (actual vs expected), and over what scope (the act/utilitarianism lecture: universal, equal-weighted, maximising).

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 3, presenting Mill, *Utilitarianism* — a formalization):
- P1. The sole fundamental criterion of right action is the value of what the action brings about: "actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness; wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness."
- P2. No feature of an act other than its consequences — its type, its motive, its conformity to rule — has intrinsic bearing on its rightness; rules and prohibitions derive their authority entirely from the outcomes of observing them (secondary principles).
- ∴ C. Rightness is consequential through and through: in any case, including the hard ones, the right act is the one whose consequences are best, and apparent exceptions are either miscalculations or secondary principles doing their work.

**Objections** — the lecture's "too permissive": the organ-distribution case, in which the arithmetic licenses killing one to save five, tests whether "no intrinsic act-rightness" is a discovery or a reductio. From the textbook side: Williams's impartiality critique (the agent as representative of the satisfaction system — see [Are Moral Considerations Overriding?](are-moral-considerations-overriding.md)) and the standing worry about whether outcome-value can be specified without smuggling in the deontic notions it claims to replace. (The trolley problem, below, is the lecture family's preferred arena for this fight.)

### Kantian non-consequentialism (deontology): value located in duty and the will
"Moral worth does not lie in the effect" expected from an action, nor in any principle of action that borrows its motive from that effect. Rightness is conformity to the **categorical imperative** — act only on maxims you can will as universal law, and treat humanity, in yourself and others, always also as an end, never merely as a means. Persons have **unconditional worth** — a dignity that is not a price and cannot enter trade-offs — which is the deep ground of the theory's non-consequentialism: some things may not be done to a person *however good* the outcome, because the person is not a vessel of value but a source of claims. The lecture's agent-centred deontology lecture adds a structural refinement this textbook does not develop: duties can be **agent-relative** (my promises bind *me*, my children are *my* special charge) rather than agent-neutral, which softens the Williams impartiality charge from within the deontological family.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 3, presenting Kant — a formalization):
- P1. Moral worth and rightness cannot rest on effects: effects are contingent, luck-exposed, and common to good and bad actions alike; a principle whose motive is borrowed from an expected effect is heteronomous, not moral.
- P2. Rational agents possess unconditional worth as ends in themselves; a maxim is permissible only if it can be willed universally and treats every person it touches as such an end.
- ∴ C. Right action is determined by the form of the agent's maxim against the categorical imperative — one principle, exceptionlessly — and consequences are morally relevant only as they bear on what maxims are in play.

**Objections** — the lecture's "too restrictive": rules held exceptionlessly command us to watch preventable catastrophes rather than lie, break, or kill our way out. From within this corpus: the overridingness critique (previous entry) and the question whether the single imperative can generate the *content* of duty without quietly importing the teleology Kant excluded.

### Intuitionism: value located in a plurality of self-evident duties (Ross)
Ross's middle way: there is no single criterion and no consequentialist reduction, but a plurality of **prima facie duties** — fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence — each **self-evident** to mature reflection, known by reason (not by a special moral sense or occult faculty), and each genuinely binding *unless* overridden in the circumstances by a weightier one. Crucially, one's **actual duty** in a concrete situation is *not* self-evident: it is a matter of judgment weighing the prima facie duties in play, and reasonable judges can differ.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 3, presenting Ross, *The Right and the Good* — a formalization):
- P1. Certain duties present themselves to reflection as self-evidently binding in their own right (a promise creates a claim; an injury, a debt of reparation) and none is reducible to another or to a single master-value.
- P2. These duties can conflict in concrete cases, and no further principle mechanically resolves the conflict.
- ∴ C. Moral knowledge is real but plural and non-systematic: we know the prima facie duties directly, and we *judge* — fallibly — what our actual duty is.

**Objections** — Rawls's, recorded in the chapter: a plurality of principles with no priority rule is an "unconnected heap" — a list, not a theory; it describes our convictions without ordering them. The intuitionist's available reply (in the text's spirit): that the heap is what the moral phenomena actually look like, and tidiness purchased by reduction (to utility, or to one imperative) is tidiness at the phenomena's expense; compare pluralism in [Moral Objectivity and Realism](moral-objectivity-and-realism.md).

### Virtue-centred theory: value located in character (Aristotle)
The oldest form and, in the chapter's architecture, the standing alternative to the whole duty-and-criterion project. Aristotle seeks **no criterion** of right action: ethics is not that kind of inquiry. The standard is a *person* — the **phronimos**, the man of practical wisdom, is "standard and measure" of what is fine and pleasant — and the theory's basic notions are virtues: stable states of character in which reason and desire are aligned (see [Moral Motivation](moral-motivation.md)), each a **mean** between excess and deficiency (courage between cowardice and rashness — the doctrine the lecture on Aristotle's mean presents). "What ought I to do?" is answered by perception of the situation as the practically wise person perceives it, not by applying a decision procedure. The lecture family's subdivisions map onto this: agent-centred versions (motivation and paragon theories) are the textbook's Aristotle formalised; target-centred versions rebuild virtue from the ends each virtue aims at.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 3, presenting Aristotle — a formalization):
- P1. Practical matters are irreducibly particular: what courage, justice, or generosity require varies with circumstances in ways no finite rule-set captures in advance.
- P2. The practically wise person reliably perceives and does what the situation requires, because his desires and judgments have been formed, by habituation, to track the genuinely good.
- ∴ C. The locus of moral value and knowledge is character, not criteria: right action is what the virtuous agent would do, and moral education (formation of character) replaces decision-procedure as ethics' central practical concern.

**Objections** — Hursthouse's, in the chapter: "what ought I to do?" still needs an answer *tonight*, in the hard case, and "what the phronimos would do" can seem to defer rather than answer — the virtue ethicist's reply (premises about what virtues require *do* generate action-guidance: "do what is honest, do not do what is dishonest") is recorded alongside. Williams's distinct worry: that virtue ethics, pressed to justify its virtues, collapses into a teleology (flourishing as the outcome to be produced) and becomes a disguised consequentialism — the target-centred lecture's consequentialist lean illustrates the pressure. And the chapter's caveat: Aristotle's *essentialist perfectionism* (a fixed human nature that virtue perfects) is detachable from the virtue-centred structure, and most modern versions detach it (see the naturalism discussion in [Naturalism](naturalism-and-the-open-question.md)).

### Contractarianism: value located in agreement
Moral norms are justified as the terms of an **agreement** — actual or hypothetical — among parties who must live together. Everything depends on how the parties and their situation are described, and the family's members are best distinguished by their descriptions. **Hobbes**: parties in a state of nature, roughly equal in vulnerability, self-interested; morality is the covenant that insecurity rationally compels. **Locke**: a state of nature already under natural law; agreement adds enforcement, not the norms themselves. **Rawls**: parties behind a **veil of ignorance**, ignorant of their place, talents, and conception of the good, choose principles of justice in an **original position** designed to be fair — the veil does the moral work (contrast the intuitionism objection above: Rawls's construction is meant to *order* our convictions, in reflective equilibrium, not merely list them). **Gauthier**: no veil — bargaining among fully informed, self-interested parties; morality as mutual advantage. **Scanlon**: no bargain at all — an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles no one could *reasonably reject*; agreement as a test of justifiability to each person.

**Canonical argument** (source: Jacobs, ch. 3, presenting the contractarian strategy via Rawls — a formalization):
- P1. A moral principle is justified if it would be chosen (or could not reasonably be rejected) by free and equal parties under conditions that model fairness — in Rawls's construction, ignorance of one's own position, so that no one can rig the principles to his advantage.
- P2. Principles so chosen bind not because they track an independent moral order but because no one situated as the parties are situated could object to them.
- ∴ C. The authority of morality is the authority of fair agreement: the locus of value is relocated from the world, the will, or character to the *procedure* of mutual justification. (Jacobs's caution: since everything turns on the description of the parties, disputes *within* contractarianism — veil or no veil, bargain or rejectability — are disputes about what fairness itself requires, and the form's neutrality is only apparent.)

### The chapter's closing observations
Two remarks end Jacobs's survey and belong in this entry as its own meta-lesson. (1) **All five forms speak the idiom of duty**: even the virtue-centred theory, whose basic notion is character, articulates its demands as oughts — the idiom is harder to escape than any particular theory. (2) **Purity is not required**: the forms interpenetrate in practice — Mill "domesticates" virtue and justice inside utilitarianism (virtues as the dispositions utility commends; justice as utility's most stringent region) — and a theory's classification is a claim about what it makes *basic*, not a ban on its using the other forms' materials.

## Thought experiments
- **The assisted-suicide case** (Jacobs, ch. 3). A competent patient, suffering irremediably, requests assistance in dying. The chapter uses the case to *differentiate* the forms: the consequentialist weighs outcomes only; the Kantian asks after maxims, autonomy, and the humanity formula; the virtue theorist asks what compassion and practical wisdom perceive here; the contractarian, what terms no one could reasonably reject. Same case, five different questions — the cleanest exhibit of the locus thesis.
- **The trolley problem** (lecture-only; the textbook never mentions it). A runaway trolley heads for five workers; you may pull a switch diverting it to a track where it will kill one. The lecture presents the standing result — most judge diverting permissible, some even required — and frames it as the deontologist's hard case ("let five die rather than kill one") against the utilitarian's easy one ("kill one, save five"), with intuition siding with the utilitarians. It is included here, following the pilot's precedent of admitting series-neighbour cases, explicitly as lecture material: it pressures exactly the permissiveness/restrictiveness axis of the 90-second version. Its many variants in the series (Fat Man, Loop, Organ Donor, and others) are catalogued in the lectures index; they are not individually claimed by this entry.

## Where the debate stands
No adjudication is offered by either source, and the two sources' maps differ instructively. The textbook's five-form map (with intuitionism and contractarianism as full members) is finer than the lectures' three-way primary-question map (outcomes/rules/virtue), under which intuitionism distributes awkwardly (deontic in act-focus, but its epistemology is sui generis) and contractarianism does not appear at all. Where the sources agree: the classification question — *what is basic?* — is the right first question to ask of any moral theory, and the characteristic objections are structural (permissiveness for outcome-theories, restrictiveness for rule-theories, guidance for character-theories, heap-ness for pluralism, description-dependence for agreement-theories), recurring wherever the forms are rebuilt. This entry's reconstructions are the textbook's; the lecture refinements (agent-relative deontology, target- vs agent-centred virtue) are marked as lecture material where they appear.
