# Fallacies and Sophistry
*Subfield: Logic & Critical Thinking · Also known as: 謬誤剖析 (fallacy analysis); the anti-sophistry system; fallacy vs factual error*

## The question
Bad reasoning fails in recurring, nameable ways — and a second, uglier phenomenon stands behind the failures: sophistry, reasoning engineered to *win* rather than to be right, which reaches for the named failures precisely when its position has already been refuted. Can the failures be catalogued without the catalogue becoming pedantry? And what distinguishes a fallacy — an error in the *way* of thinking — from merely believing something false? The corpus's richest source, Li Tianming, built both a catalogue and an "anti-sophistry system," and states the distinction as a definition pair.

## The 90-second version
A fallacy is a repeatable way an argument can go wrong while wearing the costume of going right. **Begging the question** builds the conclusion into the premises — at its purest, "A, therefore A," which is valid and utterly unconvincing; in practice it hides the contested premise, or restates the conclusion in fresh negations, or loops a long chain of inferences back to its starting point. The **appeal to ignorance** treats an absence as a presence: "no one has proved ghosts don't exist, so ghosts exist" — and it cuts both ways, since the same form "proves" a claim and its negation from the same ignorance. The **ad hominem** abandons the argument for the person, in abusive, circumstantial (you'd benefit, so you're wrong), and *tu quoque* (you're a hypocrite, so you're wrong) dress — though facts about a person are legitimate when they are genuinely at issue, as when the drunk is denied the choice of route. The **straw man** refutes a weakened double of the opposing view (better ventilation becomes demanded air-conditioning); the **red herring** changes the subject to something more sensational; the **false dichotomy** presents two options where a third exists; **equivocation** lets one word do two jobs ("a centipede has a hundred *feet*…"). The lectures group these as fallacies of relevance, presumption, weak induction, and ambiguity — failure modes of support, each needing the arguer to notice *where* the support was supposed to come from. Li adds the meta-lesson: spotting the pattern is the practical point — naming it makes it "easy to identify when met." (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Begging the Question](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IJ2dWrI-PTA), [Appeal to Ignorance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p9ezNBBcg_g), [Ad Hominem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IEQDP2pYrmM), [Straw Man](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D312ssRfWMA), [Equivocation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kXlIAXx5TXE), [False Dichotomy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XIyLqUOJ0lY), [Red Herring](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZggiwS7iv2A).)

## Positions

### Fallacy vs factual error (Li's distinction)
Li's taxonomy begins by separating two kinds of error that everyday speech merges (source: Li, *Essays in Critical Thinking*, book 002 ch13 §II — read in my English translation):

- **Fallacy （謬誤）**: "errors in ways of thinking." Self-contradiction, hasty generalisation, begging the question, shifting the thesis, strained analogy.
- **Factual error （訛誤）**: "errors of knowledge." Believing the Earth is flat; taking nitric acid for sulphuric acid.

The distinction does diagnostic work in the same section: *making* a bad syllogistic inference is a fallacy, but *misdescribing* what a syllogism is — claiming it the simplest expression for any thoughtful argument — is a factual error, and a common one, since infinitely many valid arguments (Li's example: "All circles are figures; therefore, anyone who draws a circle draws a figure") exceed syllogistic form; the syllogism is only "a small item within the monadic quantification logic within first-order quantification logic within standard first-order logic." In his notes Li refuses both tightenings of "fallacy": the broad use (any wrong belief — too wide) and the logicians' use (only invalid arguments — too narrow, excluding self-contradiction and complex question). The fallacy/ factual-error line, he concedes, is itself approximate and overlapping.

### Li's catalogue, from the worked examples
*Essays in Critical Thinking* is a demolition of two real published replies to Li's September Debate performance; its fallacies are therefore exhibited in the wild (source: ch13, §§I–III):

- **Pseudo-refutation and pseudo-proof** (§I). Announcing "if each of six aspects had its own god…" is not refuting anything — a refutation must point at an actual fault; and "understanding God from the angle of love" is not a proof, since understanding ≠ proving.
- **Begging the question** (§I(B)). "God loves the world" presupposes what the debate is about — God's existence. Li's general form: using a thesis awaiting proof as if it were a thesis already proved. §IV(B)(4) catches the circular version in the omnipotence debate, where "God is omnipotent" serves both as premise ("Why is there no stone God cannot lift? Because God is omnipotent") and as conclusion ("so He is still omnipotent") — "the end point turns out to be just the start point."
- **Appeal to ignorance** (§II(A)(1)). "If the negative side cannot break his arguments, God's existence has obtained proof" — Li: "an erroneous argument does not become a correct argument because others cannot break it," with the reductio of debating a small child ("pigs have two ears; you have two ears; so you are a pig"). His note extends the fallacy symmetrically (unproven ⇒ false is the same fallacy) and excepts declared conventions like the presumption of innocence, which is a rule of the game, not a law of logic.
- **Self-defeating statements** (§II(A)(2)). A claim that "in logic or in effect damages and disables itself": the "No Posting" notice; the reviewer who writes "I am not here championing Horner" across pages of championing Horner.
- **False-value transmission** (§II(A)(3)). From the validity condition (premises true ⇒ conclusion true), nothing follows from premises *false*; inferring a false conclusion from false premises — "the major premise has been overthrown, so the conclusion has been overthrown" — is Li's named fallacy, "the fallacy that people who talk about logic should least commit," found verbatim in a book titled *How to Correct Logical Errors*.
- **Non-sequitur** (§IV(B)(1)). "He created the whole universe, so naturally He is omnipotent": even granting the creation, nothing follows about other possible worlds.

And §III adds the explanatory counterfeit: the **seeming explanation** — an "explanation" with no verifiability and no predictive power (developed in *Closed Systems and Pseudo-Explanation*) — plus the procedural point that answering a demand for clarification by *specifying which* god is meant is a failure to engage: "specifying is not clarifying."

### The anti-sophistry system (Li)
Where the catalogue names single failures, *From the Journey of the Bloody Road to the Journey to the Kingdom of Heaven* (book 002 ch12) builds a system for the *practice* of sophistry, organised over Leung Yancheng's *Review* as twelve "fatal points" (source: ch12, prologue and taxonomy sections — read in the Chinese original; translation outstanding and flagged in the translation README):
- **The four blindnesses**: metaphor-blindness (reading figurative language literally), irony-blindness, semantic-analysis-blindness (the parody of analysis that asks "what is…?" in a volley without doing the work — Li calls it "silly-analysis"), logic-blindness (lacking elementary logical competence — distinct, Li insists, from never having studied logic).
- **The three delusions**: the criterion delusion (demanding that criteria be stated before criticism may begin — a standard the *Review* itself fails, digging its own trap), the out-of-context delusion (charging distortion without exhibiting any distorted passage), the irrelevance delusion (mistaking the relevant for the irrelevant and vice versa).
- **The five tricks**: dragging in irrelevancies; fabricating from nothing; passing fish eyes off as pearls; resorting to abuse; shrinking and fleeing.

Ch11 (*Critical Thinking and Religion*, Part II) supplies the motivational theory: **the three knacks of sophistry** are (1) seem plausible — the sophism must look right, up to accusing the analyst of "nitpicking over words"; (2) make up in volume — keep talking, answer the opponent's *style* rather than his points, cite everything from Confucius to Descartes, and above all get the last word, so onlookers think the question "still under dispute"; (3) move by emotion rather than reason. And its frame distinction — Li's "two senses of winning": to *be* in the right is one thing; to be *awarded* the win (or to have the audience believe you) is another. "The way of critical disputation's sure-win method is: never engage in sophistry; always only argue correctly."

### The lecture taxonomy
The Carneades Fallacy February series classifies independently: **fallacies of relevance** (ad hominem, red herring, straw man, appeal to force/pity/people), **fallacies of presumption** (begging the question, false dichotomy, complex question), **fallacies of weak induction** (appeal to ignorance, hasty generalisation), **fallacies of ambiguity** (equivocation and kin). Its definitions match Li's at the overlap points — begging the question's three forms (hidden shaky premise; restated conclusion; circular chain) are the same phenomenon Li prosecutes in the September Debate literature — with one honest qualification the series itself makes for ad hominem: an attack on the person is *not* fallacious when the personal fact is what the argument is actually about.

## Thought experiments
- **The child and the pig** (Li, ch13 §II(A)(1)). Arguments a child cannot break are not thereby proved; inability to refute measures the audience, not the argument — the appeal-to-ignorance detector.
- **"I am not championing Horner"** (Li, ch13 §II(A)(2)). Thirty pages of defence prefaced by its own denial: self-defeat as an effect, not just a form ("one part of itself conflicting with another part").
- **"No Posting"** (Li, same section). The self-defeating limiting case, legible at a glance.
- **Horner's six explanations** (Li, ch13 §III(A); debate transcript in book 002 ch14). Six arguments of the shape "God is the best explanation of X, therefore God" — Li's charge is that an untestable explainer explains everything and hence predicts nothing: the stones of the fallacy catalogue set into a closed system (see the next entry).

## Where the debate stands
No source in the corpus disputes the individual fallacy analyses; the live tensions are taxonomic and practical. Taxonomic: Li's rough, overlapping categories versus the lecture series' four neat families — the same item (equivocation) is a "fallacy of ambiguity" for the lectures and the engine of "conceptual slippage," a *linguistic harm* rather than a fallacy proper, for Li's ch10 framework; the corpus offers no arbitration. Practical: Li himself states the limit of his published corpus — the systematising "four-nots framework" （四不架構） of his lectures has not appeared in his published works, so the most systematic version of his fallacy theory is announced but unavailable. And the sophistry half of the problem adds a claim the lectures do not make: fallacies in print are often not mistakes but *moves*, deployed after refutation by an arguer who knows he has lost — which is why Li's remedy is not only a catalogue but a theory of the arguer's incentives (win vs be right; volume and emotion as substitutes for answerability).
