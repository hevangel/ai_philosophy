# Historical Explanation and Narrative
*Subfield: Philosophy of Science · Philosophy of History · Also known as: narrative vs nomological explanation; history as "one damned thing after another"*

## The question
What kind of explanation is a historical explanation? On one model, to explain an event is to subsume it under a law: given the law and the initial conditions, the event had to follow, and the explanation therefore also tells you what *would* have happened under other conditions. Much historical explanation looks nothing like that. It tells you a story — a chain of particular causes, each plausible, none an instance of any known law connecting the kinds of things the story is about. Is that a defect, a second-class kind of explanation waiting to be replaced by laws? Or are some domains simply historical, so that narrative is not their failed theory but their correct form? This corpus's fullest treatment of the question comes from an unexpected direction: Fodor and Piattelli-Palmarini's *What Darwin Got Wrong* (book 011) concludes that the adaptationist explanations which actually persuade are historical narratives rather than law-subsumptions — and generalises the point to history itself. The dedicated textbook for this subfield, Mark Day's *The Philosophy of History: An Introduction* (book 010), is **absent from the corpus** — its extraction folder contains a different book entirely (see "Sources read") — so Day's own treatment of historical explanation, narrative, and objectivity cannot be stated here, and this entry is built only from what was actually read.

## The 90-second version
The Carneades lecture [Philosophy of History vs History of Philosophy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TGkdxsz2Gek) frames the field this entry belongs to — distilled here, not quoted. *History of philosophy* documents what past philosophers argued; *philosophy of history* asks questions about history itself: what does history consist of — periods, individual actions, civilisations? Does it have a direction or an end, and by what measure would we judge progress? And, most pressingly for this entry, how can we *know* about the past at all: what counts as justification for facts in the distant past, and are we allowed weaker justification for temporally distant events than for present ones? The lecturer is candid that the channel — steeped in the analytic tradition, where the subject is less common than in the Continental one — has barely covered philosophy of history, and names Hegel as its central, and famously difficult, figure. The detailed treatment in this entry — historical explanation as narrative rather than law — is then distilled not from the lecture but from book 011, chs. 7–9, and is marked as textbook material wherever it appears below.

## Positions

### The nomological model (as book 011 characterises it)
On the covering-law picture, an explanation subsumes an event under a natural law: it cites a property of the event in virtue of which a law applies to it, and because laws support counterfactuals, the explanation also settles what would have happened otherwise. "Nothing has a nomological explanation unless it belongs to a natural kind." The authors take the model's virtues to be truisms: prediction and counterfactual support are what such explanation *is*. Their question is not whether the model is coherent but whether the explanations we actually accept, in evolutionary biology and in history, are instances of it.

### The narrative model: plausible causal chains, post hoc
Historical explanations "offer (not laws but) plausible narratives; narratives that purport to articulate the causal chain of events leading to the event that is to be explained".

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 7, closing sections — a formalization of the authors' stated contrast):
- P1. Nomological explanations rest on relations among *properties* and therefore support counterfactuals; historical narratives rest on causal relations among *events* and therefore do not.
- P2. The explanations that persuade in the contested domains have the second form: "Napoleon lost at Waterloo because it had been raining for days, and the ground was too muddy for cavalry to charge" — yet "it doesn't… follow that there are laws that connect the amount of mud on the ground with the outcomes of battles".
- ∴ C. Such explanations are inherently post hoc: given the known outcome, a plausible chain can be told — but "there would have been a plausible story to explain what happened whoever had won", so prediction and retrodiction are asymmetric, and several reasonable accounts of the same event can coexist with "nothing to choose between them".

**The authors' concession, stated with the argument** (source: book 011, ch. 7): none of this impugns narratives. "Historical narratives are, as far as we know, perfectly OK; certainly they are often thoroughly persuasive." Nor do they deny that the events narrated fall under *some* laws: Waterloo "must have been subsumed by some covering laws or other… laws of the mechanics of middle-sized objects" — the claim is only that there are no laws *about mud so described, or about battles so described*, still less laws connecting them, which is what law-subsumption *of the explanation as given* would require.

### The application: adaptationist explanation is natural history
The distinction is load-bearing in the book because it is offered as the rescue — and the demotion — of adaptationism. (The argument that adaptationism *needs* rescuing is the subject of the companion entry; here it is the rescue that matters.)

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 7, "Then what kind of theory is the theory of natural selection?"):
- P1. Some textbook adaptationist explanations plainly do give reasons why phenotypes are as they are — why there are many t1s and few t2s.
- P2. What they provide, on inspection, are historical narratives: plausible post-hoc causal chains, not laws — exactly as with Waterloo's mud, or with case-by-case stories of why this man got rich (heredity, acuity, luck, highway robbery), where "it is most unlikely that there are generalizations that are lawful… that specify the various situations in which it is possible to get rich".
- ∴ C. "If adaptationist explanations are species of historical narratives, everything can be saved from the wreckage except the notion of selection-for." The mechanism of evolution is then not a theory's subject matter but "the vignettes that natural history retails case by case. Evolution is a kind of history, and both are just one damned thing after another."

Natural history, so conceived, has two marks, both drawn from ch. 8: it is **invariably post hoc** ("lacking observations of spiders, nothing… could have predicted that there are creatures that have the spider's kind of adaptation" — somebody watched how spiders make a living and worked backwards) and it **differs wildly from case to case** (the spider's web story and the oak's seed story "have little or nothing in common; there aren't… laws — or even reliable empirical generalizations — about their mechanisms of adaptation"). It also collapses across levels: what fixes a phenotype may be weather, salinity, a prey species' biochemistry, the anatomy of a birth canal, or cosmic radiation, "or all of these acting at once" (ch. 9).

### The generalisation: there is no level of historical explanation
Ch. 9 states the entry's most sweeping claim, and states it as a comparison. Psychology *has* a level of explanation — the intentional level, whose generalisations (about belief, perception, memory) plausibly support counterfactuals. Historical phenomena, the authors claim, do not constitute such a level at all.

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 9 — a formalization of the chapter's closing movement):
- P1. A theoretical domain requires generalisations at its own level that support counterfactuals (as intentional psychology's plausibly do, and as putative "laws of selection" — traits per se vs fitness — plausibly do not, because everything depends on the whole phenotype and the whole ecology).
- P2. Historical explanation chains wildly heterogeneous causes — "for want of a nail, the shoe was lost; for want of the shoe, the horse was lost…" — in a vocabulary belonging to no science, under no laws or reliable generalisations that any science articulates.
- ∴ C. "Marx and many other nineteenth-century luminaries notwithstanding, there is no level of historical explanation; a fortiori, there is no theory of history." Darwin's mistake is paired with Marx's: each "imagined that history is a theoretical domain; but what there is, in fact, is only a heterogeneity of causes and effects."

**Scope note (the authors' own).** The claim is not that historical processes are uncaused, indeterministic, or unlawful *tout court*: "this is not in the least to suggest that the fixation of phenotypes is other than a deterministic, causal and lawful process through and through." What is denied is a *unitary theory at the historical level* — not causation, but a level. Their preferred replacement image is contagion, not selection: organisms "catch" their phenotypes from their ecologies as they catch colds, by processes that run simultaneously at many levels of organisation and differ from case to case. "Well, actually there are lots" is their answer to "what is the mechanism of the fixation of phenotypes?" — and, by extension, their answer about mechanisms of historical change generally.

## Thought experiments
- **Waterloo** (book 011, ch. 7). Napoleon lost, we're told, because days of rain left the ground too muddy for cavalry. Persuasive — but no law connects mud to battle outcomes, Wellington might equally be explained by the Prussians' arrival or Napoleon's decline, and a story would have been available whoever won. Meant to isolate the narrative form: sufficient-cause chains without counterfactual support. (The same example appears in the book's prospectus, where Napoleon's defeat is explained by "his age, his upbringing, his social class and his personality type… the weather and how much caffeine there was in his morning coffee" — historical explanation as "egregiously 'multilevel'".)
- **The banana peel** (book 011, ch. 7). "She fell because she slipped on a banana peel." Very likely true; but "there's no law — there's not even a statistical law — that has 'banana peel' in its antecedent and 'slipped and fell' in its consequent." The minimal case of explanation-without-subsumption.
- **For want of a nail** (book 011, ch. 9). The nursery-rhyme chain — nail, shoe, horse, rider, message, battle — "may well explain the loss of the battle", but its transitions instance no science's laws. Meant to show that a narrative can be a complete explanation while belonging to no theoretical level.
- **The extinction of the dinosaurs** (book 011, ch. 7). Small mammals outlasted large dinosaurs — because of their smallness (egg-snatching), or because of their temperature tolerance (which mattered only given the meteor)? Both stories are plausible; the narratives on offer support neither counterfactual against the other. Pressures any claim that a narrative explanation settles what *mattered*.
- **Getting rich** (book 011, ch. 7). For every rich person there is some explanation — inheritance, cupidity, luck, robbery — irreducibly context-dependent across eras (no one can now get rich as Genghis Khan did). "Adaptationist explanations of the evolution of heritable traits are really a lot like that": convincing stories, case by case, without a theory of getting rich — or of adaptation — behind them.

## Where the debate stands
Within this corpus, one-sidedly — and the entry says so rather than smoothing it over. The only sustained treatment read is Fodor & Piattelli-Palmarini's, and their verdict is stated at full strength: historical (including adaptationist) explanation is narrative, narratives do not support counterfactuals, and there is no theoretical level — hence no theory — of history. Two qualifications travel with that verdict in their own text: narratives are legitimate, often rationally compelling explanations, not failed science; and the events they recount remain fully causal and law-governed at *other* levels (physics, mechanics), so nothing supernatural or indeterministic follows. What the corpus cannot supply is the other side: no covering-law theorist (Hempel is mentioned in book 011 only for the heart/function puzzle, not for his model of historical explanation), no narrative theorist, and no Day — so questions the subfield standardly treats as central (whether narratives can be objectively better or worse; whether historical knowledge of a singular past is knowledge at all; whether history has direction) are raised by the framing lecture but answered by no text read for this entry. They stay open here, pending a genuine extraction of book 010.
