# Selection-For and the Critique of Adaptationism
*Subfield: Philosophy of Science (philosophy of biology) · Also known as: the free-rider problem; the intensionality of selection-for; Fodor & Piattelli-Palmarini's critique of natural selection*

## The question
Evolutionary theory says that creatures with adaptive traits are *selected*. Adaptationism says more: that creatures are *selected for* their adaptive traits — that the trait is why they flourished. Are these the same claim? Fodor and Piattelli-Palmarini's *What Darwin Got Wrong* (2010) argues that they are not, that inferring the second from the first is a fallacy, and that no mindless process could ever be sensitive to the difference. On their account, this is not a repairable gap in the theory of natural selection but a crack in its foundations. This entry presents their argument at the strength at which they state it — together with the concessions and counter-considerations the book itself enters — and does not present the argument as settled: the book is a polemic by two authors, and the standing of its conclusion is part of what the entry records.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture covers natural selection, Darwin, or adaptationism (the lecture folder was searched by title and by full text), so this version is distilled from the textbook itself — book 011, Terms of Engagement and chs. 1 and 6 — and is marked accordingly.* A trait can spread through a population in two very different ways: because it helps its bearers survive and reproduce, or because it is glued — genetically, developmentally, geometrically — to another trait that does. Gould and Lewontin made this vivid with the spandrels of St Mark's: domed churches have arches because arches hold the dome up, and the little triangles between the arches, the spandrels, are simply what geometry gives you for free — they are there *because of* the arches, not *for* anything. To say which of two always-together traits is the real adaptation and which is the free-rider, you have to answer a counterfactual: *if the two came apart, which would selection keep?* Breeders and architects can answer such questions because they have minds — they can think about what didn't happen. Natural selection, the argument runs, cannot: it is a purely local process that responds only to actual causes in the actual world, and merely possible predators, merely possible environments, and merely possible separations of traits exert no pressure at all. Yet the vocabulary of adaptationism — *selected-for*, *function*, *ecological niche*, *problem of adaptation* — is built entirely out of such distinctions. Strip out the phantom breeder ("Mother Nature") who alone could make them, and, the authors claim, the theory of natural selection does not become false the way Skinner's learning theory became false; it becomes *empty*.

## Positions

### The target: adaptationism, as the authors characterise it
Adaptationism is the thesis that natural selection is the mechanism of evolution: that the distribution of phenotypic traits in populations is explained, in the main, by exogenous (ecological) selection filtering randomly generated heritable variation. The book is careful about what it is *not* attacking. Evolutionary theory has two parts — a **genealogy of species** (GS: species are related by descent, plausibly from a common ancestor) and the **theory of natural selection** (NS). "We have no quarrel to pick with the genealogy of species; it is perfectly possible — in fact, entirely likely — that GS is true even if NS is not." Both authors are atheists and naturalists, seeking mechanical explanations without final causes; the book is explicitly not about God, intelligent design, or creationism. Its contention is only that NS cannot be the causal mechanism that generates the taxonomy of species.

**The confusion at the core** (source: book 011, Terms of Engagement):
- P1. Adaptationist theories trade on two claims: (1) evolution is a process in which creatures *with* adaptive traits are selected; (2) evolution is a process in which creatures are selected *for* their adaptive traits.
- P2. Darwinism is committed to inferring (2) from (1).
- ∴ C. That inference is where the theory breaks: it is invalid — "in fact it's what philosophers call an 'intensional fallacy'" — and, the authors argue across chs. 6–7, there is no way to repair it consistent with naturalism.

### The Skinner parallel: one architecture, two theories
Before the selection-for argument proper, the book argues that the theory of natural selection and B. F. Skinner's theory of operant conditioning are "identical in all but name": both are black boxes mapping a distribution of traits in a population (phenotypes; stimulus–response repertoires) onto a successor distribution as a function of environmental variables, and both endorse the same six proprietary constraints — iterativity, environmentalism, gradualism, monotonicity, locality, mindlessness. Inside the boxes, both are *generate-and-test* theories: a random generator (mutation; random operants) plus an exogenous filter (selection; reinforcement), "and that is all".

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 1 — a formalization of the chapter's closing move):
- P1. Skinner's learning theory, built on this architecture, is dead beyond resuscitation, and the reasons are principled (the Chomsky critique and its successors): learning is not the exogenous filtering of randomly emitted behaviour.
- P2. Darwin's theory of evolution proposes "essentially the same mechanisms to compute essentially similar functions under essentially identical constraints."
- ∴ C. "The position that retains the second but not the first is not stable." With a caveat the authors insist on: Skinner's theory is *false* (associationism is a story about filtering, just a wrong one), whereas Darwin's, they will claim, is *empty* — it has no account of the filtering at all.

### The biological argument: the generator is not random, and the filter is not in charge
Part One of the book argues empirically — from the biology literature as the authors read it — that the generate-and-test picture misdescribes the machinery. Phenotypes are not offered to selection as random, independent variants: between genotype and phenotype stand layers of internal filters (developmental processes and conserved master genes in evo-devo; gene regulatory networks, entrenchment, robustness and pleiotropy; chaperones that bank hidden variation; alternative splicing), and fixation can proceed without selection at all (molecular drive, biased gene conversion). "We think of natural selection as tuning the piano, not as composing the melodies."

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 2 — a formalization of the chapter's conclusion):
- P1. Before any phenotype can be "offered" to selection, "a host of internal constraints have to be satisfied"; mutations, even if random at source, do not produce random phenotypes.
- P2. Traits are not independently selectable units ("beanbag genetics" is false: genes are jointly regulated, master genes move many traits at once, developmental modules and coordination bind traits into complexes).
- ∴ C. "There is… no single 'arrow' connecting a random generator of genetic diversity to the phenotypes on which exogenous selection acts" — so the more the internalist story is true, "the less work is left for appeals to natural selection to do."

**The authors' own caveat, entered in the same Part** (source: book 011, ch. 3, conclusion): the biologists whose work Part One surveys — including the discoverers of these mechanisms — mostly "still today endorse natural selection as the determinant par excellence of the course of evolution", and determined defenders treat the same results as *supporting* selection. The authors "confess to not understanding that", and at this stage "are prepared to settle for a stand-off": the empirical case shows adaptationism is "less than apodictic", no more. The book's claim to a *fatal* flaw rests on Part Two, not on Part One.

**A second empirical strand: the laws of form** (source: book 011, ch. 5). Some biological structures are optimal or near-optimal (quarter-power scaling laws, wiring-minimised nervous systems, optimal foraging ratios), and the spaces of candidate solutions are often astronomically large.
- P1. By neo-Darwinism's own account, selection only *satisfices* — it finds locally satisfactory solutions and cannot optimise — and blind search of those spaces by random generation plus filtering is intractable.
- P2. Optimal and near-optimal solutions nevertheless exist, across unrelated lineages.
- ∴ C. "If natural selection cannot optimize, then something else must be involved" — plausibly physico-chemical law and self-organisation. The authors are frank that this is a dilemma, not an explanation: of the parade cases "nobody today really has a clue to a solution", and they claim no rival mechanism.

### The conceptual argument: selection cannot tell arches from spandrels
Part Two's argument turns on *free-riders*: traits that spread because they are linked to traits that affect fitness (spandrels on arches; heart noises on blood-pumping; in the book's laboratory case, a bacterium's capacity to digest a second sugar, which rode along with selection on the first and silently blocked a third). Distinguishing the selected-for trait from its free-rider always requires a counterfactual — what would happen if the coextensive traits came apart. And that, the authors argue, is a resource natural selection does not have.

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 6 — the authors' own numbered argument, lightly compressed):
- P1. Selection-for is a causal process.
- P2. Actual causal relations aren't sensitive to counterfactual states of affairs: if it wasn't the case that A, then the fact that A's being the case *would have* caused B does not explain B.
- P3. But the distinction between traits that are selected-for and their free-riders turns on the truth (or falsity) of relevant counterfactuals.
- P4. So if T and T′ are coextensive, selection cannot distinguish the case in which T free-rides on T′ from the case in which T′ free-rides on T.
- ∴ C. "So the claim that selection is the mechanism of evolution cannot be true" — a theory of evolution must explain the distribution of traits by their connection to fitness, and selection cannot perform the individuation that requires.

**Why minds are the exception, and why that doesn't save Darwin** (source: book 011, ch. 6). Architects *can* select for arches rather than spandrels, and breeders *can* select for market value rather than root systems, because minds represent counterfactuals: an architect can think "if I took the spandrels out, the dome would fall", and let a merely possible state of affairs govern actual behaviour. Gould and Lewontin's paradigm case of selection-for, and Darwin's own model of natural selection, are both cases of *mental* causation with the minds then abstracted away. "But that… is what we are saying can't be done." Natural selection is mindless — Darwin was right about that — and what is mindless cannot register the counterfactuals on which trait-individuation depends.

### The four exits, and why the authors close them
Ch. 7 considers the replies available to adaptationism — in effect, the objections to the argument above — and answers each. They are recorded here as the authors present them; the book engages no named living respondent at length, and Sober (1993) is noted by the authors themselves as the honourable exception who took the counterfactual question seriously.

**Objection 1 — Mother Nature.** Take the breeder analogy literally: what selection selects-for is what it "has in mind". → **Reply:** then the counterfactuals are grounded, but in a fiction. "Natural selection doesn't have a mind. A fortiori, it has nothing in mind when it selects among frogs." Mother Nature, the Blind Watchmaker, and the Selfish Gene are intentional systems only as metaphors, and "fictions can't select things, however hard they try."

**Objection 2 — Laws of selection.** Laws support counterfactuals without minds; perhaps there are laws of the form "all else equal, t1s beat t2s in ecology E with probability p". → **Reply:** fitness is massively context-sensitive. Whether a trait helps depends on the whole phenotype it is embedded in (phenotypes are "fusions of traits", not Swiss apples that fall apart into discrete constituents) and on the whole ecology; outcomes of competitions are interaction effects. So there are no nomological generalisations about which *traits* win — no underlying relation obscured by noise, as with genuine ceteris paribus laws, but "just nothing to choose between" rival generalisations. And where coextensive traits are *nomologically* coextensive, there may be no nearby world in which one is altered and the other left intact, so even laws could not underwrite the trait-level counterfactuals adaptationism needs.

**Objection 3 — Sober's sieve.** A sieve that passes only small red marbles sorts *for size*, not colour — so the select/select-for distinction can be drawn mechanically. → **Reply:** we know what the sieve sorts for only because we know its endogenous mechanism, which is exactly what adaptationist explanations typically lack; and the intuition is unstable — sift flour and the good stuff falls through, pan for gold and the good stuff stays behind, so even for the sieve, what counts as "selected" waits on a sorter's purposes. "Extensions don't determine intensions."

**Objection 4 — A theory schema.** Adaptationism claims only that for each adaptation there *is* some trait that was selected-for and some ecological problem it solved, without saying which. → **Reply:** that is an empirical claim only if "adaptation", "selection-for" and "ecological problem" are independently defined; in practice they are interdefined, so "adaptations are always solutions to ecological problems" collapses into a truism — "like 'bachelors always turn out to be unmarried'".

### The exquisite-fit argument, and the niche dilemma
Ch. 8 addresses what the authors call perhaps the strongest argument *for* adaptationism: organisms fit their environments too well for it to be luck, and only selection by those environments seems to explain the fit naturalistically.

**Canonical argument** (source: book 011, ch. 8 — a formalization of the chapter's dilemma):
- P1. A creature's *environment* is the world, common to all; its *ecology* (or niche) is, by contrast, "whatever-it-is-about-the-world that makes its phenotype viable" — so "ecology" and "phenotype" are interdefined, as are "niche", "adaptation", "problem of adaptation", "good for", and "selection-for".
- P2. A claim explained by interdefined terms is tautological, not contingent: if a species fails to fit a supposed niche exactly, it follows only that that wasn't exactly its niche. "If seagulls didn't have wings, their ecology wouldn't be airy."
- ∴ C. The exquisite fit is "either true but tautological or irrelevant": fit to the *world* needs no selection theory (anything alive is ipso facto adapted to it), and fit to a *niche* is guaranteed by the individuation of niches. What genuinely needs explaining — in virtue of what a phenotype can make a living, and by what historical process it came to — is the business of natural history, not of a theory of selection.

## Thought experiments
- **The spandrels of San Marco** (Gould & Lewontin 1979, via book 011, ch. 6). Churches with domes need arches; spandrels come free with the geometry. Adaptationist storytelling that asks what spandrels are *for* invents functions for by-products. Meant to show: coextensive traits divide into selected-for and free-rider, and surface fit cannot tell you which is which. In the book's hands it also turns against its inventors: Gould & Lewontin's own case is an architect's — a mind's — selection.
- **The frog and the ambient black nuisance** (book 011, ch. 6). Frogs snap at flies; suppose flies are locally coextensive with "ambient black nuisances" (ABNs). Every actual snap is both a fly-snap and an ABN-snap. Which disposition did the frog evolve? Only a counterfactual world — ABNs that aren't flies — could decide, and natural selection, confined to the actual world, cannot consult it. Pressures: any causal theory that fixes a trait (or a content) by actual causes alone.
- **The three sugars** (Hall's *E. coli* experiments, book 011, ch. 4). Selecting bacteria for digesting sugar 1 yields, as a free-rider, digestion of sugar 2 — which turns out to block, permanently, digestion of sugar 3; the order of selective steps is irreversible and, in nature, unreconstructible. Experimenters could detect the free-rider because they could run the splitting experiments; "natural selection doesn't perform experiments." Meant to show free-riding is not a marginal exception but undetectable-in-principle wherever traits are coextensive in the selective environment.
- **Sober's sieve** (Sober 1993, via book 011, ch. 7). Marbles differing in size and colour pass a sieve; all and only the small ones are red. Intuition: it sorts for size. The authors use it as a test of whether the select/select-for distinction can be grounded without intentions or laws — and argue it cannot (see Objection 3).
- **Pigs with wings** (book 011, chs. 8–9). No winged pigs exist, or ever did — not because winged pigs lost competitions, but because "there is no place on pigs to put them": the phenotype is endogenously impossible. Meant to show that the *absences* in the space of forms, which a theory of evolution should explain, are explained by internal constraints, about which selection has nothing to say.

## Where the debate stands
As far as this corpus can show: unresolved, and presented by its only source as unresolved in a specific way. Fodor & Piattelli-Palmarini claim the *conceptual* argument of Part Two is fatal — selection-for is intensional, counterfactuals decide trait-individuation, and a mindless local process cannot be sensitive to counterfactuals — while conceding that the *empirical* argument of Part One reaches only a stand-off: the biologists they cite largely still endorse natural selection, read the same evo-devo and epigenetic results as compatible with or supportive of it, and the authors offer no alternative mechanism ("we don't know what the mechanism of evolution is… nobody knows"). Their residual positive claim is deflationary: where adaptationist explanations work, they work as *historical narratives*, case by case (see the companion entry). Nothing in the corpus states the adaptationist rejoinder in its own voice, so this entry's "positions" are the critique and the replies the critics themselves chose to answer. The critique must therefore be cited as *Fodor & Piattelli-Palmarini's argument*, not as a result about natural selection.
