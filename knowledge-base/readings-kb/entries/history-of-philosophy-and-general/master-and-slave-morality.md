# Master Morality, Slave Morality, and the Problem of Morality Itself
*Subfield: History of Philosophy & General · Also known as: Nietzsche's critique of morality; the natural history of morals*

## The question
Where do moral values come from — and what is a morality *for*? Philosophers, Nietzsche charges, have always taken their own morality as simply given and looked for a rational foundation underneath it; nobody had treated morality itself as a problem. What happens when you do — when you compare many moralities across peoples and ages and ask what type of human being each one expresses and serves? *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), the corpus's Nietzsche primary text, answers with a typology: two basic ways of valuing, born from two opposite social positions, whose mixture is still the moral air Europe breathes.

## The 90-second version
Nietzsche is the philosopher of "God is dead" — not a corpse in heaven, the lecture explains, but the collapse of the Christian worldview under secular and scientific pressure, and with it the standing morality. That collapse opens an era of *nihilism*: if the old values lose their ground, morality and meaning can look unjustifiable altogether — a result Nietzsche predicted and opposed, arguing we should pass *through* nihilism, tearing down the old structures, to create new values rather than stay in the wreckage; his Übermensch is that value-creator, closer (the lecture insists, against the Nazi misuse arranged by his sister's doctored posthumous editions) to an Aristotelian figure of excellence than to a master race. The nihilism lecture adds the spectrum this creates: anti-nihilists who say morality needs God, atheist moral cognitivists who keep morality on other grounds, non-cognitivists, and only at the far end actual nihilists. Nietzsche's own move in *Beyond Good and Evil* is diagnostic before it is creative: first find out what moralities there have been and what they are symptoms *of*. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Who Was Friedrich Nietzsche?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ugkxY1ntDu8), [What is Nihilism?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B9aMLkwpf0U).)

## Positions

### The problem of morality itself (Nietzsche, Part Five)
Before any typology: the discipline that should have produced one never saw its object. "In all 'science of morals' so far one thing was *lacking*, strange as it may sound: the problem of morality itself; what was lacking was any suspicion that there was something problematic here."

**Canonical argument** (source: Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, Part Five, §186):
- P1. Moral philosophers "knew the facts of morality only very approximately in arbitrary extracts or in accidental epitomes — for example, as the morality of their environment, their class, their church, the spirit of their time, their climate and part of the world."
- P2. What they called supplying "a rational foundation for morality" was therefore "merely a scholarly variation of the common *faith* in the prevalent morality… in the last analysis a kind of denial that this morality might ever be considered problematic."
- ∴ C. The real problems of morality "emerge only when we compare *many* moralities"; the justified task so far is descriptive — "to collect material, to conceptualize and arrange a vast realm of subtle feelings of value" — to prepare a *typology* of morals, since "moralities are also merely a sign language of the affects" (§187).

### Master morality (Nietzsche, Part Nine, §260)
Valuation originates with a ruling group conscious, with delight, of its difference from the ruled. "The noble human being separates from himself those in whom the opposite of such exalted, proud states finds expression: he despises them."

**Canonical argument** (source: Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, Part Nine, §260 — a formalization of the section's typology):
- P1. Moral designations were "everywhere first applied to *human beings* and only later, derivatively, to actions," and the noble type "experiences *itself* as determining values; it does not need approval; it judges, 'what is harmful to me is harmful in itself'… it is *value-creating*."
- P2. Its governing opposition is therefore "good" and *bad* — "approximately the same as 'noble' and 'contemptible'" (the opposition of "good" and "evil" "has a different origin") — and what it honours is itself: fullness of power, pride, severity toward oneself; it helps the unfortunate "not, or almost not, from pity, but prompted more by an urge begotten by excess of power."
- ∴ C. Master morality is "self-glorification": faith in oneself and hostility to "selflessness" belong to it "just as definitely… as does a slight disdain and caution regarding compassionate feelings and a 'warm heart.'"

### Slave morality (Nietzsche, Part Nine, §260)
"Suppose the violated, oppressed, suffering, unfree, who are uncertain of themselves and weary, moralize: what will their moral valuations have in common?"

**Canonical argument** (source: Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, Part Nine, §260 — a formalization of the same section):
- P1. The slave's eye "is not favorable to the virtues of the powerful: he is skeptical and suspicious, *subtly* suspicious, of all the 'good' that is honored there — he would like to persuade himself that even their happiness is not genuine."
- P2. What gets honoured instead are "those qualities… which serve to ease existence for those who suffer: here pity, the complaisant and obliging hand, the warm heart, patience, industry, humility, and friendliness are honored — for here these are the most useful qualities."
- ∴ C. "Slave morality is essentially a morality of utility," and its governing opposition is "good" and *evil*: "into evil one's feelings project power and dangerousness, a certain terribleness, subtlety, and strength that does not permit contempt to develop" — while its own "good" human being must be *undangerous*: "good-natured, easy to deceive, a little stupid perhaps, *un bonhomme*."

Nietzsche's own caveats, stated in the same section, are part of the position and are not to be trimmed off: in higher, mixed cultures the two types mediate, interpenetrate, and misunderstand each other, "and at times they occur directly alongside each other — even in the same human being, within a *single* soul." And §262 adds the historical sequel: moralities fix a type under constant unfavourable conditions, and when conditions relax, variation erupts and "the 'individual' appears, obliged to give himself laws."

### The will to truth, questioned (Nietzsche, Part One, §§1–4)
The critique of morality is one instance of the book's opening move: turning on philosophy's own ruling value. "We asked about the *value* of this will. Suppose we want truth: *why not rather* untruth? and uncertainty? even ignorance?" (§1). §2 names the metaphysicians' "faith in opposite values" — that the highest things cannot grow from their opposites — and §4 re-prices judgement itself:

**Canonical argument** (source: Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, Part One, §4):
- P1. "The falseness of a judgment is for us not necessarily an objection to a judgment… The question is to what extent it is life-promoting, life-preserving, species-preserving, perhaps even species-cultivating."
- P2. The falsest judgements (the synthetic a priori among them) are "the most indispensable for us": without "a constant falsification of the world by means of numbers, man could not live."
- ∴ C. "To recognize untruth as a condition of life — that certainly means resisting accustomed value feelings in a dangerous way; and a philosophy that risks this would by that token alone place itself beyond good and evil."

**Objections and standing pressures, as the corpus presents them** — Kaufmann's anthology introduction (book 018) records the catastrophic misreading: the Nazis "had no use at all for any of his books as a whole" yet "brazenly used him, too"; the Nietzsche lecture makes the same defence against the Übermensch-as-master-race reading. Kaufmann also anthologizes Nietzsche inside *existentialism* (book 018, ch. FOUR, "Live Dangerously") — selections on self-overcoming — which is a different Nietzsche emphasis than Book 019's moral typology, and the two should not be conflated. The corpus contains no systematic rebuttal of the typology itself (no defence of pity-morality from within its own texts, apart from the positions Nietzsche is describing); the entry therefore reports the typology as Nietzsche's claim, historically bold and empirically underargued in these pages — he offers it, by his own §186 standard, as the beginning of a descriptive science, not its completion.

## Thought experiments
- **The two origins** (§260). One act of valuation, staged twice: from the ruling group's delight in itself, and from the suffering group's need to endure. Same words ("good"), different opposites (bad vs. evil), different virtues. Meant to show that a morality's content is legible from the position of whoever made it — which pressures any morality that presents itself as positionless.
- **The barbarian beginning** (§257). Every higher culture "has *begun*" with "men of prey… more *whole* human beings" hurling themselves on weaker races — Nietzsche explicitly warns against "humanitarian illusions about the origins of an aristocratic society." A deliberately hard case for any progress story about morality's origins.

## Where the debate stands
The corpus stages Nietzsche's critique at full strength and surrounds it with defences against the political misreading, but it does not stage the scholarly counter-arguments (historical challenges to the typology; defences of compassion-based morality on independent grounds), and Nietzsche's own text concedes the descriptive work is preliminary. Two things the texts do settle between themselves: the typology is two ideal types, not two peoples — mixture "within a single soul" is built into §260 — and the critique of the will to truth (§§1–4) applies to the typology too: by §4's standard its worth is the question of what it promotes, not a correspondence it demonstrates.
