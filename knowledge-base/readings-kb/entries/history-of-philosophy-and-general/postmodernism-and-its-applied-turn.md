# Postmodernism and Its Applied Turn
*Subfield: History of Philosophy & General · Also known as: the postmodernism debate; "Theory" (capital T, in book 003's usage)*

## The question
What is postmodernism, and what happened to it? The corpus contains two incompatible-looking stagings. The Carneades lectures present a loose family of positions — sceptical of single right answers and of *metanarratives*, the broad stories (progress, classical mechanics, a faith, a political programme) by reference to which smaller claims get judged — and warn that treating this family as one worldview is the standard beginner's error. Pluckrose and Lindsay's *Cynical Theories* (2020), the corpus's book-length treatment, argues the opposite way: underneath the diversity sit two principles and four themes that never changed, and when those were made politically actionable in the 1980s–90s and then asserted as dogma after about 2010, they became an illiberal ideology the authors call "Social Justice" — which, they argue, harms the very equality causes it claims. This entry presents each staging as its source presents it; the second is a partisan argument and is labelled as its authors' throughout.

## The 90-second version
Postmodernism is notoriously hard to define — the lecture opens with the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s concession that "that postmodernism is indefinable is a well-known truth" — because it names loosely related movements across art, architecture, literature, politics, and philosophy, not one doctrine: you can be an architectural postmodernist and a philosophical modernist. Its common thread is a contrast. The modernist picture is like computing the digits of π: one correct answer, approached ever more closely — one right way, independent of history and culture, whether in art, justice, or truth. The postmodern picture is like inventing new uses for an eraser: many ways, assessable only in context. Following Lyotard, the lecture defines the stance as scepticism toward metanarratives: the big stories can be used to test claims *inside* a context, but the stories themselves cannot be proved — including Lyotard's own targets, the narratives that we are moving toward full knowledge and toward a just society. On this telling, postmodernism is a *descriptive, metaphysical* thesis about how things are, not an ethical programme for how they should be — so disliking its implications is not an argument against it. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is Postmodernism?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RnXxvfo-jJU), [What is a Metanarrative?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VYdwAulpWqw).)

## Positions

### Postmodernism as pluralism about the one right way (the lectures' staging)
**Thesis** (source: Carneades, "What is Postmodernism?"): across its fields, postmodernism doubts "whether there is only one right way to do something, and whether we can judge the final product objectively by rules" — experience is pluralistic, outcomes depend on method and on historical, linguistic, and cultural context, and there is no single goal being approached. Metanarratives organize judgement within a context but cannot themselves be verified.

**Objections the lecture itself clears away first** — pointing at postmodern painting to reject postmodern philosophy (the family is not one position, so refuting one member refutes nothing else); and treating postmodernism as an ethical thesis to be rejected for its unwelcome implications (it is offered as a description; "if this is a problem for you, it is not the fault of postmodernism").

### The two principles and four themes (Pluckrose & Lindsay's analytic core)
The authors concede the diversity — postmodernism "isn't just hard to define; it's also notoriously difficult to summarize" — and then claim an invariant core "that [has] remained central to all the Theory that followed":

**Thesis** (source: Pluckrose & Lindsay, *Cynical Theories*, ch. 1):
- **The postmodern knowledge principle**: "Radical skepticism about whether objective knowledge or truth is obtainable and a commitment to cultural constructivism."
- **The postmodern political principle**: "A belief that society is formed of systems of power and hierarchies, which decide what can be known and how."
- **Four themes**: (1) the blurring of boundaries; (2) the power of language; (3) cultural relativism; (4) the loss of the individual and the universal.

Their characterization goes further than the lectures': postmodernism so understood "raised such radical doubts about the structure of thought and society that it is ultimately a form of cynicism," born of postwar disillusionment — with progress, with empire, above all with Marxism — and of suspicion toward science as the legitimator of "truths" and their metanarratives.

### The applied turn, then reification (Pluckrose & Lindsay's genealogy)
**Canonical argument** (source: Pluckrose & Lindsay, *Cynical Theories*, Introduction and chs. 2, 8 — a formalization of the book's historical thesis, at the strength the authors state it):
- P1. The original postmodernism (Foucault, Derrida, Lyotard, 1960s–70s) was deconstructive and politically paralysing: its principles, held as sceptical tools, dissolve every claim including their own.
- P2. In the late 1980s and 1990s a second generation made it actionable — *applied postmodernism* (ch. 2): postcolonial Theory, queer Theory, critical race Theory, and intersectional feminism (chs. 3–6) took the principles and themes "to be real" for the specific systems they targeted, keeping the scepticism for everyone else's knowledge while treating oppression as objective.
- P3. Around 2010 the stance hardened again — *reified postmodernism* (ch. 8): the principles and themes were fused into a single methodology, "Social Justice scholarship," held with "the sort of firm conviction associated with religious adherence," in which disagreement is itself evidence of complicity ("thou shalt not disagree with Theory").
- ∴ C. (The authors' conclusion.) A worldview that began in radical doubt has become "one of the least tolerant and most authoritarian ideologies that the world has had to deal with since the widespread decline of communism" — and, they argue, it does not further the racial, gender, and LGBT equality the authors say they support.

### The liberal alternative (Pluckrose & Lindsay's positive case)
**Canonical argument** (source: Pluckrose & Lindsay, *Cynical Theories*, Introduction and ch. 10 — the authors' argument):
- P1. Liberalism — "political democracy, limitations on the powers of government, the development of universal human rights, legal equality for all adult citizens, freedom of expression, respect for the value of viewpoint diversity and honest debate, respect for evidence and reason" — is a framework within which people of varied views "can rationally debate the options for public policy," and historical struggles for justice "ha[ve] always been strongest when [they have] cast [themselves] as the defender of liberal values universally."
- P2. Theory and liberalism are point-by-point opposed: "Liberalism sees knowledge as something we can learn about reality, more or less objectively; Theory sees knowledges as completely created by humans… Liberalism accepts the correspondence theory of truth…; Theory promotes the idea that truth is a 'language game'… Liberalism believes in progress; Theory is radically cynical about the possibility of progress."
- ∴ C. The remedy is not counter-dogma but recommitment: "counter these ideas through a clearly articulated mass commitment to the universally liberal principles and rigorous, evidence-based scholarship that define modernity" — "Liberalism is not perfect. Nevertheless it is the antidote to Theory."

### A neighbouring case, staged differently in the corpus: critical race theory (the lecture's staging)
Because book 003's ch. 5 treats CRT as applied postmodernism ("ending racism by seeing it everywhere"), the corpus's own lecture on CRT belongs in this entry as a control. It defines CRT as "a legal and philosophical viewpoint" asking why racial inequality of outcomes persists *after* legal racial equality was won, and stages it as a critique of liberalism from inside the equality project: equality (same treatment) vs. equity (treatment commensurate with need); systems — its example is redlining — that can harm a *group* without any individual being singled out by a racist actor; and a challenge to Rawls' veil of ignorance, which may blind choosers to pre-existing injustice. It then stages the objections (the moral case for equal treatment; the operational difficulty of race-based remedies; groups vs. individual rights; blame for ancestors' acts) and CRT-side replies (outcome equality as the goal; wealth- and need-based policies that disproportionately help the historically marginalized without race-based criteria). None of this maps neatly onto book 003's account — the two sources are describing the same name with different centres of gravity, and a reader of the corpus should know both exist. (Source: Carneades, [What is Critical Race Theory?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2c4cluLqfpY).)

## Thought experiments
- **π vs. the eraser** (the postmodernism lecture). Every discipline is implicitly assigned a model: is it like computing digits of π (one answer, convergence) or like inventing uses for an eraser (many answers, context-bound assessment)? The lecture's own use is ecumenical — science may fit the first model and art the second — which is itself a position in the debate this entry stages.
- **The veil of ignorance, re-entered with history** (the CRT lecture). Rawls' choosers do not know their race; the CRT challenge asks what they also do not know — the pre-existing distribution of wealth and standing they are choosing *into* — and whether equal rules chosen in that ignorance can entrench an unequal start.

## Where the debate stands
Genuinely unsettled, and the corpus shows why: the disputants do not agree on what the subject is. The lectures hold that "postmodernism" names a heterogeneous family whose members can be held independently, so a global verdict — for or against — is a category error; book 003 replies that the family has a genetic core (two principles, four themes) whose career can be followed from sceptical tool to activist dogma, and that the later stages are what now matter socially. Both cannot be assessed from the corpus alone, because the primary texts of the accused tradition are absent from it. What the corpus does establish is the liberal counter-position at full strength (book 003, ch. 10) and at least one applied field (CRT, per the lecture) whose self-presentation — closing outcome gaps that formal equality left — fits book 003's template only partially. The entry therefore leaves the master question open: one family wrongly unified by its critics, or one core rightly traced by them?
