# Existence Precedes Essence: Sartre on Freedom and Self-Deception
*Subfield: History of Philosophy & General (Existentialism) · Also known as: Sartrean existentialism; "condemned to be free"; bad faith (in this corpus, "self-deception")*

## The question
Is a human being a thing with a nature fixed in advance — by God, by biology, by temperament, by upbringing — which then explains, and excuses, what the person does? Or does a person exist first, with no nature given, and become whatever their choices make them — so that nothing, not even a strong feeling or a hard past, can be produced as an excuse? Sartre's 1946 lecture *Existentialism is a Humanism* is the corpus's fullest staging of the second answer, and the *Being and Nothingness* chapter anthologized beside it poses the problem that answer creates: if I am transparent to myself and radically free, how can I possibly lie to myself?

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus treats Sartre or existentialism, so this version is distilled from the textbook texts themselves (Sartre's lecture, book 018, ch. NINE §4) and is marked accordingly.* A paper-knife is made to a conception: its essence — what it is for, how it is made — precedes its existence. Religious doctrine treated man the same way, as an article in the mind of a divine artisan; eighteenth-century atheism dropped the artisan but kept the conception, as "human nature." Sartre's atheistic existentialism drops both: "man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world — and defines himself afterwards." There is no human nature because there is no God to conceive it, and so no determinism and no excuse: "man is condemned to be free" — condemned because he did not create himself, free because, once thrown into the world, "he is responsible for everything he does." Choosing is unavoidable and never private: in choosing myself I fashion an image of man for all. The feelings that accompany this are anguish (total responsibility, with no sign to certify a choice), abandonment (no values written in an intelligible heaven), and despair (relying only on what is within our wills). And the standing temptation is to flee all of it into *self-deception* — playing at being a thing with a fixed nature, as the café waiter plays at being a waiter.

## Positions

### Atheistic existentialism: existence precedes essence (Sartre)
Man "is nothing else but that which he makes of himself. That is the first principle of existentialism." What is true of manufactured things and of the God-made man of doctrine — essence first — is false of us.

**Canonical argument** (source: Sartre, "Existentialism is a Humanism," book 018, ch. NINE §4):
- P1. For an article of manufacture (Sartre's example is the paper-knife), essence precedes existence: the artisan's conception and technique define the thing before it is produced. The traditional doctrine of God as creator, and even godless doctrines of a universal "human nature," treat man on this same model.
- P2. If God does not exist, that model loses its ground for man: "there is no human nature, because there is no God to have a conception of it."
- ∴ C. For man, existence precedes essence: he first exists and encounters himself, and defines himself afterwards — "he will be what he makes of himself."

**Canonical argument — freedom and the absence of excuses** (same source):
- P1. If existence precedes essence, "one will never be able to explain one's action by reference to a given and specific human nature; in other words, there is no determinism — man is free, man is freedom."
- P2. Nor, if God does not exist, are there a priori values or commands "inscribed in an intelligible heaven" that could justify or excuse a choice: "It is nowhere written that 'the good' exists, that one must be honest or must not lie."
- ∴ C. "We are left alone, without excuse… man is condemned to be free" — he did not create himself, yet "from the moment that he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does," passion included: "man is responsible for his passion."

**Choosing for all men** (same source) — the move that is meant to answer the charge of mere subjectivism: "when we say that man chooses himself, we do mean that every one of us must choose himself; but by that we also mean that in choosing for himself he chooses for all men," since every act fashions "an image of man such as he believes he ought to be." Anguish is the felt form of this: Kierkegaard's "anguish of Abraham," who must decide, with no possible proof, that the voice is an angel's and that he is Abraham.

### Self-deception (*mauvaise foi*): the flight from freedom (Sartre)
The same volume's *Being and Nothingness* selection (in this corpus's translation: "self-deception," where most editions print "bad faith" — see the Sources note) asks how lying to oneself is even possible, and answers by locating its patterns.

**Canonical argument** (source: Sartre, "Self-Deception," book 018, ch. NINE §2 — a formalization of the chapter's opening puzzle):
- P1. An ordinary lie requires a deceiver who possesses the truth and a deceived who does not; in self-deception "the one to whom the lie is told and the one who lies are one and the same person," so "I must know in my capacity as deceiver the truth which is hidden from me in my capacity as the one deceived" — and consciousness is wholly translucent, so a deliberately cynical lie to oneself "fails completely… the lie falls back and collapses under my regard."
- P2. The Freudian rescue — splitting the psyche into id and ego behind a censor — restores the duality only by making part of me a thing to be interpreted from outside, which relocates rather than solves the problem.
- ∴ C. Self-deception must be an "evanescent," "metastable" project of consciousness itself: a pattern in which a person flees what they are (free, self-making) by playing at being a fixed thing — and it "can even be the normal aspect of life for a very great number of people."

The patterns (§2, Part II): a woman on a first date who knows the man's intentions perfectly well but "does not want to realize the urgency," attending only to what is respectful in his conduct; and the café waiter whose movements are "a little too precise, a little too rapid," who is *playing* at being a waiter — "I can be he only in the neutralized mode, as the actor is Hamlet… What I attempt to realize is a being-in-itself of the café waiter," as if his duties were not his free choice to confer value upon.

### The editor's caution: the slogan obstructs the movement (Kaufmann)
The anthology's editor refuses to let this entry's title stand for the whole anthology. "Existentialism is not a philosophy but a label for several widely different revolts against traditional philosophy"; Jaspers, Heidegger, and Sartre "are not in agreement on essentials," and the alleged precursors include a dedicated Christian (Kierkegaard) and "an impassioned anti-Christian" (Nietzsche). On the slogan itself: "it has become a commonplace that existentialism is the doctrine that existence precedes essence, and this phrase obstructs the understanding of this complex movement as well as of Sartre's philosophy" — Heidegger's *Letter on Humanism*, prompted by Sartre's lecture, is quoted making the reversal point: Plato's metaphysics already says *essentia* precedes *existentia*, and "Sartre reverses this sentence" rather than leaving metaphysics behind.

**Objections Sartre stages and answers in the lecture** — (i) *Quietism of despair* (the Communist reproach): the doctrine "declares that there is no reality except in action" and that "man is nothing else but what he purposes"; despair means only acting without hope beyond one's will, as Descartes' "conquer yourself rather than the world" meant. (ii) *Cowardice as temperament*: "there is no such thing as a cowardly temperament… he has made himself into a coward by his actions"; likewise "there is no genius other than that which is expressed in works of art" — the genius of Proust is the totality of Proust's works. (iii) *Which adviser, which sign?*: the pupil torn between staying with his mother and joining the Free French can get no a priori answer from Christian doctrine or Kant's ethic (each horn treats someone as a means); even choosing an adviser "is nevertheless to commit oneself by that choice." "You are free, therefore choose — that is to say, invent."

## Thought experiments
- **The paper-knife** (Sartre, lecture). An artifact whose essence precedes its existence, because a maker conceived it first. Sets up the contrast the whole position turns on: everything made is like this; the question is whether man is made.
- **The pupil's choice** (Sartre, lecture). A student must choose between his widowed mother, who lives only for him, and a possibly futile journey to join the Free French. Every offered guide fails in turn — doctrine, Kant, feeling (a feeling's strength is known only by the act that ratifies it, so consulting it is circular), and advice (choosing the adviser chooses the advice). Meant to show abandonment concretely: no sign is vouchsafed, and interpreting any sign is itself the chooser's responsibility.
- **The café waiter** (Sartre, *Being and Nothingness* ch., §2). A waiter performing waiterness a fraction too eagerly, balancing his tray "with the recklessness of a tight-rope-walker." Meant to make self-deception visible as *play*: treating a freely sustained role as a thing's nature. Pressures anyone who says "that's just my job / my character / how I am."

## Where the debate stands
Inside this corpus, the position is stated at full strength and answered only against the objections Sartre himself selects (quietism, subjectivism, the denial of values). The deepest pressure in the volume comes from its own editor and from Heidegger, and it is taxonomic rather than refuting: the slogan may be a reversal inside the metaphysics it claims to escape, and the movement it names may not exist as one doctrine at all. Whether a fixed human nature can be reinstated on other grounds (biology, psychoanalysis) is a fight the corpus stages only in Sartre's dismissal of the Freudian censor; the texts here leave it open.
