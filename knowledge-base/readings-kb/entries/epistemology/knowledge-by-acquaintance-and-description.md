# Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: acquaintance vs description; knowledge of things vs knowledge of truths*

## The question
Almost everything you would say you know concerns things you have never encountered: Julius Caesar, the centre of the earth, other people's thoughts, the physical object behind your present sensations. Yet some things you know in a radically more direct way — the colour you are seeing now, a pain, a remembered sound, your own current desire. Russell's distinction names the two modes. You are *acquainted* with what you are directly aware of, without inference. You know something *by description* when you know that there is exactly one thing answering to a description — "the physical object which causes such-and-such sense-data," "the first Chancellor of the German Empire" — without being acquainted with the thing itself. The question the distinction is built to answer: how can knowledge extend so far beyond the narrow circle of acquaintance without becoming mysterious — and what is that circle's exact boundary?

## The 90-second version
Start by separating *knowledge that* (propositional: knowing that the train leaves at six) from *knowledge of* (knowing a person, a place, a face) and *knowledge how* (an ability, like riding a bike). The masked-man case shows why the separation matters: a hooded man approaches and you do not know who he is; he turns out to be your father, whom you know well — so in one sense you know him and in another you do not, and the puzzle dissolves once the senses of "know" are kept apart. Russell's version of the same cut, for knowledge of things, is acquaintance versus description: acquaintance is direct awareness with no intermediary of inference — the sense-data of your table, things remembered, your own thoughts and feelings noticed in introspection, and universals like whiteness and diversity. Everything else that we "know of" — physical objects, other minds, historical persons — is reached by description: we know truths about a thing picked out as *the* so-and-so, and the thing itself never comes before the mind. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture [The Three Kinds of Knowledge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=59RMe5ccT7o) together with Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. V; the lecture is also used, for its framing distinctions only, in the Gettier entry.)

## Positions

### Acquaintance: the foundation (Russell)
"We shall say that we have acquaintance with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths." Acquaintance is simpler than any knowledge of truths and logically independent of it: you know the colour itself "perfectly and completely when you see it," and statements about it add knowledge *about* the colour without improving knowledge *of* it. Russell's inventory (ch. V) has five heads: (1) **sense-data**, in sensation; (2) **things remembered** — acquaintance by memory is "the source of all our knowledge concerning the past," since without it there would be nothing past to infer; (3) **the contents of our own minds** in introspection or self-consciousness — "my seeing the sun," "my desiring food" — which is also our only route to the very idea of other minds; (4) **probably the Self** (see the caveat below); and (5) **universals** — whiteness, diversity, brotherhood — awareness of which Russell calls conceiving. Conspicuously *absent* from the list: physical objects as opposed to sense-data, and other people's minds. Both are known, if at all, by description.

### Knowledge by description, and the fundamental principle (Russell)
An object is known by description when we know that it is *the* so-and-so — that there is one object, and no more, having a certain property — without acquaintance with it. Proper names are the everyday disguise of descriptions: the thought of someone who never met Bismarck, saying "Bismarck was an astute diplomatist," contains not Bismarck but a description ("the first Chancellor of the German Empire") assembled from particulars and universals with which the speaker *is* acquainted — ultimately involving, somewhere, a reference to testimony heard or read, to a here or a now, to something experienced. This yields the chapter's governing claim:

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. V — the chapter's fundamental principle, stated as an argument in the form Russell gives it):
- P1. To make a judgement or entertain a supposition is to know what one is judging or supposing about; the words used must have a meaning, and "the meaning we attach to our words must be something with which we are acquainted."
- P2. A description known to be applicable to a particular must, if our knowledge about the thing described is not to be merely what follows logically from the description, involve some reference to a particular with which we are acquainted (as all mention of past, present, here, there, or of what others have told us does).
- ∴ C. "Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted" — and knowledge by description is everywhere reducible, at its base, to acquaintance.

**What the distinction buys** — "the chief importance of knowledge by description is that it enables us to pass beyond the limits of our private experience": in spite of the fact that we can only know truths composed of terms we have experienced in acquaintance, we can have knowledge by description of things we have never experienced at all. Russell's closing verdict is that until this is understood, "much of our knowledge must remain mysterious and therefore doubtful."

**Objections and open points, as Russell himself leaves them** — two are flagged in the chapter itself. First, the fundamental principle is asserted before it is defended: "We shall not at this stage attempt to answer all the objections which may be urged against the fundamental principle," beyond the claim that the objections must be answerable because significant speech requires understood constituents. Second, acquaintance with the Self is left genuinely undecided. When we try to look into ourselves "we always seem to come upon some particular thought or feeling, and not upon the 'I' which has the thought or feeling"; yet the truth "I am acquainted with this sense-datum" seems to require acquaintance with something called 'I'. Russell's verdict: acquaintance with our Selves "seems probably to occur," but "it is not wise to assert that it undoubtedly does occur," and no more permanent a self than the momentary subject of the experience is warranted. The pilot's skepticism entry records the same reserve applied to the cogito.

### The three kinds, and why the boundaries matter (the lecture's cut)
The Carneades lecture divides the territory by grammar rather than by Russell's inventory: knowledge *that* (propositional), knowledge *of* (acquaintance-like familiarity — knowing your father), and knowledge *how* (abilities). The cuts cross: Russell's "knowledge of things" is itself split into acquaintance and description, and his knowledge of truths is the lecture's knowledge that. The masked-man case (below) is the lecture's device for showing that confusing the kinds manufactures paradoxes out of equivocation — the same service Russell's distinction performs when someone asks how we can "know" a table we never directly encounter, or a Caesar we never met.

## Thought experiments
- **Bismarck, in four removes** (Russell, ch. V). Bismarck judging about himself (his own name used directly, if self-acquaintance is granted); Bismarck to a friend, who knows his body and mind only as the body and mind connected with certain sense-data; Bismarck to us, through history, as "the first Chancellor of the German Empire"; and beyond him, *the man with the iron mask*, of whom we know many truths without knowing who he was; and finally *the most long-lived of men*, about whom we know nothing beyond what the description logically gives. The series is meant to show descriptive knowledge shading off from near-acquaintance to pure logic — and that communication survives our varying descriptions because we know there is one true proposition about the actual object, whatever description each of us uses.
- **The masked man** ([The Three Kinds of Knowledge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=59RMe5ccT7o)). A hooded man approaches; you do not know who he is; it is your father. You both know and do not know him. Meant to show the paradox is an equivocation between knowledge of and knowledge that. It pressures any analysis — Russell's included — that lets one sense of "know" do the work of the others.
- **Julius Caesar** (Russell, ch. V). When we make a statement about Caesar, Caesar himself is not before our minds, since we are not acquainted with him: we have in mind a description — "the man who was assassinated on the Ides of March," or merely "the man whose name was Julius Caesar," where the name is a noise or shape with which we *are* acquainted. Meant to show that even proper names, the words that seem to reach their objects directly, route through acquaintance with something else. Pressures the idea that language or thought can hook onto unexperienced objects without a descriptive intermediary.

## Where the debate stands
Within this corpus the distinction is Russell's construction, and the debate is mostly Russell arguing with his own reservations. The inventory's core — sense-data, memory, introspection, universals — is asserted with confidence; the Self is expressly left probable-not-certain; and the fundamental principle, the load-bearing claim that all understood propositions resolve into acquainted constituents, is announced with its objections postponed rather than answered. The lecture tradition represented in the corpus accepts the *need* for the cut (its three kinds, its masked man) without adjudicating Russell's version of it. No text in books 006 or 007 offers a rival taxonomy of knowledge of things — Reid's original/acquired perception distinction (see the perception entry) is the nearest neighbour, and it classifies perceptions, not modes of knowing an object. What is settled here: the phenomenon — that we judge truly about much we never encounter, and that some awareness is inference-free. What is not: whether Russell's principle about constituents is the right explanation of the first fact, and whether the second extends to a Self.
