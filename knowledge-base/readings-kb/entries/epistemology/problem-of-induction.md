# The Problem of Induction
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: Hume's problem; the justification of induction; (Goodman's version) the new riddle of induction*

## The question
Nearly everything we expect — that the sun will rise tomorrow, that bread will nourish rather than poison, that dropped stones fall — rests on induction: inferring from observed cases to unobserved ones, from "every A so far has been B" to "the next A will be B" or "all A's are B." The inference is not deductively valid: no contradiction follows from supposing the pattern breaks tomorrow. And the obvious bridging principle — nature is uniform, the future will resemble the past — seems to be supportable only by pointing to its past success, which is the very kind of inference in question. Can induction be justified at all, and if not, what are we doing when we rely on it?

## The 90-second version
Draw black balls from an urn, one after another — black, black, black. The *strong* conclusion, that the next ball must be black, does not follow: to get from past to future you need a bridge premise, "the future will resemble the past," and the only support for that premise is that the future has resembled the past *so far* — a claim about what has already happened, offered in support of a claim about what will happen, by assuming the bridge itself. That is begging the question. The sunrise is the same argument at larger scale. So the lecture counsels suspending judgment about any claim that science delivers certainty — and even the *weak* conclusion, that the next ball will *probably* be black, gets attacked in the follow-up: Nelson Goodman's new riddle asks which predicates induction is allowed to project. All observed emeralds are green; but define "grue" — green if examined before some future time *t*, blue thereafter — and every observation so far confirms "all emeralds are grue" just as well, with the opposite prediction after *t*. Goodman's own answer, that "green" wins only by *entrenchment* (our habit of projecting it), makes the choice look conventional rather than justified. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [The Problem of Induction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sd8cxXfPJU4), [The New Riddle of Induction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kGE2Ig2dvaE).)

## Positions

### Hume's sceptical argument: no reasoning supports the step
Hume's *Enquiry* §IV first narrows all factual reasoning to cause and effect, and causal knowledge to experience: "the knowledge of this relation is not, in any instance, attained by reasonings *a priori*; but arises entirely from experience, when we find that any particular objects are constantly conjoined with each other." Adam, with perfect rational faculties, "could not have inferred from the fluidity and transparency of water that it would suffocate him." An effect "is a distinct event from its cause" and can never be discovered by scrutinizing the cause. Then Part II presses the remaining question: what founds our conclusions *from* experience?

**Canonical argument** (source: Hume, *Enquiry*, §IV, ¶¶28–32 — the negative argument):
- P1. All our experimental conclusions "proceed upon the supposition that the future will be conformable to the past."
- P2. This supposition cannot be established demonstratively: its contrary implies no contradiction — "May I not clearly and distinctly conceive that a body, falling from the clouds, and which, in all other respects, resembles snow, has yet the taste of salt or feeling of fire?"
- P3. It cannot be established by probable (experimental) argument either: all such arguments are themselves founded on the relation of cause and effect as learned from experience, and so "must be evidently going in a circle, and taking that for granted, which is the very point in question."
- ∴ C. Even after we have experience of constant conjunction, "our conclusions from that experience are *not* founded on reasoning, or any process of the understanding."

Two supporting tests in the text. The missing medium: "These two propositions are far from being the same, *I have found that such an object has always been attended with such an effect*, and *I foresee, that other objects, which are, in appearance, similar, will be attended with similar effects*" — whoever claims a chain of reasoning joins them is asked to produce it. And the infant test: if the inference were made by reason, it "would be as perfect at first, and upon one instance, as after ever so long a course of experience" — yet nothing so like as eggs still requires long experience before we trust the taste; children and even animals learn from experience, so the operative process cannot be an argument too abstruse for them (¶¶31, 33).

### Hume's sceptical solution: custom or habit
Hume's answer to his own argument is not a justification but a diagnosis of what actually moves us.

**Canonical position** (source: Hume, *Enquiry*, §V, ¶¶34–36, 44–45):
- P1. After the constant conjunction of two objects, "we are determined by custom alone to expect the one from the appearance of the other." Custom — repetition producing a propensity to renew the inference "without being impelled by any reasoning or process of the understanding" — is "the ultimate principle, which we can assign, of all our conclusions from experience."
- P2. This principle operates as instinct: nature has "implanted in us an instinct, which carries forward the thought in a correspondent course to that which she has established among external objects," and it "will preserve its influence as long as human nature remains the same" regardless of our sceptical conclusions.
- ∴ C. Inductive inference is natural, unavoidable, and indispensable — but its foundation is a propensity of human nature, not a justification by reason. Hume calls this the *sceptical solution* of the doubts of §IV, in contrast to a refutation he does not offer.

### Russell: the inductive principle, accepted a priori
Russell takes up exactly Hume's question — "Do any number of cases of a law being fulfilled in the past afford evidence that it will be fulfilled in the future?" — and states the principle on which all such inference tacitly relies. (The chicken is his: the man who fed the chicken every day of its life "at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken.")

**The principle of induction** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. VI), for particular cases and, repeated, for general laws:
- (a) When a thing of sort A has been found associated with a thing of sort B and never dissociated, "the greater the number of cases in which A and B have been associated, the greater is the probability that they will be associated in a fresh case."
- (b) "Under the same circumstances, a sufficient number of cases of association will make the probability of a fresh association nearly a certainty, and will make it approach certainty without limit."

**Russell's argument for accepting it** (sources: *Problems*, chs. VI–VII):
- P1. The principle "is equally incapable of being proved by an appeal to experience": experience could confirm it only for cases already examined, while its whole use concerns the unexamined — "we can never use experience to prove the inductive principle without begging the question."
- P2. Nor can experience disprove it: probability is always relative to data, and failures (the black swan against "all swans are white") show only that other data can alter a probability, not that the original estimate was wrong.
- P3. Some principles are known otherwise than by experiential proof: the self-evident logical principles ("whatever follows from a true proposition is true"; the traditional laws of thought) cannot be proved by experience because all proof presupposes them, yet are known — experience *elicits* such knowledge without *proving* it; this knowledge is a priori.
- P4. The inductive principle belongs with these principles of inference, except that where the logical ones yield certainty it yields only probability; in ch. VII Russell calls it "perhaps the most important example" of the principles that let us infer "a greater or less probability that something is true."
- ∴ C. "We must either accept the inductive principle on the ground of its intrinsic evidence, or forgo all justification of our expectations about the future."

**Objections** — the standing objection is Hume's circle itself (above), which Russell does not dispute: his move is to relocate the principle outside experience rather than to answer the circularity charge on its own ground. Whether "intrinsic evidence" names a genuine kind of justification, or merely labels the place where justification runs out, is a question Russell's text leaves the reader to judge; in his ch. VII he is careful that all a priori knowledge concerning *existence* is only hypothetical — connexions between universals, not guarantees about what will happen.

### Goodman's new riddle: which predicates may we project?
Goodman grants, for argument, what Hume and Russell debate, and finds a second problem underneath: induction does not work for generalizations as such, but only for *law-like* ones, and we have no non-circular way to say which are law-like. ("The last three men to board this bus wore glasses" is not confirmed by its instances the way "all icicles are cold" is.)

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades lecture, "The New Riddle of Induction," presenting Goodman, "The New Riddle of Induction," 1954 — formalized; Goodman's paper is not in this corpus):
- P1. A generalization is confirmed by its instances, in a way that supports prediction, only if its predicates are law-like (projectible); coincidental generalizations are not.
- P2. Define *grue*: an object is grue if it is examined before time *t* and green, or not examined before *t* and blue. Every emerald examined so far is grue as well as green, so the same evidence confirms "all emeralds are grue" — which predicts that emeralds first examined after *t* will be blue.
- P3. The only difference Goodman finds between "green" and "grue" is *entrenchment*: we are accustomed to projecting "green." In a language that had entrenched "grue" and "bleen" instead, "green" would be definable only disjunctively ("grue before *t*, bleen after") and would look like the artificial predicate.
- ∴ C. We cannot determine, from the evidence and the logic of confirmation alone, which predicates form law-like generalizations — and so cannot tell, by that route, when induction is working and when it is not.

**Objections** — the corpus's lecture series stages two. The *disjunctive objection* (grue is disjunctive, green is not) is answered, per the lecture's report of Goodman, by the symmetry of P3: relative to a grue/bleen language, green is the disjunctive predicate. *Swinburne's objection* (source: Carneades, [Swinburne's Objection](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ye9qprxFP28)) draws a different line: *qualitative* predicates (like green) can be applied without knowing the time or place, while *locational* predicates (like grue) cannot — around *t*, the grue-speakers "would have to look at their watches in order to talk about colors." So, when two predicates fit equally, prefer the qualitative: grue is superfluous. The lecture closes by announcing a further video giving Goodman's possible response — so within the corpus's own staging, the exchange is left open.

## Thought experiments
- **The urn** ([The Problem of Induction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sd8cxXfPJU4)). Black ball after black ball from an unseen urn; the case is built to separate the strong conclusion (the next *will* be black) from the weak one (it *probably* will), and to show the strong one needs the question-begging bridge.
- **The sunrise** (the lecture; Russell, ch. VI). "We are all convinced that the sun will rise to-morrow" — Russell's point is that appealing to the laws of motion only relocates the doubt, since our reason for trusting those laws tomorrow is again that they have held so far.
- **The chicken** (Russell, ch. VI). Fed daily by the same hand throughout its life; the hand wrings its neck. Crude expectations of uniformity are "liable to be misleading" even after a lifetime of confirmations — the case pressures naive confidence without touching the principle's probabilistic core.
- **Bread** (Hume, §IV ¶29; Russell, ch. VI). Bread's sensible qualities tell us nothing of its "secret powers" of nourishment; that past bread nourished does not, without the contested step, show the next loaf will not poison.
- **Grue emeralds** (Goodman, via the lecture). The time-indexed predicate; see the argument above. What it is meant to show: confirmation is predicate-relative, and evidence alone does not choose between rival projections.

## Where the debate stands
Within these sources, the negative core is common ground: no justification of induction from experience alone can avoid circularity — Hume proves it, Russell concedes it, and the lecture's strong-claim verdict ("suspend judgment") restates it. The disagreement is over the remainder. Hume stops at psychology: custom does the work, and no foundation in reason is to be had. Russell insists a foundation exists but is a priori and merely probabilistic — acceptance "on the ground of its intrinsic evidence." Goodman's riddle (as the lectures present it) undercuts a lazy version of the probabilistic remainder by asking which generalizations probability is even allowed to attach to, and in this corpus its exchange with Swinburne is unfinished — a response on Goodman's behalf is promised, not delivered. No source here claims induction yields certainty; the most anyone claims is probability approaching it.
