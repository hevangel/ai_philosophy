# Memory and Knowledge of the Past
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the epistemology of memory; memory as a source of knowledge*

## The question
Everything you know about the past — that you ate breakfast, that there was a yesterday at all — you know, in the end, by memory. But memory is a present state: an image, a feeling of familiarity, a judgement made now. How can a present state amount to knowledge of something that no longer exists? If memory merely stores and revives old perceptions, what marks a revival as a *memory* rather than an imagining — and what secures it against the plain fact that memory is, as Russell concedes, "notoriously fallacious"? The problem is foundational in an unusually literal way: take memory away and there is no past to reason about, no testimony to check (witnesses must remember), no induction (its premises are remembered observations), and no persisting self to do the reasoning.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus is devoted to memory, so this version is distilled from the textbook presentation — Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XI — and is marked as such.* Suppose an insistent questioner drives your beliefs back, reason by reason, to what needs no further reason. For the past, the regress ends at judgements of memory: you simply remember that the house next door was struck by lightning half a minute ago, and demanding a further ground misses what memory is. But memory will not stay uniformly solid. Run back over your day and certainty thins by degrees: you are quite certain of breakfast, less certain of the conversation at it, doubtful of some of it, blank on the rest — and there exist firm, confident memories that are wholly false. Russell's proposal is that the gradation is itself the guide: memory has *degrees of self-evidence*, and these correspond to its degrees of trustworthiness, reaching a limit of perfect self-evidence in the recent and vivid. Whether that correspondence can be trusted is the problem the three positions below divide over.

## Positions

### Locke: memory is a power of revival, not a storehouse
Locke's *retention* (Essay, Book II, ch. x) is the mind's keeping of the simple ideas it has received, in two modes: contemplation, keeping an idea actually in view, and memory. Memory is usually pictured as a repository — "the storehouse of our ideas" — but Locke immediately corrects the picture. Our ideas "are nothing but actual perceptions in the mind, which cease to be anything when there is no perception of them"; so laying ideas up in memory "signifies no more but this — that the mind has a power in many cases to revive perceptions which it has once had, **with this additional perception annexed to them, that it has had them before**."

**Canonical argument** (source: Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, ch. x, §§1–6 — a formalization):
- P1. Ideas exist only while perceived; therefore nothing is literally warehoused in the mind between perceptions.
- P2. What we call memory is the power to revive a past perception, with an annexed perception of pastness — of *having had it before*. It is that annex, not the revived content, that makes the state a memory rather than a fresh imagining.
- P3. Revival is imperfect and perishable: attention, repetition, pleasure and pain fix ideas; without renewal they fade "like pictures drawn in fading colours," some minds retaining characters "like marble," others "little better than sand"; a fever can in days "calcine all those images to dust and confusion," and children blinded young lose their very ideas of colours.
- ∴ C. Knowledge of the past, so far as it rests on retention, is the exercise of a fallible reproductive power whose credential is a present feeling of pastness — Locke gives no further guarantee, and the decay he catalogues is the price of the storehouse metaphor's correction.

**Objections** — Russell's (next position): if what is before the mind is only a present image plus a feeling, we could never *compare* the image with the remembered object and know how far it is accurate — yet we manifestly do, "within somewhat wide limits." A revived image whose pastness is a present annex seems to leave the past itself out of the transaction.

### Russell: acquaintance with the past, and self-evidence in degrees
Russell classifies memory twice, and the two classifications do different work. In ch. V, memory is a form of *acquaintance*: "we are still immediately aware of what we remember, in spite of the fact that it appears as past"; and this "immediate knowledge by memory is the source of all our knowledge concerning the past: without it, there could be no knowledge of the past by inference, since we should never know that there was anything past to be inferred." In ch. XI he analyses the corresponding *judgements* of memory as intuitive — not inferred from anything more evident — and faces the fallibility Locke catalogued.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XI, "On Intuitive Knowledge" — a formalization):
- P1. The essence of memory is not an image. The image is present; what is remembered is known to be past. And we can compare our image with the object remembered and judge its accuracy — impossible unless the object, as opposed to the image, is "in some way before the mind."
- P2. Hence there must be intuitive judgements of memory: the past object itself immediately before the mind, recognized as past. "But for the fact of memory in this sense, we should not know that there ever was a past at all, nor should we be able to understand the word 'past'."
- P3. Fallibility is real but graded: memory "is trustworthy in proportion to the vividness of the experience and to its nearness in time," and degrees of self-evidence correspond to degrees of trustworthiness, up to the limit case (lightning half a minute ago) where doubt would be preposterous.
- ∴ C. Memory is a basic, non-inferential source of knowledge of the past whose deliverances are self-evident *in degrees*; its fallibility qualifies its certainty case by case rather than disqualifying the faculty.

**Russell's diagnosis of the counterexamples** — the apparently fatal cases, of very firm belief in a wholly false memory, are argued to be not memory at all in the strict sense. George IV "is said to have at last believed that he was at the battle of Waterloo, because he had so often said that he was": what he immediately remembered was his repeated assertion; the further belief was produced by association with what was remembered, and association is not memory. "It would seem that cases of fallacious memory can probably all be dealt with in this way" — note the *probably*: Russell advances the diagnosis as a programme, not a proof.

**Objections** — the difficulty Russell himself states and does not claim to dissolve: fallacious memory "throws doubt on the trustworthiness of intuitive judgements in general." His own late-chapter suggestion (end of ch. XI) is that "self-evidence" combines two notions — one, corresponding to the highest degree, an infallible guarantee of truth; the other a mere presumption — and that he cannot yet develop the distinction. The degrees-reply also leans on a correlation (vividness/recency with accuracy) that is itself known, if at all, through memory.

### Reid: memory is an original principle of belief — asking for its reason is a category error
Reid's *Inquiry* (ch. II, §3, "Sensation and remembrance, natural principles of belief") draws the parallel Russell's account half-suggests. "There is a smell," is the immediate testimony of sense; "There was a smell," is the immediate testimony of memory — and neither admits a further reason. "If you ask me why I believe that the smell exists, I can give no other reason… than that I smell it. If you ask why I believe that it existed yesterday, I can give no other reason but that I remember it."

**Canonical argument** (source: Reid, *Inquiry into the Human Mind*, ch. II, §3 — a formalization; the corpus copy is a noisy OCR scan, and the passage used is among its legible ones):
- P1. Sensation compels my belief in the present existence of its object; memory compels my belief in its past existence; the compulsion in both cases is immediate, not the conclusion of an argument.
- P2. Sensation and memory are "simple, original, and perfectly distinct operations of the mind," and both are original *principles of belief* — starting points of the constitution, not inferences from more basic data (imagination, by contrast, compels no belief at all).
- ∴ C. Memory's authority is constitutional and underived. To demand a justification of memory from outside memory is to demand of a first principle what first principles, by their nature, cannot have — the same demand Reid shows being made, with sceptical results, of sensation itself.

**Objections** — from within this corpus, the standing difficulty is fallibility again: a principle that "compels belief" compels it also in cases like George IV's. Reid's framework can distinguish genuine remembrance from associated belief only by the felt character of the operation, which is what the false-memory cases put in doubt; the *Inquiry*'s text as we have it does not address that case directly, and this entry does not pretend otherwise.

## Thought experiments
- **George IV at Waterloo** (Russell, ch. XI). The king, having so often said he was at the battle, came at last to believe he had been there. Meant to test theories of memory against confident falsehood. Russell's use is diagnostic: the case must be reclassified (remembered assertion + associated belief) if memory proper is to remain a knowledge-source — a reclassification whose availability in *every* case is asserted only as probable. Pressures acquaintance and constitutional accounts hardest; Locke's revival-with-annex model accommodates the case more cheaply, since the annexed pastness can attach to the wrong content.
- **Half a minute ago, and breakfast** (Russell, ch. XI). Lightning struck the house next door half a minute ago: doubt is preposterous. Breakfast this morning: certain. The conversation at breakfast: partly recallable, partly doubtful, partly gone. Meant to exhibit self-evidence as a continuous magnitude tied to vividness and recency. Pressures any theory on which memory is simply reliable or simply not — and supplies the data the degrees-doctrine is built to fit.
- **The blinded child's colours** (Locke, Book II, ch. x, §5). Children who lose their sight young find, years later, that their ideas of colours have worn out entirely, "no more notion nor memory of colours left in their minds, than in those of people born blind." Meant to show ideas unrevised by the senses decay to nothing. Pressures the storehouse picture from inside (warehouses do not selectively evaporate) and any acquaintance account on which remembered data persist as objects of awareness: on Locke's showing, the past they give access to is itself shrinking.
- **"There is a smell / There was a smell"** (Reid, *Inquiry*, ch. II, §3). The minimal pair, offered as a complete epistemology of memory in two sentences: the second judgement has exactly the immediacy, and exactly the absence of supporting argument, as the first. Meant to shift the burden: memory needs no more vindication than perception does. Pressures the assumption — shared by Locke's revival model and by Russell's worry — that memory is perception's poorer relation.

## Where the debate stands
The three sources agree on the load-bearing negative point: knowledge of the past is not inferred from present data by an argument that could be supplied on demand — each, in his own vocabulary (revival with annexed pastness; acquaintance with the past object; an original principle of belief), makes memory a basic source. They disagree about its anatomy and its credential. Locke's model explains fallibility and decay most naturally but leaves the pastness-annex doing unexplained work, and Russell's comparison objection — we can check an image against the remembered object — is directed at exactly that gap. Russell's model explains directness but must manage fallibility with the degrees doctrine and the not-really-memory diagnosis, both of which he presents with visible hedges ("probably," "it would seem," "only a suggestion"). Reid declines the management problem: compulsion by constitution is the end of justification, here as for perception. Nothing in the corpus adjudicates between them, and the corpus's contemporary epistemology (book 007, Parts II) contains no memory reading at all — the debate, as this corpus can state it, is a three-way classical stand-off over the faculty on which all the other entries' historical premises depend.
