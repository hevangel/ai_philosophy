# Perception and Sense-Data
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the problem of perception; appearance and reality*

## The question
When you look at a table, what are you actually aware of? The colour you see changes with the light, the point of view, and the state of your eyes; the shape you see changes as you move; a microscope replaces the smooth surface with hills and valleys. If what you immediately perceive is never quite the object as it is in itself, then perception does not hand you the world directly — it hands you appearances, which depend as much on you as on the object. What, then, do we perceive: the objects themselves, private data caused by them, or nothing but our own ideas? And which of the qualities we perceive — colour, warmth, shape, size — belong to the objects at all?

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus is devoted to this problem, so this version is distilled from the textbook presentation — Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. I — and is marked as such.* Concentrate on a table. To the eye it is oblong, brown, and shiny; to the touch, smooth, cool, and hard. But try to be precise and the troubles begin. No one colour is pre-eminently the table's colour: parts reflecting the light look brighter, the colour changes under artificial light, for a colour-blind observer, and through blue spectacles, and in the dark there is no colour at all, though touch and hearing find the table unchanged. The shape fares no better: a rectangular table looks, from almost every viewpoint, as if it had two acute and two obtuse angles, and its parallel sides look as if they converged. What we see is constantly changing as we move; the "real" shape is not seen but inferred. Russell's conclusion is terminological and substantive at once: call the things immediately known in sensation — colours, sounds, hardnesses, roughnesses — *sense-data*, and the experience of being aware of them *sensation*. The real table, if there is one, is not the sense-data and is not immediately known at all; it must be inferred from them. Everything else in this entry is a fight over what that inference delivers, and whether it is needed.

## Positions

### Locke: primary qualities resemble, secondary qualities are powers
Bodies produce ideas in us, but the ideas divide. *Primary* qualities — solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, number — are inseparable from body in every state and are found by sense in every perceptible particle of matter. *Secondary* qualities — colours, sounds, tastes, smells — are nothing in the objects themselves but powers, arising from the bulk, figure, texture, and motion of their insensible parts, to produce sensations in us.

**Canonical argument** (source: Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, ch. viii, §§9–21 — a formalization):
- P1. Primary qualities survive every division and alteration: divide a grain of wheat as far as you like, each part still has solidity, extension, figure, and mobility.
- P2. Secondary-quality ideas fail the parallel tests. Porphyry's red and white vanish in the dark and return with the light, though no real alteration is made in the stone; pounding an almond changes its colour and taste by changing only its texture; the same water feels cold to one hand and warm to the other, which it could not do if heat and cold were both really in it — and nobody thinks the pain or sickness manna causes is *in* the manna, though it is produced by the same kind of operation as its sweetness and whiteness.
- ∴ C. "The ideas of primary qualities of bodies are resemblances of them, and their patterns do really exist in the bodies themselves; but the ideas produced in us by these secondary qualities have no resemblance of them at all" (§15).

**Objections** — Berkeley (below): the two classes cannot be prised apart, since extension and figure cannot be conceived without some secondary quality, and the primary qualities are just as relative to the perceiver as the secondary ones.

### Berkeley: there is no material substratum behind the ideas
Berkeley accepts Locke's starting point — what we perceive are ideas — and denies the step beyond them. In the *Treatise* (§§3–4) he argues that the very meaning of *exist*, applied to sensible things, is to be perceived: "Their *esse* is *percipi*, nor is it possible they should have any existence out of the minds or thinking things which perceive them." The table exists when nobody is in the study because some other spirit perceives it, or because it would be perceived by anyone suitably placed — permanence is supplied by God's perception, not by an unthinking substance.

**Canonical argument** (source: Berkeley, *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, Part I, §§3–4, 9–11 — a formalization):
- P1. The objects of perception are the things we perceive by sense; and what we perceive besides our own ideas or sensations, Berkeley challenges us to name.
- P2. It is repugnant that any idea, or combination of ideas, should exist unperceived, since an idea can be like nothing but another idea, and its whole being is to be before a mind.
- P3. The escape route — Locke's distinction — fails: figure, motion, and the rest of the primary qualities are inseparably united with the secondary qualities and cannot even in thought be abstracted from them (§10); and great/small, swift/slow are relative to the perceiver's frame and organs, so mind-free extension would be neither great nor small — nothing at all (§11).
- ∴ C. The notion of matter — an inert, senseless substance in which extension, figure, and motion actually subsist unperceived — involves a contradiction. Sensible things are ideas, and ideas exist only in minds.

**Objections** — Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. IV: the argument trades on confusing the *act* of apprehension, which is mental, with the *object* apprehended, which need not be. "When a man says that some business he had to arrange went clean out of his mind, he does not mean to imply that the business itself was ever in his mind": being *before* the mind is not being *in* the mind in the sense that would make the tree mental. Russell adds that Berkeley's arguments of detail — that sense-data depend on us — are "almost certainly valid," but they prove dependence on the perceiver's organs and standpoint, not mentality.

### Russell: sense-data are signs; of the physical object we know structure, not intrinsic nature
Granting (ch. II, and the skepticism entry) that physical objects exist independently, Russell asks what perception tells us about them — and answers: their relations, not their qualities. Physical space is public and one; our spaces of sight and touch are private and many. The two must correspond in arrangement — the house that looks nearer is reached sooner, is nearer on the map, and looks nearer to everyone — but correspondence of arrangement is all we get.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. III, "The Nature of Matter" — a formalization):
- P1. Sense-data are in private spaces (the space of sight, the space of touch), which differ from person to person and sense to sense; the physical object is in one public, physical space — as the sun we see is the sun of eight minutes ago shows, sense-data and object are not even straightforwardly simultaneous.
- P2. If sense-data are to be evidence for physical objects, the relative positions of objects in physical space must more or less correspond to the relative positions of sense-data in private spaces; order (before/after, left/right in a marching regiment) is preserved across viewpoints while shape is not.
- P3. We can know only what the correspondence requires: that one physical distance is greater than another, or collinear with it — as a man born blind can know everything about the space of sight that other people can tell him, except what sight is like.
- ∴ C. "Although the relations of physical objects have all sorts of knowable properties, derived from their correspondence with the relations of sense-data, the physical objects themselves remain unknown in their intrinsic nature, so far at least as can be discovered by means of the senses."

**A resemblance Russell refuses** — the tempting compromise, that physical objects really have colours and we sometimes see them as they are, is "not capable of being definitely refuted, but it can be shown to be groundless": the colour seen depends only on the light-waves reaching the eye, modified by the intervening medium and the manner of reflection, so the same colour would be seen whether the object had any colour or not. What holds for colours "is closely similar to what holds for other sense-data."

### Reid: perception is the constitution reading natural signs — no inference, no resemblance required
Reid rejects the shared premise of Locke, Berkeley, and (as he would see it) Russell: that the mind's immediate objects are ideas or data from which the world must be recovered. In the *Inquiry* a sensation is a *natural sign*: nature has established a connexion between sign and thing signified, and has also taught us the interpretation, so that "previous to experience, the sign suggests the thing signified, and creates the belief of it." By one original principle of our constitution a certain sensation signifies hardness in the body handled; by another, motion. Perception therefore involves, from the start, a conception of the external quality and an irresistible belief in its present existence — not a conclusion drawn from a private datum.

**Canonical argument** (source: Reid, *Inquiry into the Human Mind*, ch. V, "Of Touch," and ch. VI, "Of Seeing" — a formalization; the corpus copy is a noisy OCR scan of a 19th-century edition, and the reconstruction keeps only to passages legible in it):
- P1. There are three ways the mind passes from a natural sign to the conception and belief of the thing signified: by original principles of our constitution, by custom, and by reasoning. Our *original* perceptions come by the first way; *acquired* perceptions by the second.
- P2. The conception of hardness — one of our most common and distinct notions — resembles no sensation and is derived from neither sensation nor reflection considered as a copy; it is "suggested by our constitution," together with belief.
- ∴ C. Perception is not the inspection of an inner object plus an inference outward. The theory that makes ideas the immediate objects of perception is the innovation to be justified — and, Reid argues in ch. I, it is the shared system of Descartes, Malebranche, and Locke that, consistently developed by Berkeley and Hume, ends in scepticism.

**Objections** — Russell's variation arguments (ch. I) are the standing pressure: if perception delivered the quality itself, why does the delivered colour vary with light, organ, and viewpoint while the object does not? Reid's framework has a reply available in his own terms — the sensation varies because it is a sign, and signs need not resemble what they signify — but it comes at the cost of making the connexion between this sensation and that quality a brute fact of our constitution, which is exactly where Reid is content to stop and his opponents are not.

## Thought experiments
- **The table** (Russell, ch. I). Setup as in the 90-second version: colour, texture, and shape all vary with viewpoint, light, and instrument. Meant to show that no appearance can simply be identified with the object. Pressures any direct identification of what is perceived with the physical object; it is the seed of the sense-data vocabulary.
- **Porphyry in the dark** (Locke, II.viii §19). Hinder light from striking the red-and-white stone and its colours vanish; restore the light and they return — with no real alteration in the stone. Meant to show that colour is a power grounded in texture, not a quality resident in the stone as perceived. Pressures naive realism about colour specifically; Berkeley extends the same treatment to the primary qualities Locke exempts.
- **Manna: sweetness and sickness** (Locke, II.viii §18). Everyone agrees the sickness and griping manna causes are not *in* the manna. But sweetness and whiteness are produced by the same sort of operation of the same parts. Meant to show the asymmetry between primary and secondary qualities cannot be the familiar one between "really in it" and "merely caused in us." Pressures the common-sense placement of tastes and colours in objects.
- **The same water, two hands** (Locke, II.viii §21). Water feels cold to one hand and warm to the other at the same time. If both ideas were resemblances of qualities in the water, the water would be both hot and cold at once. Meant to show heat/cold ideas are effects of relative motion in the perceiver's parts. Pressures resemblance theories for the felt qualities.
- **The circular coin and the blind man** (Russell, ch. III). A coin judged circular looks oval from almost every angle; its real shape belongs to physical space, which nobody's private space reproduces. And what we can know of physical space is exactly what a man born blind could be told about visual space — every structural fact, nothing of what it is like. Meant to fix the ceiling on perceptual knowledge: structure without intrinsic nature. Pressures both Locke (resemblance survives, at best, for structure) and any view promising direct acquaintance with physical qualities.

## Where the debate stands
Nothing here is settled, and the four positions in this corpus disagree at different joints. Locke and Berkeley agree that colours as perceived are not in objects as perceived; they split on whether figure and extension fare better — Berkeley's inseparability argument is aimed exactly at that exemption, and Russell judges Berkeley's detail arguments largely valid while rejecting his conclusion on act/object grounds. Russell and Locke agree there is a mind-independent object; they split on resemblance — Russell keeps only structural correspondence and calls fuller resemblance groundless. Reid rejects the common starting point of the other three: if sensations are signs interpreted by constitution, the demand to recover the object from a datum never arises — but his stopping point (original principles of our constitution) is one the others treat as the thing to be explained. The corpus's later anthology material that would develop the debate (C. I. Lewis on the given, book 007 ch16; Ayer, ch15) is present only as citation stubs, so no further side can be stated from the corpus yet.
