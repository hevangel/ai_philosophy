# Knowledge by Testimony
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the epistemology of testimony; believing what you are told*

## The question
Most of what you know, you were told. That the Emperor of China exists, that Bismarck was an astute diplomatist, what happened before you were born, what is happening now in places you will never visit — all of it rests on the reports of others, usually on reports of reports, in chains that end, if they end anywhere, at someone who saw. Why is believing a report rational at all? Two very different answers divide this corpus. On one, testimony earns our trust the way everything else does: we have *observed* that reports generally conform to facts, and we extend that observed regularity, case by case, with deductions for interested, hesitant, or outnumbered witnesses. On the other, our trust in testimony is not earned but planted: children rely on what they are told long before they could check a track record, and the reliance is an original principle of the mind, prior to experience and reason alike. Which is it — and what follows for the hardest case, testimony to the marvellous and the miraculous?

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus is devoted to testimony, so this version is distilled from the textbook presentation — Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §X — and is marked as such.* There is no species of reasoning "more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life" than that derived from the testimony of men and the reports of eye-witnesses. Hume's wise man proportions his belief to the evidence, and testimony is evidence of a familiar kind: its force comes from our past experience of the veracity of human testimony — of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses — had men not, as experience discovers, a tenacious memory, a common inclination to truth, a principle of probity, and a sensitivity to shame when caught in falsehood. So testimony is weighed, not swallowed: witnesses who contradict each other, who are few or of doubtful character, who have an interest in what they affirm, or who deliver their testimony with hesitation or with too violent asseverations, all diminish its force — and when the fact attested is extraordinary, the experience that credits witnesses collides with the experience that attests the fact, and belief goes to the balance that remains.

## Positions

### Hume: trust in testimony is derived from experience — and only from experience
Hume's *Enquiry* §X, Part I (§§87–90) gives testimony no exemption from his general maxim: all inferences from one object to another are founded on experienced constant conjunction, and "we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other." Nor is there any connexion perceived *a priori* between testimony and reality: "the reason why we place any credit in witnesses and historians is not derived from any connexion, which we perceive a priori, between testimony and reality, but because we are accustomed to find a conformity between them."

**Canonical argument** (source: Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §X, ¶¶87–89 — a formalization):
- P1. Our assurance in any argument from testimony "is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses" (¶88) — a conformity that would vanish were memory not tenacious, and men not inclined to truth, ashamed of detected falsehood, and (in the normal case) without the marks of the delirious or the villainous, who "have no manner of authority with us."
- P2. Evidence from testimony therefore varies with experience, as proof or probability, and is subject to defeaters enumerated in ¶89: opposition of contrary testimony; the character or number of witnesses; the manner of delivery; interest in the matter affirmed.
- P3. When the fact attested is extraordinary, two experiences compete: the experience crediting testimony and the experience against the fact. "The very same principle of experience, which gives us a certain degree of assurance in the testimony of witnesses, gives us also, in this case, another degree of assurance against the fact, which they endeavour to establish" — the superior operates only by the force that remains after the deduction (¶89).
- ∴ C. Belief in testimony is rational exactly insofar as, and to the degree that, experienced conformity supports it, discounted by the ¶89 factors and by the antecedent improbability of the fact attested. The marvellous case is the limiting instance: a miracle, whose testimony is set against uniform experience, yields "proof against proof, of which the strongest must prevail" (¶90).

**History as testimonial chain** — §IV makes the same structure diachronic: "We learn the events of former ages from history; but then we must peruse the volumes in which this instruction is contained, and thence carry up our inferences from one testimony to another, till we arrive at the eyewitnesses and spectators of these distant events." Every chain of reasoning about real existence must terminate in a fact present to memory or senses — one's own, or, via testimony, someone else's.

### Reid: reliance on testimony is an original principle, prior to experience
Reid's *Inquiry* (ch. VI, "Of Seeing," §24) arrives at testimony from language learning. The difficulty: experience shows only that men *have* used words with certain meanings; how do we come to *rely*, with assurance, on their continuing to do so — and to mean what they say? Not by promise: a promise must itself be expressed in signs whose usual meaning and whose speaker's veracity we already take for granted. "No man of common sense ever thought of taking a man's own word for his honesty; and it is evident that we take his veracity for granted, when we lay any stress upon his word or promise. I might add, that this reliance upon the declarations and testimony of men, is found in children long before they know what a promise is."

**Canonical argument** (source: Reid, *Inquiry into the Human Mind*, ch. VI, §24 — a formalization; the corpus copy is a noisy OCR scan of a 19th-century edition, with some interleaved pages in this section, and the reconstruction keeps only to passages legible in it):
- P1. Our reliance on the declarations and testimony of others is present in children before they can have assembled a track record of conformity, and before they understand the institutions (promising) that might seem to underwrite it.
- P2. The reliance cannot be founded on a prior assurance of veracity, since any such assurance would itself have to be taken on testimony (a man's word for his honesty) — a circle; nor does past experience of word-use, which is wholly of the past, by itself yield belief about future voluntary actions.
- ∴ C. "There is, therefore, in the human mind an early anticipation, neither derived from experience, nor from reason" — a principle of credulity built into the constitution, on which the social operations of the mind, and instruction itself, depend. (The scanned text breaks into OCR disorder immediately after this sentence; the surrounding discussion adds the social cost of the opposite policy — a settled distrust would leave men unable to find reasons for believing the thousandth part of what is told them, and children absolutely incredulous and therefore incapable of instruction.)

**Relation to Hume** — the two positions contradict each other at the root, and the corpus lets the contradiction stand bare. Where Hume derives assurance from observed veracity, Reid argues the observation cannot get started: the child's trust precedes the evidence, and the adult's evidence-gathering itself moves within a practice of taking words at their usual meaning and speakers at their word. Where Reid posits an original principle, Hume's framework replies in kind: the principle, if it exists, is known to us only as the experienced uniformity it produces — and ¶89's defeaters describe how even constitutional trust is in fact corrected by experience. Neither text answers the other directly; Hume's *Enquiry* (1748) precedes the *Inquiry* (1764), whose opening chapters attack the "ideal system" Hume inherited rather than §X in particular.

### Russell: testimony is how descriptive knowledge reaches the world — and it bottoms out in sense-data
Russell does not theorize testimony separately; he assigns it a structural place. In ch. V, our judgements about Bismarck rest, for assurance, on "something with which we are acquainted — usually a testimony heard or read"; in ch. VII, the general principle is stated outright: "Our belief that the Emperor of China exists, for example, rests upon testimony, and testimony consists, in the last analysis, of sense-data seen or heard in reading or being spoken to."

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, chs. V and VII — a formalization):
- P1. Nothing can be known to exist except by the help of experience: to prove the existence of something not directly experienced, our premises must include the existence of things of which we *have* direct experience (ch. VII).
- P2. Testimony is the standard vehicle of such proofs beyond our private experience — and it is, in the last analysis, acquaintance with sense-data: marks on a page, sounds in the air.
- ∴ C. Testimonial knowledge is knowledge by description whose chain of dependence terminates in the hearer's or reader's own acquaintance. This vindicates testimony's reach (it is how most descriptive knowledge of existents is possible at all) while locating its foundation exactly where Hume's account does — in the individual's experience — though Russell's argument is architectural (existence-claims need experiential premises) rather than inductive (reports have usually conformed).

**The reading case** — ch. XIII adds a mechanism: reading the announcement that the King is dead, we pass from print to meaning by association, without performing any inference; Russell counts this derivative knowledge anyway, because a valid logical connexion, discoverable on reflection, runs parallel to the psychological one. Testimonial uptake, on this picture, is mostly not reasoning at all — it is a habituated passage from sense-data to belief whose credentials are assessed only when challenged.

## Thought experiments
- **The Indian prince and the frost** (Hume, *Enquiry*, §X, ¶89). A prince who has never left a warm climate is told that water freezes solid in cold ones. The fact is not contrary to his experience — he has no experience of water in Muscovy winters — but not conformable to it either; Hume judges that he "reasoned justly" in refusing belief, and that very strong testimony was *required* to engage his assent. Meant to show that testimonial evidence is not all-or-nothing: its required strength scales with the attested fact's distance from uniform experience. Pressures Reid's constitutional trust (the prince's constitution presumably inclines him to believe; his reason rightly resists) and naive versions of Hume alike (the testimony was, in fact, true and sufficient eventually — the scaling must not harden into refusal).
- **"I should not believe such a story were it told me by Cato"** (Hume, §X, ¶89, citing the Roman proverb). Even the witness of maximal character and authority can be invalidated by the incredibility of the fact. Meant to fix the ceiling of testimonial authority: character multiplies evidence but cannot substitute for it. Pressures any deference-based account of testimony — no witness's standing exempts a report from the balance of experiences.
- **The Emperor of China** (Russell, chs. IV and VII). Russell is not acquainted with the Emperor of China, nor (in ch. IV's use) needs to be, to judge truly that he exists: the judgement rides on other people's acquaintance, transmitted to him as testimony, and ultimately on his own sense-data in reading and hearing. Meant to exhibit, in one mundane fact, the whole structure this entry is about — a true existential judgement with no acquaintance anywhere in the judger. Pressures the principle that we cannot know to exist what nobody is acquainted with (ch. IV's target) and displays testimony as the bridge the acquaintance/description machinery requires.

## Where the debate stands
The corpus stages a genuine, unresolved contradiction about foundations. Hume: no a priori connexion between testimony and reality, assurance wholly from observed conformity, rationally discounted by witness-factors and by the improbability of the fact — a policy whose limiting case (miracles) is the *Enquiry*'s most famous application. Reid: the observed-conformity story cannot be the foundation, because reliance precedes observation in children and is presupposed by every attempt to gather the observations; trust is constitutional. Russell declines to referee and instead locates testimony within his architecture, foundationally dependent on the individual's sense-data — which aligns his account's *terminus* with Hume's while leaving Hume's inductive justification of the transitions unendorsed. What is not in dispute anywhere in these texts: that testimony is indispensable ("more useful, and even necessary to human life" than almost any reasoning), that it is defeasible, and that its force diminishes with distance from eyewitnesses. What is in dispute: whether its rational authority is borrowed from experience or original — and, with it, whether the child's trust is a prejudice to be corrected or a principle to be acknowledged.
