# A Priori Knowledge
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the rationalism–empiricism controversy; innate ideas; the synthetic a priori*

## The question
Some things you know only because experience taught you: that it rained yesterday, that copper conducts electricity. Others seem to be knowable without any such checking — that two and two make four, that nothing can both be and not be, that whatever follows from a true proposition is true. A single careful instance of two pairs is enough for the general truth about all pairs, including pairs nobody will ever meet; no enumeration of instances adds to its certainty; and its denial does not describe a possible world but an absurdity. Knowledge of this second kind is called *a priori* — known independently of experience. The problem is double. Does it exist at all (the empiricist says every apparent case is really experience, habit, or a report about our own concepts)? And if it exists, how is it possible — how can we know, in advance and with necessity, general truths about a world we have only partly experienced?

## The 90-second version
A priori knowledge can be known without experience or sense-data; a posteriori knowledge only with it. "Five is prime" and "if Socrates has more wine than Plato, and Plato more than Aristotle, then Socrates has more than Aristotle" are the lecture's a priori examples; "Socrates has a glass of wine" and "it often rains in Portland" are a posteriori — you have to go out into the world and check. Traditionally the a priori travels with two companions: the *necessary* (true in every possible world) and the *analytic* (true in virtue of meanings, as "brothers are male siblings"). Kant is credited with prising the package apart: he claimed that much of our knowledge — arithmetic above all — is *synthetic* a priori: the predicate is not contained in the subject concept (analyse "7 + 5" as you will, you will not find 12 inside it), yet the judgement is knowable without experience and carries necessity. How that combination is possible is the problem this entry tracks through Locke, Leibniz, Kant, and Russell. ## Positions

### The empiricist denial, in its Lockean form: no innate principles
Locke's Book I attacks the alleged foundation of a priori knowledge: principles supposedly imprinted on the soul in advance of experience. The standard proof of innateness was universal consent; Locke replies that the consent does not exist, and would not prove innateness if it did.

**Canonical argument** (source: Locke, *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book I, ch. ii, §§3–5 — a formalization):
- P1. If a truth were imprinted on the mind, the mind would perceive it: "to imprint anything on the mind without the mind's perceiving it, seems to me hardly intelligible."
- P2. The most celebrated candidates — "Whatsoever is, is" and "It is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be" — are not known to children, to "idiots," or to a great part of mankind; they come to be assented to, when they are, only when men come to the use of reason.
- P3. Retreating to *capacity* does not help: if being in the mind means only being knowable by it, then every truth the mind will ever know is equally "innate," including ones a man dies ignorant of — and the term loses its meaning.
- ∴ C. There are no innate speculative principles. Whatever we know, we come to know — which leaves the empiricist owing an account of how general, necessary-seeming truths are acquired from particular experience.

**The debt comes due** — Russell (*Problems of Philosophy*, ch. VIII) states the standard empiricist account of arithmetic, derivation by induction from particular instances, and gives two reasons it fails: the validity of the inductive principle itself cannot be proved by induction; and the general propositions of mathematics "can obviously be known with certainty by consideration of a single instance, and gain nothing by enumeration of other cases in which they have been found to be true."

### Leibniz: necessary truths are innate — as dispositions, not inscriptions
Leibniz's *New Essays* is a reply to Locke, book by book, and its Preface concedes the letter of Locke's objection while denying its force. Necessary truths need not be read in the soul "as in an open book, as the praetor's edict is read upon his album"; it is enough that they can be discovered in us by attention, the senses furnishing the occasions.

**Canonical argument** (source: Leibniz, *New Essays on the Human Understanding*, Preface — a formalization):
- P1. The senses never give us anything but examples — particular or individual truths. All the examples that confirm a general truth, however numerous, do not suffice to establish its universal necessity: it does not follow that what has happened will happen in the same way (day has always followed night within twenty-four hours — yet not everywhere, and perhaps not forever, since the sun itself need not exist).
- P2. Necessary truths, such as those of pure mathematics, logic, metaphysics, and ethics, have proofs that do not depend on examples or on the testimony of the senses, though without the senses it would never have occurred to us to think of them.
- ∴ C. Their proof "can come only from internal principles which are called innate." Experience is the occasion and the confirmation; the ground is in us — as sparks are in the steel, struck out by the encounter of the senses.

**Replies to Locke** — reflection, which Locke himself admits as a source of ideas alongside sensation, "is nothing else than attention to what is in us, and the senses do not give us what we already carry with us": being, unity, substance, duration, change, action, perception are present to the understanding always, even when distraction keeps us from apperceiving them. Innate knowledge, on this account, is virtual — a structured capacity with content, not a set of propositions consciously entertained from birth.

### Kant: synthetic a priori knowledge, grounded in the forms of sensibility
Kant's *Prolegomena* (§2) divides judgements by content: *analytical* judgements are explicative — the predicate is already thought in the concept of the subject ("all bodies are extended"), and they rest wholly on the law of contradiction. *Synthetical* judgements are expansive — the predicate adds something not contained in the subject ("all bodies have weight"). Empirical judgements are always synthetic; Kant's claim is that some synthetic judgements are also a priori, and that mathematics consists of them.

**Canonical argument** (source: Kant, *Prolegomena*, §2 and First Part, §§6–11 — a formalization):
- P1. Mathematical judgements are a priori — they "carry with them necessity, which cannot be obtained from experience" (§2) — yet synthetic: the concept of the sum of 7 and 5 contains only the union of the two numbers in one, not the number 12; to reach 12 we must go beyond the concepts and call intuition to aid, adding the units of the five (shown on fingers or points) successively to the seven. Likewise "a straight line is the shortest between two points": *straight* contains a quality, nothing of quantity; *shortest* is wholly additional.
- P2. Intuition can precede its object — can be a priori — in only one way: if it contains "nothing but the form of sensibility," antedating in the subject all actual impressions. Space and time are such forms: strip from our intuitions of bodies everything empirical, everything belonging to sensation, and space and time remain; they are pure intuitions lying a priori at the basis of the empirical ones (§§9–10). Geometry constructs its concepts in the pure intuition of space; arithmetic accomplishes its concept of number by successive addition of units in time (§10).
- ∴ C. "Pure mathematics, as synthetical cognition a priori, is only possible by referring to no other objects than those of the senses" (§11): a priori propositions concern the form of sensuous intuition, and are "possible and valid for objects of the senses" — for things as they appear to us, not as they are in themselves.

**The price** — the same restriction governs the whole critical philosophy: all synthetic a priori principles "are nothing more than principles of possible experience, and can never be referred to things in themselves, but to appearances as objects of experience" (§31, as extracted in Russell's companion discussion). A priori knowledge is saved by being confined.

### Russell: the controversy decided in parts — and Kant's explanation rejected
Russell's ch. VII delivers a split verdict on the historic controversy. The rationalists were right on the most important point: logical principles are known by us and cannot be proved by experience, since all proof presupposes them. The empiricists were right on another: nothing can be *known to exist* except by the help of experience — all a priori knowledge concerning existence is hypothetical ("if one thing exists, another must exist"), and existence itself is always empirical. And the word *innate* should be dropped: a priori knowledge is "elicited and caused by experience" — particular experiences direct our attention so that we *see* the general truth without proof from experience. His stock contrasts: "two and two are four," where one instance suffices and a world in which it failed (unlike Swift's immortal Struldbrugs, imaginable for "all men are mortal") would "upset the whole fabric of our knowledge"; and the class of a priori knowledge is wider than logic — it includes pure mathematics and, Russell holds, judgements of intrinsic value ("happiness is more desirable than misery"), which likewise can be neither proved nor disproved by experience.

Ch. VIII then takes up Kant's explanation and rejects it on two grounds.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. VIII — Russell's counter-argument to Kant, a formalization):
- P1. What needs accounting for is our certainty that *the facts* must always conform to logic and arithmetic. Saying that logic and arithmetic are contributed by us does not account for it: "Our nature is as much a fact of the existing world as anything, and there can be no certainty that it will remain constant. It might happen, if Kant is right, that to-morrow our nature would so change as to make two and two become five."
- P2. A priori judgements are about things, not about our thoughts: believing the law of contradiction is believing that if a tree is a beech it cannot at the same time not be a beech — not believing a fact about the constitution of our minds. And their scope exceeds possible experience: two physical objects and two others make four "even if physical objects cannot be experienced."
- ∴ C. Kant's solution both fails to explain the certainty and "unduly limits the scope of a priori propositions." Russell's alternative: a priori knowledge concerns relations and qualities — entities that do not, properly speaking, exist in either the mental or the physical world (the relation *in*, in "I am in my room," is something, though it does not exist as I and the room do). That an earwig is in the room is true whether or not anyone is aware of it; so relations are not the work of the mind, and a priori truth is about a world that is neither mental nor physical — the world of universals, treated in chs. IX–X.

### The alignment under pressure: Kripke's contingent a priori (lecture-presented)
Book 007 gives the a priori its own section — C. I. Lewis (ch23), Chisholm (ch24), Kripke (ch25) — but in this corpus all three are citation stubs, so the contemporary debate cannot be read from the anthology. The Carneades lecture [Kripke's Contingent, A Priori Statements](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=enIsXrWAqvc) presents Kripke's challenge to the traditional alignment (a priori = necessary = analytic) and is used here labelled as a lecture presentation: let stick S be the standard metre in world W, the object by reference to which "one metre" is defined. "The length of S is one metre" is *contingent* — S has different lengths in other possible worlds, and "the length of S" does not rigidly designate — yet for the people in W it is knowable *a priori*, since the metre is defined by S and no measurement of S is needed to discover its length. If the example stands, contingency and a priority come apart, and the tidy threefold alignment that Kant's framework presupposes — and that the lecture series sets out to doubt — breaks at a second joint (the first being Kant's own synthetic a priori).

## Thought experiments
- **Two Londoners a century hence** (Russell, ch. VIII). We do not know who will inhabit London in a hundred years, but we know that any two of them and any other two will make four. Meant to make the puzzle vivid: a priori knowledge anticipates facts about things of which we have no experience whatsoever. Pressures pure empiricism (no instance of these people has been or could be checked) and any account that ties a priori truth to what we happen to have encountered.
- **Struldbrugs vs two and two make five** (Russell, ch. VII). Swift's immortal race can be imagined without strain; a world where two and two make five cannot — it "seems quite on a different level." Meant to isolate the *necessity* that marks a priori general propositions off from even the best-attested empirical generalizations. Pressures the claim that the difference between the two kinds is merely degree of confidence or habit.
- **Fingers and points for 7 + 5** (Kant, *Prolegomena*, §2). Try to extract 12 from the concepts 7, 5, and addition by analysis alone; you cannot — you must successively add units given in an image. Meant to show arithmetic is synthetic (it amplifies concepts) rather than analytic. Pressures the pre-Kantian view that all a priori truth is unpacking, true by the law of contradiction alone.
- **The standard metre stick** (Kripke, via the Carneades lecture). As above: the stick that defines the metre is a metre long, knowably without measurement, yet might not have been. Meant to detach the a priori from the necessary. Pressures the traditional alignment from the opposite side to Kant: Kant found necessary truth that is not analytic; Kripke's case purports to find a priori truth that is not necessary. (Presented from the lecture; Kripke's text, book 007 ch25, is a stub in this corpus.)

## Where the debate stands
On the existence of a priori knowledge, the sources in this corpus mostly converge, once terms are fixed: Locke's target is innate *propositions* consciously possessed, and Leibniz's dispositional innateness and Russell's "elicited by experience, not proved by it" both grant Locke's observation (children do not entertain the laws of thought) while denying his conclusion. On the explanation there is no convergence at all. Leibniz grounds necessity in internal principles whose content he does not derive; Kant grounds it in the forms of our sensibility and pays with confinement to appearances; Russell refutes Kant's confinement and relocates the a priori in relations among universals — a world he begins, but in these chapters only begins, to describe. And the Kripke lecture, if its example is sound, breaks the framework's background assumption that the a priori, the necessary, and the analytic march together — a challenge the corpus can currently state only from the lecture, since the anthology's a priori section is unreadable here (stubs). Unsettled, then, at every joint except one: the induction-based empiricist account of mathematics, which Russell's two objections — pressed already in the induction entry's territory — leave, in these texts, without a defender.
