# External-World Skepticism
*Subfield: Epistemology · Also known as: the problem of the external world; scepticism about the senses*

## The question
Everything you have ever encountered has arrived as experience — colours, sounds, pressures, thoughts. How, then, can you know that anything exists outside that experience: that the table you see is a real object which persists when nobody looks at it, that other bodies and other minds are not figures in a prolonged dream? The skeptical scenarios are devices for making this gap vivid: they describe situations in which your experience would be exactly as it is now, yet the world would not be as you believe it to be.

## The 90-second version
A skeptical scenario is a thought experiment designed to give you the intuition that you could be deceived — about anything from how one sense represents the world to everything you think you know. Optical illusions show the possibility in miniature: lines that look unequal are equal. The scenarios then escalate. In a dream, everything seems perfectly real while it lasts, and you can never prove from inside an experience that you have genuinely woken up rather than dreamed waking up. Descartes' evil deceiver goes further: where dreams and simulations leave your reasoning intact (two plus two is four even in a dream), the deceiver tampers with the reasoning itself, nudging you to conclude that two plus two equals five. The brain in a vat is the same deceiver rebuilt with technology — a scientist stimulating your envatted brain could produce your whole experiential world and even interfere with your inferences — and it convinces where the supernatural deceiver may not, because something like it might be physically possible a century from now. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Skeptical Scenarios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5xQ3X-KRZOs), [What If You Were Dreaming?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hXVZ6ybYaI), [The Evil Deceiver](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ki7_QGkLAPI), [Brain in a Vat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Cpm0NUYMUY).)

## Positions

### The skeptical challenge (Descartes' method of doubt, as Russell presents it)
Descartes determined to believe nothing he could bring himself to doubt, and invented the deceitful demon to make doubt about the senses unavoidable: such a demon could present "unreal things to his senses in a perpetual phantasmagoria," and although it is very improbable that such a demon exists, "still it was possible, and therefore doubt concerning things perceived by the senses was possible."

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. II — a formalization of the doubt Descartes' method generates):
- P1. If a deceiver (or an equivalent scenario — a lifelong dream, envatment) were producing my experiences, my sense-based beliefs about physical objects could all be false while my experiences remained exactly as they are.
- P2. Nothing in my experience rules such a scenario out; it is possible.
- ∴ C. My sense-based beliefs about physical objects are not certain; doubt concerning them is always possible.

### The cogito: one certainty survives (Descartes)
Doubt, turned on everything, stops at one point: "if he doubted, he must exist; if he had any experiences whatever, he must exist." Hence *Cogito, ergo sum* — 'I think, therefore I am'.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. II; Carneades, [The Cogito (Meditation I)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P6z1kDa9wvY) and [The Cogito (Meditation II)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SzaKqVGec_Q)):
- P1. To be deceived, or to doubt, there must be something that is deceived or doubts.
- P2. Doubting is occurring — this very activity cannot itself be doubted away, since trying to doubt that I doubt is itself a case of doubting.
- ∴ C. I exist — at least as a thinking thing, for as long as the thinking occurs.

**Objections** — Russell himself enters a caution: "'I think, therefore I am' says rather more than is strictly certain." What has absolute, convincing certainty is not a permanent Self but particular experiences: when Russell sees a brown colour, what is quite certain is not "'I am seeing a brown colour', but rather, 'a brown colour is being seen'." The enduring 'I' is "as hard to arrive at as the real table." The Meditation II lecture adds a structural worry: stated as a syllogism (whatever thinks exists; I think; so I exist), the cogito relies on a general premise and on inference itself — both fair game for the deceiver; Descartes' own formulation avoids "therefore" because the certainty is meant to be immediate, not inferred. The Carneades series presses harder still in [Doubting "I Think"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CTVOHxe7ym0): it constructs cases in which the belief "I am thinking" is produced by a mismatched cause (one sensation habitually signalling thought, another — a tickle — triggering the belief instead), arguing that a true belief formed by the wrong route is luck, not knowledge — a Gettier-style problem applied to the cogito itself.

### Idealism (Berkeley, as Russell presents him)
Berkeley's *Three Dialogues* undertake "to prove that there is no such thing as matter at all, and that the world consists of nothing but minds and their ideas." Berkeley does not deny that something persists when we leave the room; he denies that this something is non-mental. The "real" table is an idea in the mind of God, which gives it the required permanence and independence of *us* without making it something we can never be directly aware of.

**The idealist argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. I — in the compressed form in which Russell states it, and which he judges fallacious):
- P1. Whatever can be thought of is an idea in the mind of the person thinking of it.
- P2. Therefore nothing can be thought of except ideas in minds.
- ∴ C. Anything else is inconceivable, and what is inconceivable cannot exist.

**Replies** — Russell: "Such an argument, in my opinion, is fallacious" (being thought *of* does not make a thing an idea *in* a mind). But he credits Berkeley with the lasting result: showing "that the existence of matter is capable of being denied without absurdity, and that if there are any things that exist independently of us they cannot be the immediate objects of our sensations."

### Russell's answer: instinctive belief and simplicity
Russell concedes at the outset that proof is impossible: "it must be admitted that we can never prove the existence of things other than ourselves and our experiences. No logical absurdity results from the hypothesis that the world consists of myself and my thoughts and feelings and sensations, and that everything else is mere fancy." His case for the external world is not a demonstration but a comparison of hypotheses, resting on what he calls our instinctive beliefs.

**Canonical argument** (source: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. II):
- P1. Our sense-data behave as signs of persistent, public objects: the cat reappears in a new place having (apparently) crossed the room and grown hungry in between; other people's speech behaves exactly as the expression of thoughts like our own would.
- P2. The hypothesis of independent physical objects systematizes these data simply. The dream hypothesis does not: a cat made only of sense-data "did not exist at all while I was not looking, but suddenly sprang into being in a new place," and appetite would have to grow during non-existence.
- P3. A belief may only be rejected when it clashes with other beliefs; the belief in an independent world is instinctive, leads to no difficulties, and "tends to simplify and systematize our account of our experiences."
- ∴ C. We may rationally accept that the external world really exists and is "not wholly dependent for its existence upon our continuing to perceive it" — though "with a slight doubt derived from dreams."

**Objections** — the conclusion is deliberately weaker than knowledge-as-proof: Russell's own verdict is that the argument "is doubtless less strong than we could wish." What it establishes, if anything, is that realism is the rational default, not that skepticism is false.

### Hume: reason cannot bridge the gap — and does not need to
Hume's *Enquiry* §XII runs the skeptical argument to what he presents as its unavoidable end, and then declines to live by it.

**Canonical argument** (source: Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §XII, ¶¶118–119):
- P1. Nothing can ever be present to the mind but a perception: "the senses are only the inlets, through which these images are conveyed, without being able to produce any immediate intercourse between the mind and the object." (The table we see shrinks as we walk away; the real table, if any, suffers no alteration — so what was present to the mind was its image.)
- P2. Whether perceptions are caused by external objects resembling them is a question of fact, and questions of fact are settled by experience; but here "experience is, and must be entirely silent," since the mind never has anything before it but perceptions to compare with objects — and perceptions admittedly sometimes arise with no external object, "as in dreams, madness, and other diseases."
- ∴ C. "The supposition of such a connexion is, therefore, without any foundation in reasoning."

**Objections to attempted escapes** — appealing to God's veracity to certify the senses is "making a very unexpected circuit": if God never deceives, the senses would be infallible, which they are not; and once the external world is in question, "we shall be at a loss to find arguments, by which we may prove the existence of that Being or any of his attributes" (§XII, ¶120). Against Descartes' *antecedent* doubt, Hume adds that it "would be entirely incurable" if ever attained: there is no original principle with "a prerogative above others, that are self-evident and convincing," and every step beyond it would use the very faculties already distrusted (§XII, ¶116).

**Hume's own stance** — the skeptical argument is, at the level of pure reasoning, a topic "in which the profounder and more philosophical sceptics will always triumph" (§XII, ¶121). But the belief in body is an instinct of nature, not a conclusion of reason, and Hume's recommendation is a *mitigated* scepticism: keep the doubt as a corrective to dogmatism ("a small tincture of Pyrrhonism might abate their pride"), and limit enquiry "to such subjects as are best adapted to the narrow capacity of human understanding" (§XII, ¶¶129–130). Russell's closing position in *The Limits of Philosophical Knowledge* is a cousin of this: against the complete sceptic who demands to be compelled back into knowledge from outside it, "no logical argument can be advanced" — but such scepticism is unreasonable, and philosophy's criticism should retain whatever still appears to be knowledge after examination.

## Thought experiments
- **The dream** (Descartes, via Russell ch. II; [What If You Were Dreaming?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hXVZ6ybYaI)). You wake — except the waking is itself inside a dream (the lecture nests false awakenings, ending with the dreamer's hands turning into cicadas). Meant to show: no mark within experience certifies that it is not a dream. Pressures all sense-based belief; leaves reasoning untouched.
- **The evil deceiver** (Descartes, via Russell ch. II; [The Evil Deceiver](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ki7_QGkLAPI)). A powerful being systematically interferes with your inferences, making you conclude that two plus two equals five. Descartes devised it not to endorse skepticism but to give the skeptic everything and still find knowledge. Pressures reasoning itself, not only the senses.
- **The brain in a vat** ([Brain in a Vat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Cpm0NUYMUY)). A scientist's instruments simulate your entire environment directly on your brain and can corrupt your reasoning as the deceiver did. Pressures senses and reason together; its advantage over the deceiver, per the lecture, is plausibility — it is "an updated version of the evil deceiver" that might exist in a hundred years.
- **The experience machine** (series neighbour; [The Experience Machine](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QlgepnXl6vc)). Included in the same lecture series although, as the lecturer says, it is not a skeptical scenario: the lecture's version is a "UFO machine" (from a Vonnegut story) that floods a room with lifelong euphoria, aimed at the hedonistic claim that pleasure is the good. It belongs to the ethics of experience rather than to skepticism, but it shares the family form — a perfect stream of experience cut loose from reality — and so sits naturally in this entry's Connections.

## Where the debate stands
On the ground covered by these sources, the skeptical argument itself is nowhere refuted. Russell concedes that the existence of anything beyond ourselves and our experiences can never be proved, and settles for an instinctive belief vindicated by simplicity and harmony. Hume concedes that the sceptic "will always triumph" in the argument about the senses, and settles for nature's instinct plus a mitigated scepticism that turns doubt against dogmatism instead. Descartes, as our sources present him, secures one point — the cogito — and the lecture series goes on to worry even at that. The live disagreement in these texts is therefore not whether the skeptical scenario can be disproved (all sides here grant it cannot) but what follows: a foundation rebuilt from the cogito, a rational default held with a slight doubt, or an instinct we neither can nor need to justify. The anthology's dedicated anti-skeptical readings (Moore, Strawson) are present in book 007 only as citations, so the corpus cannot yet state their side.

## Connections
- [The Problem of Induction](problem-of-induction.md) — Hume's other great gap: there, experience cannot justify a step to the unobserved; here, it cannot justify a step to the external. Russell answers both with a mixture of instinctive belief and a priori principle.
- [Gettier and the Analysis of Knowledge](gettier-and-the-analysis-of-knowledge.md) — if knowledge demanded the certainty skepticism denies us, almost nothing would count as knowledge; the analysis debate is partly about what knowledge requires short of that. The Gettier structure also appears inside this problem: the "Doubting 'I Think'" lecture uses a luck-based objection against the cogito.
- Acquaintance and description (Russell, ch. V) — the machinery Russell builds on the sense-data foundation this problem leaves standing: physical objects and other minds are known, if at all, by description.
