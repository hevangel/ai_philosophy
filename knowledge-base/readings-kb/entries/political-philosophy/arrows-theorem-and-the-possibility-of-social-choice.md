# Arrow's Theorem and the Possibility of Social Choice
*Subfield: Political philosophy · Also known as: Arrow's impossibility theorem; the General Possibility Theorem; the problem of preference aggregation*

## The question
Democracy promises to turn many individual preferences into one collective decision. But what exactly should the conversion rule be — and can any rule be *fair*? Kenneth Arrow asked the question with unusual precision (1951): define a rule that takes any set of individual preference rankings over social alternatives and returns a single social ranking, and require of it only conditions that each look like common sense. His theorem is that, once there are three or more alternatives, no such rule exists: every rule meeting the other conditions is either imposed on the voters or is one person's dictatorship. This entry states the theorem only as precisely as its sources state it, and tracks the three escape routes the literature in this corpus actually pursues.

## The 90-second version
Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem claims there is no voting system that can choose between more than two candidates and satisfy all of our desirability criteria. The lecture proves a simpler single-winner version first: take three voters whose rankings form a cycle — each candidate beats one rival and loses to the other, so there is no Condorcet winner. Now require a system to respect the Condorcet-win criterion (a candidate who beats every other head-to-head must win) and independence of irrelevant alternatives (switching how a voter ranks two candidates must not change the outcome between other pairs). By sliding each voter, one at a time, into profiles where exactly one candidate is the Condorcet winner, the proof shows each candidate in turn *cannot* win the cyclic profile — so the system sometimes produces no winner at all. Arrow's own theorem is then stated, not proved: for systems outputting a full social ranking, no system satisfies unrestricted domain, unrestricted range, the Pareto condition, non-dictatorship, and independence of irrelevant alternatives. The stakes the lecture draws: either fair systems are confined to two choices, or one of the "obvious" criteria has to go. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (The Problem of the Third Party)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GCX7gafg0xI), series introduction [Do Third Parties Destroy Democracy?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_khUtnvhoY).)

## Positions

### The problem as Arrow poses it (Arrow, *Social Choice and Individual Values*, chs. I–III)
"In a capitalist democracy there are essentially two methods by which social choices can be made: voting… and the market mechanism." Arrow studies the first. Two ground rules shape everything: preferences are *ordinal* rankings only, and — ch. II's foundational exclusion — interpersonal comparisons of utility are not available, so no rule may use intensities or add utilities across persons (the Benthamite sum of utilities is examined in ch. III and set aside for exactly this reason).

**Definitions and conditions** (source: Arrow, ch. III — a formalization, labelled as such): A *social welfare function* is "a process or rule which, for each set of individual orderings R₁…Rₙ for alternative social states (one ordering for each individual), states a corresponding social ordering of alternative social states, R," where the social ordering must itself be a genuine ordering (connected and transitive). Arrow imposes five conditions, "of course value judgments," taken together to express "citizens' sovereignty and rationality":
1. **Free triple / range of orderings** — there is a set of three alternatives that individuals are free to order in any way, and the function must still deliver a social ordering (the germ of *unrestricted domain*).
2. **Positive association** — if an alternative rises, or stays, in every individual's ordering and nothing else changes, it does not fall in the social ordering.
3. **Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)** — the social choice from any set of alternatives depends only on individual orderings *within* that set (Arrow's illustration: a candidate's death mid-election should change the outcome only by deleting his name from every ballot).
4. **Non-imposition** — the function is not *imposed*: no pair of alternatives has its social ranking fixed in advance, whatever all individuals prefer.
5. **Non-dictatorship** — there is no individual whose strict preference between any pair is always society's, regardless of everyone else's orderings.

### The theorem (Arrow, ch. V — stated as printed, with its companion interpretation)
**Theorem** (source: Arrow, ch. V, Theorem 2, "General Possibility Theorem"): "If there are at least three alternatives which the members of the society are free to order in any way, then every social welfare function satisfying Conditions 2 and 3 and yielding a social ordering [satisfying the consistency axioms] must be either imposed or dictatorial." (The extraction's statement prints "Conditions 2 and 8"; the chapter's own proof summary confirms the content: given positive association, IIA, and freedom to order three alternatives, "either Condition 4 or Condition 5 must be violated.") Arrow's own gloss, ch. V: "If we exclude the possibility of interpersonal comparisons of utility, then the only methods of passing from individual tastes to social preferences which will be satisfactory and which will be defined for a wide range of sets of individual orderings are either imposed or dictatorial."

**The contrast case** (source: Arrow, ch. V, Theorem 1): with exactly **two** alternatives, majority decision satisfies the conditions and always yields a social ordering. Impossibility begins at three — the formal root of the lecture series' question whether third parties destroy democracy.

**The modern four-condition form** (source: Pattanaik's Introduction to book 036): with at least three distinct social states and finitely many individuals, *no social welfare function simultaneously satisfies unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and nondictatorship.* Pattanaik notes why the result unsettles: the qualifications "are not particularly restrictive, and… each of the four conditions seems to have some prima facie plausibility," giving the theorem "the flavor of a paradox." (The Pareto principle — if everybody strictly prefers x to y, society ranks x above y — is the welfare form of Arrow's positive-association/citizens'-sovereignty conditions; the formulations differ in bookkeeping, and this entry does not merge them.)

### Escape route 1: restrict the domain (Arrow, chs. V–VII; the lecture on avoiding Arrow)
Arrow himself indicates the first exit at the end of ch. V: give up the demand that the rule work for *every* profile of orderings — suppose individual orderings are known in advance to satisfy restrictions, and find rules satisfactory on that restricted range. Chs. VI–VII develop this (conditions on preference scales; similarity/near-unanimity of orderings as a basis for social judgment). The Carneades lecture [Avoiding Arrow's Impossibility](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WCoNvN9MDDQ) walks the same ground from the third-party advocate's side: each criterion — unrestricted domain, unrestricted range, always-a-winner, Pareto, non-dictatorship, IIA — is one the advocate must argue is worth less than having more than two choices; attempts to *strengthen* domain/range instead (demanding that off-ballot candidates be choosable) only make impossibility worse, since they tighten the requirements.

### Escape route 2: enrich the information (Sen)
**Position** (source: Sen, "The Informational Basis of Social Choice," book 036, ch. 15 in extraction): "Any procedure of social choice makes use of some types of information and ignores others" — majority decision uses votes but ignores intensities of preference, social standing, "prosperity or penury." Impossibility is relative to an informational basis: Arrow's construction *defines away* cardinal and interpersonally comparable information before the proof begins. A procedure permitted richer information is not bound by the result in the same way. Sen's lecture in the same volume adds the political edge: Arrow's axioms also do not accommodate *liberty* — the rights of minorities against majorities — recalling Mill on "busybodies": "The importance of liberty may demand going against the objections of numerous 'busybodies.' … It is not accommodated in Arrow's axioms either."

### Escape route 3: majority rule is better than the theorem suggests (Maskin; Dasgupta & Maskin)
**Position** (source: Maskin lecture, book 036; Dasgupta & Maskin, "On the Robustness of Majority Rule," book 036): the pessimistic reading — no good election method — is "too strong a conclusion." Define majority rule in Condorcet's sense: the winner beats every other candidate head-to-head. Majority rule satisfies the Pareto property, anonymity ("one person–one vote"), neutrality, and IIA — indeed Dasgupta and Maskin's abstract states it satisfies those axioms plus (generic) decisiveness "over a larger class of preference domains than (essentially) any other voting rule. Hence, in this sense, it is the most robust voting rule." Maskin's conclusion: "using the criteria [Arrow] laid out, there is a sense in which we can't do better than majority rule after all." The honest caveat travels with the claim, in Maskin's own footnote: when Condorcet-paradox rankings do arise, majority rule delivers no winner, and practice needs a tie-breaking rule (he suggests plurality) — frequency and cost of the cyclic case are where the argument moves next.

## Thought experiments
- **The Condorcet cycle** (lecture [Arrow's Impossibility Theorem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GCX7gafg0xI)). Three voters rank three candidates in rotation (the lecture's instance uses Trump, Clinton, Johnson): majority prefers the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first. Every candidate both beats and loses to another; there is no Condorcet winner. Meant to show the failure is not in any particular rule but in the profile itself: pairwise majority — the most natural aggregation of all — is intransitive here, and Arrow's theorem generalizes the embarrassment to *every* rule meeting mild conditions. Pressures: unrestricted domain (exclude such profiles?) and IIA (the lecture's proof turns on it).
- **The candidate who dies** (Arrow, ch. III). An election is held; before the count, a candidate dies. Intuition says: delete his name from every ballot and recount. A rule whose winner changes for any other reason violates IIA. Meant to make IIA look undeniable — which is what makes its role in the impossibility worth scrutinizing (Sen and the Borda count, which violates IIA and is still widely used, are the counterweights in our sources: Arrow's own ch. III shows the rank-order method's IIA failure by example).

## Where the debate stands
The theorem itself is a proof and is not in dispute in these sources; everything live is in the response. Arrow's own response was domain restriction — possibility returns when profiles are constrained (chs. VI–VII) — and the two-alternative case was never impossible (majority decision works). Sen reframes the result as a theorem about a deliberately impoverished informational basis and points social choice toward richer inputs (and toward liberty, which the axioms omit). Maskin argues the practical moral is nearly optimistic: majority rule is the most robust rule available, with cyclic profiles as a tie-breaking problem rather than a refutation. The lecture series leaves the third-party question at the criterion level: a defender of multi-candidate elections must say *which* desirability criterion to surrender — that, and not a counterexample (Arrow proved none can exist), is the honest shape of the remaining argument.
