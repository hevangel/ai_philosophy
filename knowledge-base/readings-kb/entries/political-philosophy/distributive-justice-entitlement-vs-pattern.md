# Distributive Justice: Entitlement vs Pattern
*Subfield: Political philosophy · Also known as: justice in holdings; patterned vs historical principles*

## The question
What makes a distribution of wealth and goods *just*? One family of answers looks at the shape of the outcome: shares should track need, merit, contribution, or equality, or at least leave the worst off as well off as possible. Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) answers with history instead: a distribution is just if it *arose* justly — if every holding was acquired and transferred by legitimate steps — whatever shape it ends in. If Nozick is right, maintaining any preferred pattern requires taxing and redistributing, and redistribution violates rights. If his critics are right, the argument assumes the very property rights it needs to prove.

## The 90-second version
*No Carneades lecture in the corpus covers Nozick or distributive justice directly; this version is distilled from the textbook sources below and is marked accordingly, per the build rules.* — Start from any distribution you consider ideally just — perfectly equal, say. Now let people use their shares as they choose: a million fans each pay a basketball star 25 cents to watch him play. He ends up far richer than anyone else, and the pattern is gone — yet every step was voluntary and nobody's share was taken. Nozick's moral: whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just, so justice cannot be a pattern; to keep restoring the pattern the state must continually interfere with voluntary transfers, and taxing earnings is, he says, on a par with forced labour. The reply staged in our anthology (Onora O'Neill) is that the example proves nothing until Nozick establishes that property rights are *full* capitalist rights — unlimited accumulation, transfer at will — since rival theories simply build different, more limited property rights into the pattern itself. *(Textbook-distilled — no lecture source.)*

## Positions

### The entitlement theory (Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, ch. 7, §I)
Justice in holdings has three topics: original acquisition of unheld things, transfer of holdings between persons, and rectification of past injustice. With the first two principles in place, the theory is stated as an inductive definition.

**Canonical argument** (source: Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, ch. 7, §I):
- P1. "A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in acquisition is entitled to that holding."
- P2. "A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in transfer, from someone else entitled to the holding, is entitled to the holding." (And: "No one is entitled to a holding except by (repeated) applications of 1 and 2.")
- ∴ C. "A distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution" — i.e. "a distribution is just if it arises from another just distribution by legitimate means," because the specified means of change are *justice-preserving*, as truth-preserving inference rules preserve truth. "Justice in holdings is historical; it depends upon what actually has happened."

**The supporting distinctions** — Rival principles divide into: *end-result / current time-slice principles*, which judge a distribution by its structural profile at a moment, using only information in a distribution matrix; *historical principles*, which let past actions create differential entitlements; and, cutting across, *patterned principles*, which specify that shares vary along some natural dimension (merit, need, contribution, I.Q., or a weighted sum). "Almost every suggested principle of distributive justice is patterned… The principle of entitlement we have sketched is *not* patterned."

**The Wilt Chamberlain argument** (source: Nozick, ch. 7, §I, "How Liberty Upsets Patterns" — a formalization of the example as Nozick runs it):
- P1. Let *D*1 be any distribution a non-entitlement theory favours — suppose it is just. Each person is entitled to dispose of the resources *D*1 assigns them; that is what the shares are *for*.
- P2. From *D*1, one million people voluntarily transfer 25 cents each to Wilt Chamberlain in exchange for watching him play, yielding *D*2, in which he holds $250,000 and the pattern is broken. Third parties' shares are unchanged, and they had no claim of justice against the transferred resources before the transfer.
- ∴ C. *D*2 is just if *D*1 was; hence a patterned or end-state principle can be maintained only by "continually interfer[ing] to stop people from transferring resources as they wish to" — Nozick's summary slogan: "*From each as they choose, to each as they are chosen.*" And on redistribution directly: "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor," since taking the earnings of *n* hours is like taking *n* hours from the person.

**The Lockean proviso** — acquisition is by mixing labour with the unowned, *provided* "enough and as good" is left for others: an appropriation is legitimate only if it does not worsen others' situation (or compensates them so it does not). No one may appropriate the only water hole in a desert and charge what he will; nor may he buy up all the water holes one by one — the proviso casts a "historical shadow" over later transfers too. Nozick concedes the proviso will "(almost?) never" come into effect in practice, as prices rise against the would-be monopolist.

### Rawls: justice as fairness (as staged against Nozick)
Our anthology stages the rival in two forms, and they must not be conflated. In the anthology's ch. 15, **Rawls's 1958 paper "Justice as Fairness"** treats justice as a virtue of *practices* (institutions), not of particular distributions person by person.

**Canonical statement** (source: Rawls, "Justice as Fairness," anthology 3rd ed., ch. 15):
- P1 (first principle). "Each person participating in a practice, or affected by it, has an equal right to the most extensive liberty compatible with a like liberty for all."
- P2 (second principle). "Inequalities are arbitrary unless it is reasonable to expect that they will work out for everyone's advantage, and provided the positions and offices to which they attach… are open to all."
- ∴ C. Justice is "essentially the elimination of arbitrary distinctions and the establishment, within the structure of a practice, of a proper balance between competing claims" — a complex of "liberty, equality, and reward for services contributing to the common good," chosen as the terms parties could mutually acknowledge, which is what classical utilitarianism, on Rawls's argument, cannot account for.

Nozick discusses the later, stronger version — *A Theory of Justice*'s **original position**, in which rational, mutually disinterested parties choose principles behind a *veil of ignorance* of their place, class, and natural assets, arriving at principles under which inequalities are just "only if they result in compensating benefits for everyone, and in particular for the least advantaged members of society" (the difference principle) — and contests the step from "the distribution of natural assets is morally arbitrary" to patterned conclusions, and the treatment of talents as a collective asset. That presentation is Nozick's (ch. 7, §II); the corpus holds no copy of *A Theory of Justice* itself, so the TJ position enters this entry only as Nozick presents it and as the 1958 paper states its ancestor.

### The staged objection: O'Neill on Nozick (anthology, ch. 16)
Onora O'Neill's chapter — the anthology's own critical companion to Nozick — accepts his starting rights (not to be harmed in life, health, liberty) and his minimal-state derivation for argument's sake, and queries the conclusions.

**Objection** (source: O'Neill, "Nozick's Entitlements," anthology 3rd ed., ch. 16):
- P1. The Wilt Chamberlain argument shows pattern-restoration to be unjust interference only if the transfers it blocks would violate the transferrers' property rights. But "it is just these property rights which have yet to be established": rival theories assign *limited* property rights (an upper limit on holdings, or limits on use and transfer), relative to which restoration *maintains* rather than violates rights.
- P2. Nozick concedes (p. 171 of ASU, as O'Neill cites it) that we lack a theory of property; the assumption that property rights are "rights to control resources in all ways… or to accumulate them without limit" must be established *before* pattern-restoration can be rejected as rights-violating. The intuition that there is "nothing very wrong" with paying Chamberlain is matched by an intuition that there is nothing very wrong in taxing his enlarged earnings; and restoration works by familiar devices (income tax, welfare payments) that fall short of the "continuous interference" Nozick depicts — a socialist society "would have to forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults," but a pattern-maintaining tax state need not.
- ∴ C. "Until Nozick provides an argument to show that property rights must be full capitalist property rights the Wilt Chamberlain example shows nothing."
- And on acquisition: entitlement theory is empty without its two principles specified; Nozick himself writes "I shall not attempt that task here" (of formulating them in detail). Without a principle of just acquisition, holdings are at best *conditionally* just; taint is not laundered by later sale (if A's funds were ill-gotten, C's receipt in an honest sale from A does not cleanse C's title).

**Nozick's own concessions, from the primary text** — Nozick is candid about the same gaps: the "complicated truth" about acquisition is referred to but "we shall not formulate here"; on rectification, "I do not know of a thorough or theoretically sophisticated treatment of such issues," and the principle offered is an idealization using "its best estimate of subjunctive information about what would have occurred… if the injustice had not taken place." The entry records the debate as its sources leave it: the entitlement framework is fully stated, its two load-bearing principles are sketched (labour-mixing plus proviso; voluntary transfer) but not completed.

## Thought experiments
- **Wilt Chamberlain** (Nozick, ch. 7). One million fans, 25 cents each, a special box with his name on it at every home game: $250,000 from unimpeachably voluntary steps out of an admittedly just start. Meant to show that liberty upsets patterns — any patterned theory must either forbid the fans' choices or keep confiscating the result. O'Neill's counter-pressure: the example works only if the fans' entitlement under *D*1 already included unlimited rights of transfer and accumulation — which is the question at issue.
- **The only water hole in the desert** (Nozick, ch. 7, on the proviso). Appropriating the sole supply of something necessary for life, or ending up owning it through a chain of innocent purchases, violates the Lockean proviso and places "stringent limits on what he may do with (what it is difficult any longer unreservedly to call) 'his property.'" Meant to show the entitlement theory has internal limits on acquisition — it is not a licence for any first grab.

## Where the debate stands
On the evidence of these texts, the argument is genuinely joined and unsettled. Nozick establishes a *conditional*: if holdings arise by just acquisition and voluntary transfer, their pattern is irrelevant to their justice — and he shows that every patterned rival must restrict transfers or repeatedly override them. O'Neill establishes the complementary conditional: the Wilt Chamberlain argument bites only against someone who already grants full capitalist property rights, and Nozick's own text leaves the principles of acquisition and rectification — the places where those rights would have to be established — explicitly unformulated. Rawls's side, in the form this corpus actually holds (the 1958 paper), justifies inequalities institutionally — by expected advantage for everyone and open positions — rather than by historical title; the head-on collision between the two finished theories (entitlement vs the difference principle) is visible in this corpus only through Nozick's presentation of Rawls, and is recorded here as such.
