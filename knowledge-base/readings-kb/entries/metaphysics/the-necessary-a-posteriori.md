# The Necessary A Posteriori
*Subfield: Metaphysics · Also known as: a posteriori necessity; Kripke's necessary a posteriori statements*

## The question
Before Kripke, the map of modal and epistemic categories was assumed to align: necessary truths are knowable a priori, contingent truths a posteriori. Kripke split the categories: some truths — "Hesperus is Phosphorus", "water is H₂O" — are necessary (true in every possible world) yet knowable only through experience. Is the category real? And — the question book 013's own editors press — how far does it extend beyond Kripke's original cases before its use becomes, in their word, abuse?

## The 90-second version
Take two rigid designators that in fact pick out the same object. Since each picks out that object in every possible world, a true identity between them is true in every possible world — it is necessary. Hesperus and Phosphorus were ancient names for the evening and morning appearances of Venus; astronomers *discovered* empirically that they are one body. So "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary, yet only experience could reveal it: a necessary a posteriori truth. Lois Lane needs empirical evidence to learn that Superman is Clark Kent, although — both names being rigid — the identity, if true, is necessarily true. Kripke argues the mirror category also exists: contingent a priori statements (the standard metre stick, stipulated to define the metre, is known without measurement to be one metre long, although it could have been a different length). Together the two categories break the assumed alignment of the necessary with the a priori. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Kripke's Necessary, A Posteriori Statements](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CisaX71OD9s), [Necessary and Contingent Distinction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vt4h7uvvcXc), [Kripke's Contingent, A Priori Statements](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=enIsXrWAqvc).)

## Positions

### Kripke's category (as the volume presents it)
For theoretical identities, the editors' favoured reading of Kripke runs as follows. In "water is H₂O" and "gold is the element with atomic number 79": the left-hand term is analogous to a proper name — (i) non-descriptive, (ii) rigid, (iii) introduced by baptism; the right-hand side is a definite description, used attributively, which is (i) *de facto* rigid and (ii) gives the *essence* of the kind, discovered and described by science. The identity is then necessary (nothing lacking that atomic structure could be water — "just as there is a fool's gold there could be a fool's water") and knowable only a posteriori.

**Canonical argument** (source: Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary, "On the Abuse of the Necessary *A Posteriori*", book 013 ch. 9, §2 — the editors' reconstruction of Kripke; labelled formalization):
- P1. "Water" is rigid and non-descriptive, like a name; "H₂O" / "the element with atomic number 79" states the underlying nature of the kind the name picks out.
- P2. Twin Earth–style judgement: a substance with a completely different atomic structure that merely resembled water in feel, appearance, and taste would not be water.
- ∴ C. Water's underlying nature is its essence; "water is H₂O" is metaphysically necessary — and, since the nature was found empirically, a posteriori.

### Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary: the category is real but its extension must be argued, case by case
The editors do not attack the category; they attack its unargued extension. "Even if we accept that Kripke's story holds for proper names and natural kind terms, it can by no means be taken for granted that the story extends to cover other cases."

**Canonical argument** (source: Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary, book 013 ch. 9, §§1–2):
- P1. A claim that some truth is necessary a posteriori requires a *Necessity* component: an argument that the underlying nature in question is the kind's *essence* — which, for kind terms, means running the Twin Earth–style test, since "water" might instead have meant something like "whatever potable liquid is typically to be found in rivers and lakes and falls from the sky", in which case "water is H₂O" would be contingent.
- P2. It also requires an *A Posteriority* component: the term must not have been introduced by stipulative definition (as "bachelor" is defined as an unmarried man), or the truth would be a priori after all.
- P3. Brian Ellis's scientific essentialism claims, for a vastly extended range of kinds — substance kinds and process kinds well beyond molecular constitution and species — that the laws of nature are metaphysically necessary but knowable only a posteriori; but clear cases among Ellis's "natural kinds" fail to fit Kripke's model, and no argument of the P1–P2 form is supplied for them.
- ∴ C. Ellis's extension is an *abuse* of the necessary a posteriori; and the failure of the alleged a posteriori necessities undermines his own account of de re necessity — his scientific essentialism loses the modal foundation it claimed.

### Noonan: the category, for kind terms, is a conflation
Harold Noonan's Fregean chapter reaches the more radical verdict that the necessary a posteriori *in the case of natural kinds* "stems from the conflation of the notion of an essential property, and an a posteriori but only contingently true thought" (editors' summary of book 013 ch. 5). Where the editors demand more argument, Noonan denies the phenomenon: what is genuinely a posteriori in the theoretical-identity cases is, on his view, contingent, and Kripkean metaphysical necessity of the kind-term sort belongs, in the editors' phrase for his conclusion, "on Hume's bonfire".

**Objections (to Noonan, from the volume's other chapters)** — Bird's and Hendry's chapters supply the material for a reply without addressing Noonan directly: essential truths that are not identities at all, and identities between scientific terms introduced before the relevant discoveries, look a posteriori in a way that does not obviously trade on any conflation; and chemical explanatory practice holds composition fixed across counternomic scenarios whether or not one endorses Noonan's Fregean semantics. The volume states no verdict between ch. 5 and chs. 7–9.

## Thought experiments
- **Hesperus and Phosphorus** (Kripke; lecture). Two rigid names, one planet, an astronomical discovery. Meant to show necessity and a posteriority can coincide. Pressures the pre-Kripke alignment of modal and epistemic categories.
- **Fool's water** (Kripke, via ch. 9). A substance resembling water in every observable respect but with a completely different atomic structure. The judgement "not water" is what converts "water has structure H₂O" into "H₂O is water's essence" — and the editors' point is that this judgement must actually be elicited and defended for each new alleged case, not assumed.
- **The standard metre** (Kripke; lecture on the contingent a priori). The stick that defines the metre is known a priori to be a metre long at the moment of stipulation, though its length is contingent. The mirror-image case; it matters here because Beebee & Sabbarton-Leary's A Posteriority component excludes exactly this route — a term introduced by stipulative definition cannot generate a necessary *a posteriori* truth.

## Where the debate stands
Within this volume: the existence of *some* necessary a posteriori truths (identity statements between rigid designators; the paradigmatic theoretical identities) is treated by the editors as Kripke's achievement, "relatively uncontroversial" as an interpretation of his view of names. Everything beyond the paradigms is contested. The editors' methodological rule — no extension without a Necessity argument and an A Posteriority argument — is the volume's own contribution, and their worked verdict is negative for Ellis's scientific essentialism. Noonan's stronger rejection stands in the same volume unanswered by name. LaPorte's stipulation argument (see Connections) is a further internal pressure: if identities are partly *decided*, their epistemic status needs redescription case by case.
