# Communicative Competence and Pragmatic Failure
*Subfield: Philosophy of Language · Also known as: knowing how to use a language; interlanguage pragmatics*

## The question
What does someone know when they know a language? The narrow answer — the grammar: the rules that make sentences well-formed — leaves out something every language learner discovers painfully: you can produce a flawless sentence and still fail. You can curse correctly and have the curse fall flat because you smiled; you can answer a question accurately and give offence because the form was wrong for the relationship; you can pass the grammar test and have nothing to say. The wider answer developed in applied linguistics is *communicative competence*: grammatical knowledge plus knowledge of how to do things with words, appropriately, in context. This entry states the rival answers as the corpus gives them — the narrow one, the accuracy-first policy built on it, the expanded competence that replaced it, and the fine-grained account of how utterances succeed and fail pragmatically — and uses the speech-act lectures as its front door, because the expanded answer is unintelligible without the distinction those lectures draw between what words literally say, what a speaker is doing in saying them, and what actually happens as a result.

## The 90-second version
Every utterance works on three levels at once. The *locution* is the literal meaning of the words: "The library closes in 15 minutes" states a fact about closing time. The *illocution* is what the speaker is doing in saying it — the force invested in it: the librarian is asking everyone to pack up and leave, just as "That tiger looks angry" can be a warning, and "It's getting hot in here" can be a request, a warning or a peace offering depending on context. The *perlocution* is the actual effect: the patrons gather their books and go. Illocution can be made explicit — add "so please leave" and no competent hearer can miss it — but perlocution cannot be guaranteed by any wording: you can understand perfectly that you are being asked to be quiet and still not quieten down, and effects can misfire entirely, landing as irritation where persuasion was intended. Knowing a language, on the view this entry tracks, is knowing all three levels — and pragmatic failure is what happens when the grammar is right and one of the other levels goes wrong. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [What is Locution?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MEFgY0LHM5w), [What is Illocution?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ciRkQU2WHsM), [What is Perlocution?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5dvyRtiP-N4).)

## Positions

### The narrow target: grammatical competence (and competence vs performance)
On the view Nattinger sets up as his foil, the subject matter of language teaching is the abstract system — the competence of an idealized speaker-hearer — and success is formal correctness within it.

**Canonical argument** (source: Nattinger, *Second Language Acquisition / Foreign Language Learning*, ch. 13 — a formalization of his opening case against competence-based grammars):
- P1. The goal of language teaching is that students comprehend and produce language successfully and meaningfully.
- P2. Conventional grammars offer only definitions and structure lists, social prescriptions of "correct" form, or descriptions of the abstract competence system; and "the precise relationship between the theoretical competence of an idealized speaker-hearer and the actual language performance of a specific human being is a highly tenuous matter."
- ∴ C. Limiting teaching to the underlying system is no guarantee of the goal; teachers should look to theories of language *use* — performance grammars — instead.

### Accuracy first: the fossilization warning (Higgs & Clifford, as Savignon presents and contests them)
The policy form of the narrow view: postpone free communication, correct errors early and intensely, or learners' inaccuracies harden permanently — "fossilization," the "terminal 2" profile.

**The claim as presented** (source: Savignon, ch. 12, presenting Higgs & Clifford 1982): communicative competence has been misread as "communication in spite of language, rather than communication through language"; grammatical precision has been downplayed; and the terminal profiles are explained by proactive interference plus fossilized development, found in learners whose programmes either lacked grammatically masterful instructors or chose, "for philosophical, methodological, or personal reasons," not to correct mistakes.

**Objections** — Savignon's chapter is largely a demolition of the claim's evidential base: "there is no research evidence to support the purported link between teacher correction practice and formal features of learner language," and the seminar audit she reports sorts Higgs & Clifford's support into "anecdotal opinion and hearsay, citation and quotation, and formal data" — finding the first two doing the work ("'evidence suggests', and 'evidence abounds'"). The same chapter makes the parallel objection to the ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines (1986): a hierarchy of performance levels promoted as "not based on a particular linguistic theory or pedagogical method" cannot be theory-free while prescribing methods, and its view of interaction is, in Kramsch's quoted verdict, neo-behaviouristic — behavioural functions over notional development, static content, "accuracy to the detriment of discourse aptitude." **Status:** the corpus contains this position only through its critic; it is recorded here as presented-and-contested, not as established.

### Expanded communicative competence (Savignon; Kramsch)
Competence is plural: knowing a language is knowing several distinguishable things, of which grammar is only one.

**Canonical argument** (source: Savignon, ch. 12, reporting the framework of Lapkin & Swain 1984; and Kramsch, ch. 3):
- P1. Learner ability in immersion and classroom settings is intelligible only in a broad frame: not only "sentence-level morphosyntactic features, i.e. grammatical competence, but … sociolinguistic, discourse and strategic competence" besides.
- P2. The components come apart empirically and pedagogically: the same programme is a "linguistic failure" judged by non-native morphosyntax (Hammerly) and "highly successful" judged by academic achievement *cum* functional competence (Stern) — so the verdict on learning depends on which competence is measured; and Savignon's 1971 Illinois study found adults given the opportunity to *use* French for communication from the very beginning developed communicative ability that repetition-bound learners did not.
- P3. (Kramsch) A research programme adequate to classrooms must treat language whole — not split into separate skills — as "quintessentially indeterminate and culturally relative," communication being "the negotiation of meanings intended and interpreted within an interactional context."
- ∴ C. What it is to know a language, and therefore what teaching and testing should target, is communicative competence in the expanded sense, not grammatical competence alone.

**Objections** — from inside the volume: the expanded notion is exactly what the accuracy-first camp (above) charges with vagueness and with licensing error; and Savignon herself notes the measurement problem on the other side — there is "lack of agreement on what constitutes learning 'success'," and programme-fair evaluation can be gamed by the choice of test (Beretta's Bangalore quandary: a conventional grammar test on communicatively taught beginners "would be perverse"). The expansion solves the narrowness problem at the price of a standing dispute about what counts as evidence for the wider competence.

### Pragmatic competence and its failures (Wildner-Bassett; Thomas as she presents him)
The finest-grained position in the book: using a language means managing conversation under sociocultural conventions, and there are two different ways to get it wrong.

**Canonical argument** (source: Wildner-Bassett, ch. 9, using Thomas 1983/1984 as presented there):
- P1. A learner faces conventions for managing conversation in the target culture that may conflict with the conventions of their own; failures divide into *sociopragmatic* failure (misjudging the social conditions — what this situation, relationship or setting calls for) and *pragmalinguistic* failure (mapping the intended force onto the wrong linguistic form).
- P2. What an utterance is doing is fixed not by the sentence alone but by the *discourse world* dominantly operant — a discourse world being the set of elements (intended recipient, shared presuppositions, intended speaker meaning or *illocutionary value*, interactional function) within which a unit of talk acquires its negotiated communicative value — and several worlds (role-play, real life, native-speaker–learner, teacher–student) coexist in a single exchange, with different situational frames (participants and their power relations, setting, purpose, restrictions, even facial expression).
- ∴ C. No interpretation of a learner's pragmatic performance from a single world or frame is valid; pragmatic competence is the developing ability to operate across coexisting worlds, and it must be taught — routine formulas explicitly, and metapragmatic awareness of the worlds themselves.

### The classroom as a communicative world of its own (Brooks; Green & Weade as he presents them)
Zoom in further: the place where competence is supposedly built is itself a rule-governed communicative environment whose rules are learned tacitly, and can hijack the lesson.

**Canonical argument** (source: Brooks, ch. 10, and its Appendix 10.1 propositions):
- P1. Classrooms are communicative environments in which meaning is context-specific, participation rules are implicit and constructed over time, and "communicative competence is reflected in appropriate behaviour" — so students simultaneously learn the academic content and how to conduct themselves as members of this classroom.
- P2. In the observed lesson, two students assigned a communicative simulation instead reproduced the teacher's patterns — repeat-the-word correction and *muy bien* evaluation/topic-shift signals, the initiation–response–feedback rhythm — converting communication practice into grammar-quiz preparation, having acquired the patterns by imitation and inference rather than instruction (the stronger student had not noticed doing so until he watched himself on video).
- ∴ C. What learners practise in a classroom is never only the target language's pragmatics; it is also the classroom's — and an account of competence that ignores the learning environment's own discourse world will misread both.

### Knowing as chunks first: prefabricated speech (Nattinger)
A developmental correction to every position above: whatever competence finally consists in, learners do not start from rules.

**Canonical argument** (source: Nattinger, ch. 13):
- P1. Children — and, on the adult evidence available, adults — begin with prefabricated chunks: unvarying *routines* and *patterns* with a movable slot, stored and retrieved whole to express functions they cannot yet construct from rules (Wong-Fillmore: the strategy of acquiring formulaic speech is "central to the learning of language").
- P2. Chunks are then segmented — compared across contexts, split into pieces (Brown's *korewa* audibly "cracking" into *kore* + *wa* once *kore o* is heard) — and the pieces become the raw material from which the creative rule system is built; item-learning precedes system-learning at every level.
- ∴ C. Creative, rule-governed competence grows *out of* unanalysed wholes rather than preceding them, so a curriculum should run routines → patterns with widening variation → creative use, grouped by function (Social Interactions, Necessary Topics, Discourse Devices).

### Translation competence is communicative competence (Kiraly)
The same dispute replayed in translator training, where the narrow view has a name and a canonical statement.

**Canonical argument** (source: Kiraly, ch. 14):
- P1. The traditional paradigm defines translation as Catford (1965) does — "the replacement of textual material in one language by equivalent material in another language" — and trains accordingly: learn the equivalents, produce them spontaneously, be corrected against the instructor's definitive solutions. Its lived result is Röhl's silent day: a great deal of "learning" and virtually no use of the language for self-expression or communication.
- P2. On Firth's theory, linguistic behaviour is "a network of relations between people, things, and events," and every utterance belongs to a context of situation (participants, verbal and non-verbal action, relevant objects and events, effects of the verbal action); Krashen's distinction then separates the *learner*, who consciously identifies elements and equivalence relations inside subsystems, from the *acquirer*, who uses language inside real contexts of situation.
- ∴ C. Since the translator is receiver of the source text and co-creator of the target text across two contexts of situation, translation competence is a special type of communicative competence — trainees must acquire as well as learn, and whether translators should "translate texts or words, messages or linguistic signs" is decided by which model of competence their training presupposes.

## Thought experiments
- **"This bloody England" — and the smile** (Wildner-Bassett, ch. 9, Extract 3). In role-play, a learner answers a native speaker's parody tirade with a correctly formed, forceful curse. Lexically and grammatically it succeeds; it still fails, because he smiles as he says it — the concomitant activity belongs to the real-life co-worker world, not the hostile role-play world, and it redefines the utterance toward humour, cancelling its pragmatic force. Wildner-Bassett pairs it with Harder's **reduced personality**: a foreigner "is not permitted to go beyond a certain limited repertoire; if he starts swearing fluently … he is unlikely to achieve the conventional communicative effect," and this learner seems to know it — he cannot take his own utterance seriously. The case isolates exactly what grammatical competence leaves out.
- **The pig's cold** (Wildner-Bassett, ch. 9, Extract 4). A learner responds to "I have a little cold" with *Schweine kalt* — reaching for the colloquial routine *Saukalt* ("sow-cold," a comment on bitter weather) and producing a remark about the other person's health. A pure pragmalinguistic failure: the social judgement was fine, the form-to-force mapping was wrong, the native speaker saved the exchange by changing the topic, and there is no evidence the learner noticed anything had happened.
- **The cat for 24 hours** (Bacon, ch. 11). In oral interviews, the highest-rated speakers chose unstructured topics and circumlocuted around missing vocabulary to keep developing them (the would-be cat who loves eating from the table), while the weakest clung to structured topics and abandoned them at the first missing word ("darn! Next card"). Bacon's gloss: the able treated the interview as communication, the weak as a test — evidence, against the ACTFL presumption, that measured proficiency is not context-free, because what is being measured includes the willingness and ability to *do* things with incomplete language.

## Where the debate stands
The expansion won the book's argument but not a settled metric. Every chapter in this cluster presupposes that grammatical competence is not the whole of knowing a language, and the volume's editors ratify the point by making language use the one domain where context distinctions genuinely matter. What remains open in these texts is threefold. First, evidence: the accuracy-first objection survives in weakened form — Savignon shows its flagship claim (uncorrected communication fossilizes) is undocumented, but the positive construct validity of communicative testing is itself contested in her own chapter. Second, analysis: Wildner-Bassett's discourse-worlds machinery shows single-frame interpretations are invalid, without supplying a replacement decision procedure — interpretation must be multi-level, and that is a discipline, not an algorithm. Third, the locution problem the lecture series itself raises: the speech-act front door assumes a literal level (locution) can be isolated from force and context, and the Locution lecture ends by doubting exactly that — even literal meaning may need speaker intention and context to fix it, which would blur the triad the 90-second version relies on. The corpus leaves that doubt standing.
