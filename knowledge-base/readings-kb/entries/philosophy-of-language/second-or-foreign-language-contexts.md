# Second or Foreign? The Classification of Language-Learning Contexts
*Subfield: Philosophy of Language · Also known as: the SLA/FLL distinction; the foreign–second dichotomy*

## The question
When someone learns a language that is not their first, we sort the situation into kinds: the language is a *second* language if it is learned where it is spoken, a *foreign* language if it is learned in a classroom far from its speakers. The sorting looks like a mere labelling convenience, but a surprising amount turns on it — what counts as evidence for a theory of acquisition, what a curriculum should imitate, what outcome a learner can be promised. So: does the distinction carve language learning at a real joint, is it a smooth continuum misdescribed as a dichotomy, or does it dissolve once you ask *for what purpose* the classification is being made? The one book in this corpus that takes the question head-on — VanPatten & Lee's *Second Language Acquisition / Foreign Language Learning* (book 031, mislabelled in the corpus as "Language Matters") — is a whole volume organized around it, and its contributors do not agree.

## The 90-second version
*[Textbook-distilled — no Carneades lecture covers this problem; distilled from Berns, ch. 1, and VanPatten & Lee, ch. 17, of book 031.]* Start with the textbook definition: a language is "second" when it is learned with reference to a speech community inside your own country, "foreign" when the community is outside it. Now test it on real cases. English in Japan is learned almost entirely at school, for exams and cultivated status, with few chances to use it — clearly "foreign." English in India is a medium of instruction, law and administration, used daily between Indians — clearly "second." But English in West Germany, learned at school yet heard in the media, used on holiday and at work across Europe, sits in between: forcing it into either box loses exactly what is distinctive about it. The fix proposed in the book is to replace the two boxes with a line — a cline with "foreign" at one end and "second" at the other, Germany near the middle — and to add a third label, "international," for the common case of two non-native speakers using English with each other, which belongs to neither national community. The volume's editors end somewhere more radical still: whether the distinction matters depends on what you are studying — the social context, the mental processes, or the eventual product — and for the mental processes themselves, it may not matter at all.

## Positions

### The dichotomy (Stern's definition, as Berns presents it)
Contexts come in two kinds, fixed by geography: where the reference speech community sits relative to the learner's national or territorial boundaries.

**Canonical argument** (source: Berns, *Second Language Acquisition / Foreign Language Learning*, ch. 1, presenting Stern 1983 — a formalization of the definition Berns takes as her starting point):
- P1. A learned language is used with reference to some speech community whose norms the learner approximates.
- P2. That community is either inside or outside the national or territorial boundaries within which the learning takes place.
- ∴ C. Every learning context is therefore either a second-language context (community inside) or a foreign-language context (community outside).

**Objections** — Berns's own profiles are the objection: the definition classifies Japan and India cleanly but leaves West Germany oscillating between the boxes (see Thought experiments), and it has no slot at all for English used between non-native speakers across borders. A classification that cannot place one of its three parade cases, and cannot state one of the commonest uses, is not false so much as too coarse for the work asked of it.

### Berns: replace the dichotomy with a cline — and add "international"
The geography is real, but it comes in degrees, because what matters is the pattern of users, uses, motivations and attitudes in a *sociolinguistic profile*, and profiles shade into one another.

**Canonical argument** (source: Berns, ch. 1):
- P1. The Japan, West Germany and India profiles differ continuously in opportunity for use, domains covered, and contact with users of English inside the country — not by a single inside/outside switch.
- P2. A two-box scheme forces the German case to be described as either strictly foreign (false to its in-country contact and interpersonal uses) or strictly second (false to its lack of institutional and literary roles).
- P3. One widespread use — English between non-native speakers for business, trade and commerce — belongs to an international speech community that is neither the learner's national community nor a foreign native one.
- ∴ C. Contexts are better placed on a cline of language status (Japan near the "foreign" pole, India near the "second" pole, West Germany near the midpoint), supplemented by the category "international."

**Replies and limits** — Berns adds the learner's perspective as a corrective inside her own proposal: the terms are "meaningful and useful only to researchers and other 'outside' observers," while from the learner's side purposes vary individual by individual within a single community, so even a cline describes contexts, not learners. The editors' Introduction presses the point with Spanish in the United States, which reproduces all three of Berns's English profiles within one country (see Thought experiments).

### Gass: the same/different question dissolves — ask after extent and nature instead
Look inside the learner, and the processes are the same; look outside, at what information the environment supplies, and they differ — in degree, and only sometimes in kind.

**Canonical argument** (source: Gass, ch. 4):
- P1. The psycholinguistic task is context-independent: all learners take in input, organize it within their current system, generalize and misgeneralize — classroom-induced errors (Stenson's *any*-examples) are the same generalizing process with different instantiations, and Krashen's learning/acquisition contrast itself occurs both in and out of classrooms.
- P2. What the context changes is the availability of relevant information: negative evidence (direct correction is a classroom staple, indirect cues the outside norm, and indirect cues may be unusable across cultures), the range and control of input, and some interpretation strategies that appear learnable only from ambient speech.
- P3. Even the apparent product difference — completeness — may be one of degree: on Coppieters' evidence, apparently native-like speakers' intuitions still diverge from natives', so "native-like competence is only possible in one language" may hold for second-language learners generally, not just classroom ones.
- ∴ C. SLA and FLL show both quantitative and qualitative differences amid numerous similarities; the research agenda should not be whether they are the same or different — "the answer is 'yes'" — but the extent and nature of the similarities and differences.

### Schinke-Llano: define the pair by product, and test it on immersion
A different cut entirely: stop defining the terms by where learning happens and define them by what comes out.

**Canonical argument** (source: Schinke-Llano, ch. 15 — the definition is stated as an operating definition, and the argument is a formalization of her immersion comparison):
- P1. Let "SLA" mean learning that results in native-like use of the target language, and "FLL" learning that does not (operating definitions).
- P2. Immersion schooling — target language as medium of instruction — supplies the facilitative conditions the SLA literature identifies (acquisition rather than formal learning, abundant comprehensible input, negotiated interaction, comprehensible output, a silent period, a low affective filter) far more fully than FLEX or FLES courses, and immersion pupils outperform their non-immersion peers.
- P3. Yet immersion pupils still do not reach native-like speaking and writing, needing six to seven years even for native-like listening and reading.
- ∴ C. SLA-like environments can be approximated in a classroom, and the product moves toward SLA — but approximation is not identity, so on the product definition classroom learning remains FLL-like to a degree.

**Objections** — Genesee, quoted by Schinke-Llano herself: it is "unlikely that any student will attain totally native-like competence in a language if language learning and use are restricted to the school environment," because school offers only a limited range of the functions the speech community demands. Schinke-Llano accepts the fact and rejects the defeatism: the right response is not to declare the goal unattainable but to use classroom-interaction research to close the gap.

### The editors' synthesis: the distinction is purpose-relative (contexts / processes / products)
The volume's own conclusion refuses a single verdict and sorts the question instead.

**Canonical argument** (source: VanPatten & Lee, ch. 17):
- P1. For *context* — the social situations of use, which shape the quality and quantity of input and output — the SLA/FLL distinction is important.
- P2. For *processes*, it splits: psycholinguistic processes (how a grammatical system is built from input) do not change from environment to environment, so the distinction is irrelevant there; psychosocial processes (how speakers act in speech events) do change with discourse context, so the distinction matters there — VanPatten's Spanish-clitic comparison and Eubank's German-negation analysis find only *superficial* (output) differences over shared *deep* processes, while Wildner-Bassett's differences of "pragmatic impact" are genuinely important for use.
- P3. For *products* — eventual proficiency, and how present L1 influence is in the outcome — the distinction is useful, though the influence process itself does not change, only its degree in the product.
- ∴ C. FLL is neither contained in SLA nor contains it; the two are overlapping circles, and whether the distinction should be drawn at all "is a function of one's own purpose."

## Thought experiments
- **West Germany as the midpoint case** (Berns, ch. 1). English there is a school subject (foreign-like) but also a media presence, a source of borrowings, and a working *lingua franca* with other Europeans at every social level (second-like) — while never becoming a medium of instruction, law or literature (unlike India). Any adequate classification must say *in-between* here without contradiction; the dichotomy cannot, the cline can. It is the case that does the argumentative work of the whole chapter.
- **Spanish in the United States** (VanPatten & Lee, Introduction). One language, one country, three profiles: Spanish in Alaska behaves like English in Japan, in Los Angeles and Dade County like English in India, in Chicago, New York and Dallas like English in West Germany. And the editors add the twist Berns's learner perspective predicts: a learner in a Spanish-rich community may never seek outside input, and a learner in Alaska may seek it avidly — so community-level classification and learner-level reality can come apart in both directions.
- **Immersion as the limiting classroom** (Schinke-Llano, ch. 15). Build the classroom that does everything the acquisition literature asks — years of content teaching through the target language from kindergarten — and measure the product against natives. The persistent gap in speaking and writing is the volume's best evidence that "classroom vs community" tracks something real about outcomes even where processes are shared.

## Where the debate stands
Inside this corpus, unsettled — by the editors' own admission. Chapter 17 opens its terminology discussion with the verdict that on what "second language acquisition" and "foreign language learning" mean there is "quite simply" no consensus among the volume's contributors: some subsume FLL under SLA, some restrict SLA so it does not encompass FLL, some keep the distinction only for use, some ignore it. What the volume does establish is a shared negative point — a bare same/different verdict is the wrong output — and three candidate replacements that do not coincide: a continuum of contexts (Berns), an extent-and-nature research programme (Gass), and a purpose-relative sorting (the editors), with Schinke-Llano's product definition cutting across all three. Nothing in the corpus decides among them, and the corpus's one attempt at a neighbouring classification problem — Kramsch's (ch. 3) insistence that FLL research is neither SLA nor FL education — shows the same pattern one level up: the boundaries of the *fields* are as contested as the boundaries of the *contexts*.
