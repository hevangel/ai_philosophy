# The Future of Philosophy of Religion
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: the scope and method of the field; philosophy of religion vs. religious studies*

## The question
What should philosophy of religion be *about*? The traditional answer — at least in its analytic form — is a small set of questions about a small set of doctrines: does God exist, is belief in God justified, how is evil compatible with God, studied as propositions to be assessed, usually drawn from one tradition (Christianity) or one family (theisms). Book 020 — a whole volume of practitioners reflecting on their field's future — argues, from several directions at once, that this answer is too narrow in its object (beliefs rather than practices), its population (literate elites rather than ordinary practitioners), its map (the North Atlantic theisms rather than the world's religions), and its self-awareness (a discipline that does not examine its own categories, including "religion" and "belief" themselves). This entry states the critique and the main redesign proposals as the volume presents them; it is the subfield's one *meta* entry — a problem about the field, included because book 020 is one of this slice's two source books and this is its central, bottom-up theme.

## The 90-second version
First, separate two things that share a name. *Religion* makes claims about the world — about gods, life, how we should live. *Philosophy of religion* does something else: it translates the wildly varying claims of religions into a common logical language, assesses their justifications, and puts them into dialogue with each other and with science and secular ethics — neutrally, the way it would assess any other claims. Now ask how well the field has been doing that job. One contributor's diagnosis: universities are being reshaped by four transformations — going *global*, going *critical*, going *multidisciplinary*, and going *practical* — and philosophy of religion, huddled around the beliefs of one tradition, has responded to all four slowly, while the jobs named for it disappear. The redesigns on offer share a direction of travel: from texts to *practices* (rituals, disciplines, the ways practices form the people who perform them), from elite writings to *lived* religion (the reasons ordinary people actually give, out loud, today), from justification-of-belief to the attitudes people really bring to worship — acceptance, hope, trust — and from "religion" and "race" as separate filing cabinets to *belief* understood as something material, embodied, and consequential in the world, including in America's history of anti-Black racism. (Distilled from the Carneades lecture [Religion vs Philosophy of Religion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=onex7xDHQ6k) and book 020, especially Wildman's and Eckel's framing chapters.)

## Positions

### The traditional field, as its critics describe it
The critics inside the volume describe a common target. Wildman's opening questions (book 020, ch. 2): "Why is philosophy of religion so rarely about *religion in all its complexity* … but rather typically focuses narrowly on worldview beliefs, often limited to a particular religious tradition (usually Christianity) or set of religious traditions (usually theisms)?" Why is it so distant from critical theory, so unresponsive to other disciplines (physics to cognitive neuroscience to political economy), and so absent from public discourse — and why, meanwhile, are "university positions specializing in and named for philosophy of religion *disappearing* in North Atlantic cultures"? Chignell (ch. 6) gives the internal version of the same portrait: forty years of flourishing analytic work narrowed the field twice over — to *belief* as the attitude that matters, and to *bare theism* as the content that matters — producing "scholastic stasis in which familiar problems are hashed out in ever finer detail, but genuinely new directions are difficult to find." Eckel's Introduction concedes the frame from the editor's chair: the classic questions "seemed parochial," and new work grounds religious studies "more closely in the lives of actual practitioners."

### The four transformations (Wesley Wildman)
**Thesis** (source: Wildman, book 020, ch. 2 — stated as a diagnosis, in his terms):
- Four transformations are impacting all universities in North Atlantic cultures and beyond: the **global** transformation (the study of religion can no longer be one tradition's self-study), the **critical** transformation (critical theories of power, race, gender, and suspicion are ascendant across the academy), the **multidisciplinary** transformation (relevant knowledge now lives in many disciplines), and the **practical** transformation (attention to religious practices and the solution of real-world problems).
- Philosophy of religion "has been slow to respond to the associated challenges," even though both its departmental homes — philosophy and academic religious studies — have proved more agile in some respects.
- The transformations supply *criteria* for estimating the field's future; recent field-wide empirical studies add specificity; and reversing "the trend of rapid contraction of academic positions in philosophy of religion" requires imagining — and doing — what those criteria demand.

The rest of the volume can be read as a set of responses to this exam, and Eckel's Introduction reads it that way: grouping the essays under the global perspective (the Jewish-philosophy chapters; Bhāviveka), the challenge of critical theory (Pritchard; Dole; Larrimore; Decosimo), multiple disciplinary perspectives (Speight; Reinhard), and religious practice (Schilbrack; Knepper; Chignell).

### From texts to practices: subjects are formed, not just informed (Kevin Schilbrack)
**Thesis** (source: Schilbrack, book 020, ch. 4): the academic study of religion shifted over the past century "from the study of religious texts to a broader vision that includes social practices," and philosophy of religion "ought to expand its traditional object of study to follow suit." The live theoretical question is what practices *do* to people — the *formation of subjects*. Three anthropological approaches are usually read as rivals: Clifford Geertz's hermeneutic approach (inspired by Ricoeur), Talal Asad's disciplinary approach (inspired by Foucault), and Thomas Csordas's embodiment approach (inspired by Merleau-Ponty). Schilbrack argues the disagreement among them about subjectivity is at bottom a *philosophical* one — hence an opportunity for philosophers of religion to contribute to the theorizing rather than merely consume it — and that, properly understood, the three approaches complement one another and can be integrated.

### Philosophy of lived religion (Timothy D. Knepper)
**Thesis** (source: Knepper, book 020, ch. 5): even a field succeeding at Wildman's global test has a second injustice outstanding. Religious *diversification* "is only half the battle: injustices of creed have been addressed while injustices of class remain." Philosophy of lived religion is "the philosophical study of all acts of religious reason-giving, privileging contemporary, ordinary, and spoken acts of religious reason-giving over past, elite, and written ones" — the philosophy "not only of all religious traditions but also of all religious subjectivities (insofar as possible)." Its organizing questions are the basic ones humans ask of anything — what, who, when, where, how, why — now addressed to religion as it is reasoned about in lived discourse and practice. Knepper is careful to distinguish his proposal from the nearest advance, Schilbrack's 2014 manifesto (which widens the field's object to religion's affective and conative dimensions — the ethical, political, ritual — alongside the cognitive): lived religion's unit is the *act of reason-giving* itself, wherever it occurs.

### The liturgical turn (Andrew Chignell)
Cross-reference: [Faith, Acceptance, and Hope](faith-acceptance-and-hope.md) states this position in full. In this entry's terms it is the redesign of the field's *centre*: set aside the debates over knowledge and justified belief, as an earlier generation set aside the debates over religious language, and study the attitudes actual practice involves — the liturgical stance, acceptance, hope, trust — and the thick, embodied doctrinal objects of liturgy rather than bare theism. Chignell's closing coinage for the redesigned practitioner: *pistologists* and *elpistologists*, students of faith (*pistis*) and hope (*elpis*).

### Material belief (Elizabeth Pritchard)
**Thesis** (source: Pritchard, book 020, ch. 7): the field's master category is itself part of the problem. Scholars who repudiate "belief" as an exclusively cognitive category — a reductionist distortion of religions — "take for granted that belief is nonmaterial." Pritchard argues that presumption does political work: it contributes to "the disaggregation of 'religion' and 'race,' the obfuscation of Christianity's relationship to racism and the perpetuation of American anti-black racism." Her positive case is for the *materiality* of belief — belief as embodied in practices, institutions, and their consequences — supported by recent work that portrays American anti-Black racism itself as a religion and by materialist interpretations of belief from a range of disciplines. For this entry the implication is methodological: a category the field treated as a neutral piece of philosophical psychology (a mental state of affirmation) turns out, on her account, to have a history and interests behind its dematerialization — the sort of reflexive finding the volume's critical transformation demands.

### Reflexivity about the categories themselves (Arnold; Hammerschlag; Speight; Dole)
A second redesign thread widens the *questions* rather than the object. **Arnold (ch. 3)**: against the descriptivist rule that scholars of religion must not judge, normativity is ineliminable from humanistic inquiry — intentionality itself is irreducibly normative, and freedom is our attunement to normative considerations, which cannot coherently be explained away; philosophy of religion's distinctive work is identifying and clarifying the normative considerations that "inexorably distinguish human activity as such." **Hammerschlag (ch. 12)**: answering Schilbrack's charge (quoted in her chapter) that philosophy of religion is "the least reflexive discipline in religious studies," she proposes — through modern Jewish thought's encounters with philosophy — that the field be reconceived "less as a discipline focused on the validity of concepts and more as one that investigates their historical emergence and evaluates their subsequent usage and dissemination." **Speight (ch. 14)** runs the same historicizing move on the master concept itself: "religion," like "art," may be an essentially modern concept with no ancient counterpart — which changes how the humanities should approach the long pre-histories of both. **Dole (ch. 8)** applies reflexivity to the critical tools themselves: academic *critique* (genealogy, the hermeneutics of suspicion) shares formal features with conspiracy theories — both are "suspicious explanation" — developing Latour's worry about "gullible critique." The remaining chapters extend the volume's range rather than its thesis: Larrimore (ch. 9) on philosophy of religion in the Anthropocene; Decosimo (ch. 10) against Dreyfus and Kelly's religious naturalism; Zank (ch. 11) on Jewish philosophy's fruitful marginality; Reinhard (ch. 15) on psychoanalysis and the monotheistic origins of modern science; Marion (ch. 17) as evidence, for Eckel, that "the tradition is alive and well" (see [The Ontological Argument](ontological-argument.md)).

## Thought experiments
None drives this problem in the usual sense; its nearest equivalent is the volume's own structure — a natural experiment Eckel's Introduction describes. The same cohort of philosophers of religion was asked "what is the future of this field?" twice, fifteen years apart (early-2000s symposium; reconvened 2017). The two answer-sets differ: the participants' interests had changed, and the 2017 essays are the data for what the field, left to its own practitioners, actually drifted toward — practice, lived religion, critical theory, global scope — versus what a business-as-usual forecast (Chignell's APA panel, where two of three distinguished panelists predicted and recommended continuity) said it would do.

## Where the debate stands
The volume is a symposium, not a settlement, and presents itself as one: the editors' hope is that the essays' "variety, the imagination, and the intellectual seriousness … speak well for the future of philosophy in the study of religion," alongside the admission that "important perspectives have been left out" (gender studies; fuller non-Western coverage). The contributors agree on the diagnosis (the traditional object is too narrow) more than on any single redesign: practice-formation, lived religion, the liturgical turn, and material belief are complementary directions, not rival manifestos, but no chapter argues for their unification, and two standing tensions are visible in the corpus itself. First, Chignell's business-as-usual panelists are not straw men *within* the volume's world: the classical questions retain distinguished defenders, and Marion's closing chapter is offered by the editor precisely as proof the traditional metaphysical tradition continues. Second, the field-level critique never shows that the classical problems are *bad* problems — Wildman's charge is unresponsiveness and contraction, not error. A reader should therefore not take this entry as reporting the death of the arguments treated elsewhere in this subfield; it reports a well-documented dispute about what *else* the field should be, staged entirely from inside the profession.
