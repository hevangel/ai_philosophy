# The Ontological Argument
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: ontological arguments (a family, not a single argument)*

## The question
Can the existence of God be proved by reason alone — not from anything observed in the world, but from the concept of God itself? That is the defining ambition of the ontological argument, and the first correction the sources insist on is that there is no single such argument: "a common misconception that there is only one ontological argument for the existence of God. In fact, an ontological argument is just a category of arguments," unified by the method — "an attempt to prove the existence of God solely through pure reason," in contrast to the cosmological argument's appeal to experience. The Carneades series, following the Stanford Encyclopedia's taxonomy, works through seven members of the family. This entry follows the lectures, because in this corpus the ontological argument lives in the lecture layer: the two books touch it only in passing (see "Where the debate stands").

## The 90-second version
Try to think of the greatest being you can possibly conceive. Now ask: would that being be greater if it existed in reality, and not only in your mind? Obviously yes — a real cake beats an imagined cake. So if the greatest conceivable being existed only in your mind, you could conceive a greater one — the same being, existing — which contradicts the idea that it was the greatest conceivable. Conclusion: the greatest conceivable being, God, exists. That is the family's most famous member, and every other member runs the same basic play with different machinery: define God so that existence is built into the definition (and existence is a perfection, so a perfect being has it); or note that God, if he is possible at all, cannot be a *contingent* being — he would have to exist necessarily, in every possible world, including this one. The objections press exactly where the play is run: can you really *conceive* this being, as opposed to listing its properties? Is "it is possible that God exists" doing all the work, so the argument merely unpacks its first premise — begs the question? And does being *truly describable* as existing amount to existing? (Distilled from the Carneades lectures: [Seven Ontological Arguments (for the Existence of God)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQybvNQxbMo), [Conceptual Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UXu--moRCoI), [Modal Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m_edcGIK9dw).)

## Positions

### The definitional argument
The simplest member of the family, and the template the others refine.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Ontological Argument From Definition](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GcIVSduNz64); the lecture's own formulation):
- P1. God is, by definition, a being who possesses every perfection.
- P2. Existence is a perfection. (A cake that exists is better than a cake that doesn't; something with all perfections but no existence would be worse than something that exists and has them.)
- ∴ C. God exists.

The lecture is frank that the argument's charm is its parallel with trivial definitional truths ("ice cream is by definition cold, therefore ice cream is cold") — which is also its exposure: everything turns on whether existence can be loaded into a definition this way and then read back out as a fact about the world. Its paired objections lecture ([Objections to the Definitional Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHnBvYO_F5g)) takes up that pressure point; this entry reports the argument as its source presents it.

### The conceptual argument (Anselm and Descartes, in the lecture's telling)
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Conceptual Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UXu--moRCoI); attributed there to both Saint Anselm and René Descartes):
- P1. I can think of a being higher than which it is impossible to imagine.
- P2. If such a being does not exist, then I can imagine a being higher than it — one that does exist (an existing perfect being is higher than a merely conceived one).
- P3. I cannot conceive of a being higher than that than which no higher can be conceived. (A taller building than the tallest building would simply *be* the tallest.)
- ∴ C. Such a being — God — exists.

**Objections** (source: [Objections to the Conceptual Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AvF8GCofoSQ)) — the flagship objection attacks P1's picture of conceiving. To conceive a thing, the lecturer argues, is to be able to form some picture of it: the invented creature in the lecture's example (six feet tall, scarf, pea coat, crime-solving) can be pictured; but a being that is "everywhere, all-powerful, all-good, all-knowing" is given only as a *list of properties*, with no answer to "what does it look like — what do I do in my head to imagine such a thing?" Listing properties is not conceiving, and even many theists, the objection continues, speak of wanting to understand God more deeply rather than claiming to conceive him. The objections lecture ranges over P2 and the inference as well; the conceivability objection is its lead and most developed line.

### The modal argument (Plantinga's version, in the lecture's telling)
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Modal Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m_edcGIK9dw) — a formalization of the lecture's disjunctive-syllogism presentation, labelled as such):
- P1. It is possible that God exists (God exists in some possible world).
- P2. God is not contingent: either it is impossible for him to exist, or he exists necessarily — necessary existence being one of his attributes, like omniscience and omnipotence.
- ∴ C. God exists — necessarily, in all possible worlds, and "since our world is a possible world, God must exist in our world as well."

**Objections** — this member draws the series' heaviest fire: the lecturer announces the modal argument is "one of the worst" and devotes a week of videos (36 objections, in the series' own count) to its versions. The deepest objection is that the argument **begs the question** ([Why the Modal Ontological Argument Begs the Question](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O135PuFm08c)): the argument's whole labour falls on its single substantive premise, and that premise "is actually equivalent to your conclusion" — every later step merely unpacks definitions (of possibility, of necessity, of actuality), so "when I say that it is possible that God, the greatest being possible, exists, I really only mean that God does exist." Accepting P1 is already granting the conclusion. The family of replies includes an **anti-modal ontological argument** ([Anti-Modal Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r92bK_S72zI)), constructed to run the same modal machinery to the opposite conclusion and thereby indict the form itself; further dedicated objections treat the modal perfection argument that underlies it ([Modal Perfection Argument (Objections)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VzBPN-gIGKg)) and popular restatements ([Debunking the Modal Ontological Argument Six Ways from Sunday](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kplijirg7ws); [Objections to Inspiring Philosophy's Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZZkx7yZrtRY)).

### Gödel's argument
The family's most technically elaborate member: three definitions (of a godlike object, of an *essential* property — a property that conditions all and only an object's necessary properties — and of necessary existence) and six axioms built on the undefined intuitive notion of a *positive property*. If the definitions and axioms are accepted, a theorem-chain yields that the property of being godlike is realized: something godlike necessarily exists. The lecture ([Godel's Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2iZzK_opOkg)) is explicit about its own division of labour — it reviews the axioms and definitions, deferring the logical derivation and the objections ([Objections to Godel's Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XfXAs66GW70)) — and remarks that the argument "is probably more interesting from a logical point of view than as an argument for the existence of God." Faithfulness requires the same restraint here: this corpus's lecture states the framework, not the proof.

### The Meinongian argument
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Meinongian Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iAz0rXxHMQM); presupposing Alexius Meinong's theory of objects):
- P1. All instances of the statement-form "the *F G* is *F*" are true (Meinong's characterization principle) — indeed analytically true.
- P2. "The existent perfect being is existent" is such an instance (F = existent, G = perfect being), so it is true.
- ∴ C. The existent perfect being exists; and a perfect being is what we call God, so God exists.

The lecture warns the argument cannot even be assessed without Meinong's theory of objects, and that its objections ([Objections to the Meinongian Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lD2eZ6iVGqU)) are equally steeped in that theory — the crux being the slide from "the statement is true" to "the being exists," which the lecture itself flags in the parallel: "'the sky is blue' is true, therefore the sky is blue."

### The mereological argument
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Mereological Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EmE1COo4q5c)):
- P1. I exist.
- P2. Therefore something exists (existential generalization).
- P3. Wherever something exists, the mereological sum of all things exists (mereology's composition principle: there is always a sum of any parts).
- ∴ C. God — *defined as* the sum of all things — exists.

The lecture presents the conclusion as resting on that identification: "God is just what we refer to as the sum of all things." The pressure point is correspondingly visible in the argument itself: everything depends on whether naming the totality "God" earns the theological conclusion, and the paired objections lecture ([Objections to the Mereological Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2VZOrsLNj0Y)) pursues it.

### The religious-experience argument
**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Religious Experience Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFvimAuI8MI)):
- P1. The word "God" has a meaning that is only revealed in religious experience.
- P2. The word "God" only has meaning if God exists (unlike "Santa Claus," a term we use precisely for something that doesn't exist).
- ∴ C. God exists.

The lecture supports P1/P2 with a thought experiment (see below) and is candid that on first encounter the argument "was not very intuitive and not very persuasive" — the thought experiment is offered as making it "a bit more persuasive, if not fully convincing." Its objections are treated in [Objections to the Religious Experience Ontological Argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uZeieIVY54s).

### A framing objection from the books: what does "exists" even mean for God? (Marion)
Book 020's closing chapter does not present an ontological argument; it questions the terms in which any of them is posed. Before deciding whether God is to be thought of with or without Being, Marion argues, one must ask what "Being" means: as *metaphysica* understands it, Being is perseverance in the present — beings existing according to *causa sui* — a sense that collapses into "the aporia of the present moment" and forgets manifestation, past, and future; but Being can also be understood by "the logic of the *gift* and the *event*," in which beings *are* by surrendering their claim to perseverance. The question "does not consist in the application of the predicate of Being to God, as if it were simply a matter of discussing his existence … without questioning for a moment that the verb, *to exist*, … could legitimately be applied in one way or another to God." Read against the lecture family, Marion's point is not a refutation of any member but a standing challenge to the shared presupposition that "existence" is a single, unproblematic predicate ready to be distributed by definition, conception, or modal logic.

## Thought experiments
- **The cake** (recurring across the definitional and conceptual lectures). A cake that exists is better than a cake that doesn't — you can eat it. The homely case is meant to make "existence is a perfection / an existing being is greater" feel obvious before the same move is made for God; the objections' work begins by asking whether what is obvious for cakes survives the transfer.
- **George in the cave** (Religious Experience Ontological Argument). A dark, narrow cave at the village edge admits one visitor at a time. Amy returns reporting a nice man named George who gave her tea; Bob confirms him (weather talk, tea); Karl confirms him again, down to the yellow flannel shirt and the Earl Grey. At some point, the lecture suggests, the villagers are entitled to think the word "George" has a *referent* — something all these experiences are of — and the argument asks why repeated, mutually confirming religious experience should not earn the word "God" the same standing. It is meant to move the argument from semantics to testimony: meaning anchored in convergent experience.

## Where the debate stands
Within this corpus the debate is one-sided in an instructive way: the lecture series presents each argument sympathetically and then subjects it to objections — most heavily the modal argument, which the lecturer judges question-begging in structure — and no corpus source offers a considered *defence* of any member against those objections. The books mention the family only in transit: Rowe (book 027, ch. 1) lists "Ontological, Cosmological, Teleological, Moral, etc." among the traditional arguments a theist might offer as grounds in the Moorean shift, without assessing them; Kant's objection to the *cosmological* argument (see [The Cosmological Argument](cosmological-argument.md)) holds that the cosmological argument covertly depends on this one — God would be logically necessary only if the ontological argument already worked — so the family's fortunes propagate. The honest summary is the lectures' own: seven distinct attempts to get existence out of the concept of God alone, each with a live, identified pressure point (definition-loading, conceivability, question-begging, truth-to-existence slides, totality-naming), and the whole family additionally shadowed by Marion's question whether "Being" means one thing when it is a question of God. Nothing here shows an ontological argument succeeding; nothing here shows the family refuted once for all, either — objections are argued, not conceded by the other side, because the other side is not in the room.

## Connections
- [The Cosmological Argument](cosmological-argument.md) — the complementary family (from experience and causation rather than pure reason), and, on Kant's objection as the lectures present it, a dependent one: its "necessary being" conclusion borrows necessity from the ontological argument.
- [The Evidential Argument from Evil](evidential-argument-from-evil.md) / [The Logical Problem of Evil](logical-problem-of-evil.md) — the arguments *against* God's existence that give these arguments *for* it their dialectical role in book 027: a theist's grounds for the Moorean shift.
- [Faith, Acceptance, and Hope](faith-acceptance-and-hope.md) — if existence cannot be extracted from the concept, the remaining question is what attitude toward "God exists" is rational without such a proof.
- The desire argument (Carneades, [The Desire Argument for the Existence of God](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bsbo3Ah2Qew)) — a neighbouring, *abductive* existence argument (C. S. Lewis's form: a desire for perfect happiness unsatisfiable in this world is best explained by another world, and that by God) — not ontological, since it starts from an experienced desire rather than the concept of God; the lecturer counts it among the least convincing arguments precisely because abduction is run twice.
