# Faith, Acceptance, and Hope
*Subfield: Philosophy of Religion · Also known as: faith and reason; the attitudes of religious commitment*

## The question
Philosophy of religion's default picture of religious commitment is *belief*: a person assents to the truth of propositions ("God exists," "the dead are raised") and the philosophical question is whether that belief is justified. But is belief the attitude religious people actually have toward most of their doctrines — and is it the only attitude that matters? The sources in this corpus converge, from very different directions, on a more complicated inventory: belief itself splits from *faith*; faith splits into several rival models; *acceptance* and *hope* emerge as distinct, voluntary, practically grounded attitudes that can structure a religious life without belief; and two decision-procedures — Pascal's wager and James's pragmatism — propose that where evidence runs out, practical reason may legitimately take over. The prior question underneath all of them: who bears the burden of proof in the first place?

## The 90-second version
Start with the difference between *believing* something and *having faith* in it. Belief is plain: you take a proposition to be true. Faith, on one common model, is belief that outruns its evidence — a venture. But watch what people in a liturgy are actually doing. Many of them are reciting doctrines they would not say, flatly, that they *believe* — and they are not simply pretending, either. They are *accepting* the doctrines for the purposes of the practice: taking them on board voluntarily, letting them guide what they say and do, in order to be part of the community and — perhaps — to grow toward belief. Others are *hoping*: Kant thought the minimum of religion might be just the hope that God exists, plus the admission that God is genuinely possible. Add Pascal's famous bet — believing costs you little if you're wrong and gains you everything if you're right — and James's idea that when the evidence genuinely cannot decide, our passional nature lawfully may. The old question "is religious belief rational?" turns out to be several questions wearing one coat. (Distilled from the Carneades lectures [Belief vs Faith](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o8QKkHWUSu0) and [Pascal's Wager](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p3ScupIbdkU), and Chignell's chapter in book 020.)

## Positions

### Belief, and the rival models of faith (the lecture taxonomy)
The lecture [Belief vs Faith](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o8QKkHWUSu0) fixes the baseline: belief is a *propositional attitude* — S believes P iff S assents to the truth of P, S thinks P is true — one attitude among others (desire, wonder) that a person can take toward a proposition. Faith has no agreed definition; the lecture surveys the models:
- **Doxastic venture**: faith is a *kind* of belief — belief without justification or beyond the evidence ("Petro has faith his team will come back": no evidence, believes anyway). Contrasted with knowledge (true justified belief), faith is belief that may be true but is not (yet) justified. Some Christians, especially fideists, hold this for religious faith precisely: the truths of religion are not subject to rational justification and must be taken on faith.
- **Aquinas's model**: faith is belief specifically in religious doctrines; some articles of faith can be demonstrated by reason, others are only revealed and must be believed without proof; revealed faith ranks in certainty between demonstrable proof and mere opinion.
- **Faith as belief in specific propositions**: faith is simply belief whose objects are a special set (that God exists; the revealed propositions) — no distinctive epistemic gap required.

To these the lecture *Why Science Rests On Faith* ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V-g7Glyxi98)) adds a deliberately provocative levelling model: define faith as *belief based on assumptions* and knowledge as belief with *no* assumptions, and even "Earth's gravity is 9.8 m/s²" fails the knowledge bar — it rests on chains of trust in teachers, experimenters, senses, memory, induction, logic, and on not being systematically deceived. "Science is not the only one. Things like mathematics, religion, logic, and statistics are also based on certain assumptions. You can judge them by the number of assumptions they make, but they all make them." The lecture presents this as a high-bar skeptical exercise, not as a verdict that all faiths are equal.

### The presumption of atheism: who goes first? (Antony Flew)
Before asking what justifies theistic belief, Flew asks where the debate *starts*. His paper, as the lecture [The Presumption of Atheism (Antony Flew)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pKo7FBUBhKQ) presents it, distinguishes positive atheism (believing God does not exist), *negative* atheism (lacking belief in God — the wider class, including positive atheists), agnosticism, and theism, and argues our default position should be negative atheism: the burden of proof rests with the theist, who must produce a coherent concept of God and evidence for it before disbelief needs any defence. The lecture notes the biographical coda it finds methodologically important: Flew held the presumption as an atheist and kept it after his late move to deism — he claimed to have been *convinced from* the default, not to have abandoned it. The positions this entry's other sections take can be read as so many negotiations with that default: the wager and James try to justify *moving* from it on practical grounds; Chignell's acceptance sidesteps it by changing the attitude in question; Rowe's friendly atheism (book 027, ch. 1) complicates it from the atheist side by granting that theists may be rationally justified in not moving at all.

### Pascal's wager: belief as a bet
Pascal's argument is openly pragmatic: "we cannot know whether or not God exists," so we should choose the belief "whatever would be the most profitable." The lecture presents a strengthened version of the wager and works it as a decision matrix.

**Canonical argument** (source: Carneades, [Pascal's Wager](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p3ScupIbdkU) — a formalization of the lecture's decision-theoretic presentation, labelled as such):
- P1. Reason cannot settle whether God exists; we must choose whether to believe (the choice is forced — not choosing is itself a choice against).
- P2. If we believe and God exists, the gain is infinite (heaven, "all these wonderful things"); if we believe and God does not exist, the loss is finite (some finite bad).
- P3. If we do not believe and God exists, we forgo the infinite gain; if we do not believe and God does not exist, we break even or gain finitely.
- ∴ C. For any nonzero probability that God exists, believing has infinite expected value and dominates; so we ought to believe.

**Objections** (source: [Objections to Pascal's Wager](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ACeOPJPsHd8)):
- **The many-gods problem** — the lecture calls it the compelling one. The matrix's two columns are not exhaustive: an evil god who rewards *non*-theists infinitely, a skeptical god who rewards only rational nonbelievers, a demon who rewards only cultists — each candidate column plants an infinity of its own, the infinities cancel, and the decision falls back to the finite payoffs the wager was built to avoid. Unlikelihood is no rescue: an infinite payoff "will eclipse any improbability."
- **Doxastic involuntarism** — Pascal assumes we can take up belief at will because it profits us; but "we don't always believe in what is good for us, even if we want to." The wager offers a *practical* reason where a *rational* (evidential) reason was wanted.

Note where the wager reappears below: Chignell's Ladder names Pascal as the patron of the *initiate* rung — accepting and practising one's way toward belief ("fake it to make it") is precisely the wager's psychology, redescribed as liturgy.

### Acceptance, not belief: the liturgical stance (Andrew Chignell, book 020, ch. 6)
Chignell's manifesto begins from an empirical suspicion: "many religious people do not often have the attitude of full *belief* with respect to many doctrines that characterize the traditions of which they are a part," may lack a clear sense of what they are supposed to believe, and rarely believe in an internalistically justified way even when they do. A philosophy of religion fixated on justifying *theistic belief* therefore studies a "regulative (and possibly western) ideal" — the "epistemological saint" — rather than religious life as lived. His alternative object of study is the **liturgical stance**: the complex of attitudes taken toward doctrines in communal practice (kneeling and praying toward Mecca, the Passover seder, taking the wafer), which involves what he calls *teleological insincerity* — "insincerity upwards" — in degrees, his **Ladder of Liturgical Sincerity**: the *inquirer* (performing to understand what membership is like), the *discerner* (trying to decide whether to join), the *conditional seeker* (acting on "if God exists, this is fitting"), the *initiate* (accepting in order to grow into belief — Pascal's posture), and, rarely attained and not always desirable, the *utterly convinced*. The Ladder is explicitly not a ranking of virtue: Chignell argues it is morally preferable that some confessional contents *not* be believed with full sincerity.

Against belief — construed, following Hume, as an involuntary disposition to *feel* a proposition true, with its characteristic "yes-phenomenology" — Chignell sets **acceptance**:

**The acceptance model** (source: Chignell, book 020, ch. 6, §6.2.2 — stated as a position, in his terms):
- Acceptance is a *voluntary* attitude toward p, adopted in certain contexts for broadly practical reasons; it can motivate asserting p, deliberating on the basis of p, and acting as if p, while typically lacking belief's phenomenology.
- It is more robust than mere assumption-for-the-sake-of-argument, and can be partly justified by something other than evidence — which is why it may be compatible with Clifford-style evidentialism about *belief itself*.
- On Chignell's reading, acceptance — not belief — is what James, and Kant before him, actually took to be involved in religious faith and liturgical practice.

**Hope** completes the trio (§6.2.3). Kant's "minimum of theology": the *hope* that God exists together with the belief/faith (*Glaube*) that God is really possible — a standard even agnostics and atheists could meet, read by some as appealingly low and by others as appallingly low. Chignell's reconciliation: rational hope in this "practico-dogmatic" sense requires only one commitment — *acceptance that God's existence is really possible* — while a life-structuring hope may carry a sophisticated train of desires, virtues, and *trust in* (God, nature, history, karma) that needs separate analysis. His summary challenge to the field: it is "clearly consistent with hope that p, and may be consistent with faith that p, that one fails to believe p or even believes that not-p" ('I believe, help Thou my unbelief' becomes 'I hope and sometimes even accept, help Thou my unbelief'). Philosophers of religion should be *pistologists* and *elpistologists* — students of *pistis* and *elpis* — not only of knowledge.

### James: the pragmatic orientation of faith (via David C. Lamberth, book 020, ch. 16)
Lamberth's James supplies the historical root of Chignell's reading (Chignell cites James as construing religious faith as acceptance). Three elements of Lamberth's account matter here. (i) *Thought exists for action*: in "Reflex Action and Theism" (1881) James sets aside whether God exists and asks what the *concept* of God does — it is "an important, concrete practical idea" that orients action toward our greater welfare and toward our more inclusive, long-run "ideal interests"; "anything short of God is not rational; anything more than God is not possible." To demand that subjective interests stay passive until truth arrives from the environment "is to bid the sculptor's chisel be passive till the statue expresses itself from out of the stone." *The Will to Believe* (1896) belongs to this line of thought. (ii) *Experience as data*: the *Varieties* (1902) treats transformative religious experience psychologically, individually and socially — the corpus's own studied version of the experience that other entries meet as an argument ([The Ontological Argument](ontological-argument.md)). (iii) *Meliorism*: in *Pragmatism* (1907) salvation is treated "neither as necessary, nor as impossible," but as "a possibility, which becomes more and more of a probability the more numerous the actual conditions of salvation become" — faith's object as a live possibility that human effort helps actualize, a structure strikingly parallel to Kant's minimum (hope + real possibility) as Chignell presents it. Lamberth's framing conclusion is about religion's future, not its proof: the twenty-first century is unlikely to see humankind evolve "beyond" religion in any meaningful sense.

## Thought experiments
- **Petro's team** (Belief vs Faith lecture). Petro has faith his team will come back from its deficit — no evidence, belief anyway. The trivial case isolates the doxastic-venture model: if faith is just that, religious faith differs from sports faith only in stakes; the lecture's other models exist because many accounts of faith deny exactly this.
- **The wager's matrix** (Pascal's Wager lecture). A toy game first — win $3 or lose $1 at even odds, expected value +$0.50, so play — then the same machinery with an infinite payoff in one cell. The toy case shows the machinery is ordinary decision theory; the objections lecture shows the infinity cell is where all the trouble (rival infinities; forced choices) concentrates.

## Where the debate stands
The sources agree on the diagnosis more than the cure. All of them complicate the picture "religious commitment = justified belief": the lecture by multiplying models of faith; Flew by relocating the argument to burden-of-proof; the wager and James by licensing practical grounds where evidence runs out (with the wager's licence revoked, in the objections lecture, by rival infinities and the involuntariness of belief); Chignell by arguing the field has been studying an idealization and proposing acceptance and hope as the operative attitudes; Lamberth's James by exhibiting a major philosopher for whom the practical orientation was the philosophy. What no source here does is settle the normative question the complication leaves behind: Chignell raises the justification-and-defeat conditions for acceptance and hope as a *programme* ("we will need an account of hope"), explicitly declines to answer his own deepest questions in the chapter, and the lectures present models rather than crown one. It is also worth marking the limit of the levelling move in *Why Science Rests On Faith*: its "faith" (any belief resting on assumptions) is a third sense, neither the venture model nor acceptance — the corpus uses one word for at least three ideas, and this entry keeps them separate.
