# Book map — 018: Walter Kaufmann (ed.), *Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre* (1956; Pickle Partners Publishing reprint)

*Book layer · primary-text anthology. ★ = chapter feeds one of this subfield's problem entries.*

**Extraction note — AUTHORSHIP CORRECTION.** This folder is catalogued in the corpus simply as "018 - Existentialism." It is **not** a book by Robert C. Solomon: the title page (file ch02) reads *Existentialism: From Dostoevsky to Sartre*, "Edited, with an introduction, prefaces, and new translations by **Walter Kaufmann**," and file ch04 is Kaufmann's author page. The extracted folder holds 18 files: publisher front matter (ch01), title page (ch02), Contents (ch03), About the Author (ch04), Preface (ch05), the ten anthology chapters (ch06–ch15), Sources and Acknowledgments (ch16), a publisher's review-request page (ch17, not book content), and endnotes (ch18). Chapter text is full where it matters most (Kaufmann's introduction, Jaspers, Sartre) but the finale is partial — see ch15 below. Kaufmann's editorial choices matter for citation: in the Sartre chapter he changes Hazel Barnes' rendering of *mauvaise foi* from "bad faith" to "self-deception" throughout, so quotations from this corpus say *self-deception* where most of the literature says *bad faith*.

- **Preface** [file ch05]. Kaufmann against anthologies as "a cultural supermarket": the book is an argument in selection and arrangement, not a sampler.
- **ONE — Kaufmann, "Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre"** [file ch06, ~16,300 words] ★ → both existentialism entries (framing). The editor's introduction, in sections on Dostoevsky, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Jaspers, Heidegger, Sartre, and "A Story with a Moral." Opening thesis: "Existentialism is not a philosophy but a label for several widely different revolts against traditional philosophy"; the figures share "perfervid individualism," refusal of systems, and dissatisfaction with academic philosophy — not tenets. Also carries Kaufmann's caution against the slogan: it "has become a commonplace that existentialism is the doctrine that existence precedes essence, and this phrase obstructs the understanding of this complex movement as well as of Sartre's philosophy."
- **TWO — Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*, Part One** [file ch07, ~13,400 words]. The Underground Man's spiteful monologue against rational egoism and the crystal palace; only Part One is reprinted (per Sources, ch16). Kaufmann's preface calls the *Notes* (1864) "one of the most revolutionary and original works of world literature" and man's inner life — "his moods, anxieties, and his decisions" — moved to the centre. Feeds the absurdity entry's background (the revolt against a world that will not make sense on demand).
- **THREE — Kierkegaard, "On Himself"** [file ch08, ~6,900 words]. Selections from the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* ("On his Mission," published over a pseudonym with Kierkegaard named as editor) and *The Point of View for My Work as an Author*: the individual against the theoretical system; Kierkegaard's own account of his authorship and his protest against institutional Christianity.
- **FOUR — Nietzsche, "Live Dangerously"** [file ch09, ~5,100 words]. Selections (from *Schopenhauer as Educator*, *The Gay Science*, *Zarathustra*, and others, per Sources) chosen "to bring out that aspect of his thought which makes his inclusion in the story of existentialism imperative" — self-overcoming and living dangerously rather than the moral critique, which this KB takes from book 019 itself. Kaufmann's preface also handles the Nazi misappropriation: Nietzsche's books as wholes were of no use to them.
- **FIVE — Rilke, *The Notes of Malte Laurids Brigge*** [file ch10, ~3,100 words]. Excerpts (newly translated by Kaufmann) from Rilke's prose work of urban dread and seen death; included partly because *Malte* influenced Sartre's *La Nausée*.
- **SIX — Kafka, "Three Parables"** [file ch11, ~4,100 words]. "The Imperial Message," "Couriers," and the parable from *The Trial* (pp. 268–278 of the Muir translation, per Sources) — Kaufmann calls the three "among the most important documents for the interpretation of *The Castle*." Parables of unreachable authority and endlessly deferred arrival; background to the absurd.
- **SEVEN — Jaspers, *Existenzphilosophie*** [file ch12, ~28,600 words]. The largest selection after Sartre: "On My Philosophy" (autobiographical essay), the first two lectures of *Reason and Existenz* (1935) — on Kierkegaard and Nietzsche, and on "The Encompassing" — and the postscript material. Jaspers' own route into *Existenz* philosophy and his reading of the two "exceptions," Kierkegaard and Nietzsche, as the indispensable precursors.
- **EIGHT — Heidegger, "The Way Back into the Ground of Metaphysics"** [file ch13, ~6,400 words]. Heidegger's 1949 introduction to the fifth printing of *What is Metaphysics?* (1929), translated for this volume with Heidegger's own answers to the translator's questions on key terms (*Sein* as Being). A self-contained late essay on the ground of metaphysics, not an excerpt from *Being and Time*.
- **NINE — Sartre, "Existentialism"** [file ch14, ~39,000 words] ★ → *Existence Precedes Essence: Sartre on Freedom and Self-Deception*. Four selections: (1) "The Wall" (the short story); (2) "Self-Deception" — the *mauvaise foi* chapter of *Being and Nothingness*, unabridged in Barnes' translation as Kaufmann amended it (the lie-to-oneself puzzle; the woman on the first date; the café waiter who plays at being a waiter); (3) "Portrait of the Anti-Semite" (slightly abridged); (4) "Existentialism is a Humanism" — the 1946 lecture, unabridged in Mairet's translation (existence precedes essence; the paper-knife; anguish, abandonment, despair; "condemned to be free"; the pupil torn between his mother and the Free French).
- **TEN — Camus, "The Myth of Sisyphus"** [file ch15, ~1,500 words] ★ → *The Absurd and Sisyphus*. **Partial:** only the concluding chapter of Camus' book of that name is reprinted — the Sisyphus essay itself, complete as an essay ("One must imagine Sisyphus happy"), but the book's earlier chapters, where the absurd is defined and the argument about suicide is made, are **not in the corpus**. Kaufmann's preface adds the standing caution: Camus "is often called an existentialist, though many critics insist that this is an invidious error."
- **Sources and Acknowledgments** [file ch16]; **notes** [file ch18] — the anthology's endnotes (Dostoevsky's author's note, Kierkegaard pseudonymity notes, etc.).

## Coverage
Read in full for this slice: the Preface, ch06 (Kaufmann's introduction, openings and the Sartre section), ch14 §2 and §4 (the entry chapters), and ch15 entire. Remaining chapters' descriptions rest on their prefaces, openings, and the Sources list.
