- **Introduction** — Prasanta K. Pattanaik [file ch08] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem and the Possibility of Social Choice*. The state of welfare economics when Arrow published (1950/51): Bergson–Samuelson social welfare functions, the Pareto principle and its incompleteness, the failed compensation criteria. States the theorem in the clean modern form used by this KB: with at least three distinct social states and finitely many individuals, **no social welfare function simultaneously satisfies unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and nondictatorship** — and notes the result "has the flavor of a paradox" precisely because each condition seems plausible.

## Part I — The Lectures
- **Opening Remarks** — Joseph E. Stiglitz [ch10]. Occasion-setting for the Arrow lectures.
- **Arrow and the Impossibility Theorem** — Amartya Sen [ch11] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. The theorem explained and proved in elementary steps; its democratic sting — a dictatorial outcome is "a major embarrassment for that set of axioms," not an acceptable corollary; the informational presumption behind social choice (social judgments must depend, transparently, on individual preferences). Closes by noting what Arrow's framework does **not** accommodate: liberty and the rights of minorities against majorities — Sen invokes Mill on "busybodies": "The importance of liberty may demand going against the objections of numerous 'busybodies.' … It is not accommodated in Arrow's axioms either." Feeds the entry's "where the debate stands."
- **The Arrow Impossibility Theorem: Where Do We Go from Here?** — Eric Maskin [ch12] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. Recasts the theorem for elections: plurality rule vs Condorcet's majority rule (the candidate beating each other head-to-head), and how the same rankings elect different winners under different rules. Argues the pessimistic reading ("no good election method") is too strong: using Arrow's own criteria, "there is a sense in which we can't do better than majority rule after all" — while conceding that when Condorcet-paradox rankings arise, a tie-breaking rule is needed in practice.
- **Commentary** — Kenneth J. Arrow [ch13]. Arrow responds to both lecturers.

## Part II — Supplemental Materials
- **The Informational Basis of Social Choice** — Amartya Sen [ch15] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. "Any procedure of social choice makes use of some types of information and ignores others": majority decision uses votes but ignores preference *intensities*, social standing, prosperity or penury. The escape route this opens: impossibility results are relative to an informational basis — Arrow's framework excludes cardinal and interpersonally comparable information by construction — so enriching the information a procedure may use changes what is possible.
- **On the Robustness of Majority Rule** — Partha Dasgupta and Eric Maskin [ch16] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. Paper with abstract: "simple majority rule satisfies five standard and attractive axioms — the Pareto property, anonymity, neutrality, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and (generic) decisiveness — over a larger class of preference domains than (essentially) any other voting rule. Hence, in this sense, it is the most robust voting rule." Shows concretely how Borda/rank-order and plurality violate IIA while majority rule's x-vs-y choice depends only on x-vs-y preferences.
- **The Origins of the Impossibility Theorem** — Kenneth J. Arrow [ch17]. Arrow's personal account of the steps by which he arrived at the theorem.
