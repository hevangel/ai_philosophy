- **Preface** [file ch08] ★. The book's thesis stated up front: "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)." Main conclusions: a minimal state "limited to the narrow functions of protection against force, theft, fraud, enforcement of contracts, and so on, is justified; … any more extensive state will violate persons' rights"; the state may not use coercion "for the purpose of getting some citizens to aid others, or in order to prohibit activities to people for their *own* good or protection."

## Part I — State-of-Nature Theory, or How to Back into a State without Really Trying
- **1. Why State-of-Nature Theory?** [ch11] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Political philosophy's fundamental question is whether there should be any state at all — "Why not have anarchy?" State-of-nature theory has an explanatory purpose: show how a state could arise without anyone intending it and without rights violations.
- **2. The State of Nature** [ch12] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Locke's state of nature and its inconveniences (biased self-judging, feuds, no settled dispute-ending); the remedies available *within* the state of nature — protective associations, then the dominant protective association — and invisible-hand explanations (the money analogy).
- **3. Moral Constraints and the State** [ch13] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Minimal vs *ultraminimal* state (monopoly of force, but protection only for purchasers); why the night-watchman state only *appears* redistributive; rights as side constraints rather than goals (against utilitarianism — the innocent man punished to calm a neighbourhood); the individualist anarchist's challenge stated.
- **4. Prohibition, Compensation, and Risk** [ch14] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Independents living among a dominant agency's clients; when an unreliable private enforcement procedure may be prohibited, and the principle of compensation owed to those prohibited.
- **5. The State** [ch15] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Prohibiting private enforcement of justice; the "principle of fairness" considered and found insufficient; procedural rights; the *de facto* monopoly; the step from ultraminimal to minimal state — the dominant agency is morally required to compensate those it prohibits from self-help, and compensation takes the form of protective services, which is what makes the state minimal rather than ultraminimal. The invisible-hand explanation of the state assessed: it explains the monopoly, not any special rights (the state has none).
- **6. Further Considerations on the Argument for the State** [ch16] ★ → *The Minimal State*. Objections: does the self-defence right prove too much (could each side forbid the other from combining)? Preemptive attack, legitimacy, preventive restraint.

## Part II — Beyond the Minimal State?
- **7. Distributive Justice** [ch18 + ch19] ★ → *Distributive Justice*. §I: the entitlement theory (justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, rectification — a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to their holdings); historical vs end-result (current time-slice) principles; patterning; "How Liberty Upsets Patterns" — the Wilt Chamberlain example; redistribution and property rights ("Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor"); Locke's theory of acquisition and the Lockean proviso (the desert water-hole case). §II (continues in file ch19): Rawls's theory — social cooperation, the original position and the difference principle, the argument from the moral arbitrariness of natural assets reconstructed and contested (arguments A–D), collective assets.
- **8. Equality, Envy, Exploitation, Etc.** [ch20]. Equality and equality of opportunity; self-esteem and envy; meaningful work and workers' control; Marxian exploitation and voluntary exchange; philanthropy; the non-neutral state.
- **9. Demoktesis** [ch21]. A deliberately parallel derivation: an attempt to continue the legitimate-steps story toward a more-than-minimal state ("demoktesis" — ownership of the people by the people), offered to expose what such derivations actually show; hypothetical histories.

## Part III — Utopia
- **10. A Framework for Utopia** [ch23]. No single best world for everyone; the model of imagined worlds and emigration between them; the framework — a minimal-state meta-utopia within which diverse communities form by voluntary association and exit — as "utopian common ground."
