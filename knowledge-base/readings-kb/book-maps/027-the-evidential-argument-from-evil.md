- **Preface** [file ch08]. Howard-Snyder: philosophical study of evil has shifted from compatibility ("logical") questions to "the evidential problem of evil"; the volume mixes a handful of the best previously published essays with new work written for it, each new essay refereed by other contributors, aiming at a dialogue a student can follow.
- **Introduction: The Evidential Argument from Evil** — Daniel Howard-Snyder [file ch09] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*; *The Logical Problem of Evil*. Argues "the problem of evil" is a misleading name for a family of *arguments*. Draws the book's master distinction by its paradigm members: a *logical* argument (Mackie) has a premise saying theism is incompatible with some *known* fact about evil; an *evidential* argument lacks such a premise — its key fact about evil is at most probable or reasonable to believe. Shows Mackie's version fails because his moral principle needs strengthening (MP2), after which compatibility can no longer be ruled out. Then maps the volume's central issues: total evidence, theodicy, the concept of (epistemic) probability, arguments from inscrutable evil, and explanatory arguments.
- **1. The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism** — William L. Rowe [file ch11] ★ → both evil entries. The founding statement of the evidential argument: (1) there exist instances of intense suffering an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil; (2) a wholly good omniscient being would prevent any such suffering it could, on those terms; (3) so no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being exists. Premise (2) is common ground; premise (1) cannot be *proved* (the burned fawn's suffering is only *apparently* pointless) but is, Rowe argues, rational to believe given the variety and scale of suffering. The theist's best reply is not a direct attack (naming the goods served — which the theist cannot do case by case) but the indirect "G. E. Moore shift": argue from God's existence plus (2) to the denial of (1). Closes by defending *friendly atheism* — some theists are rationally justified in their belief — against unfriendly and indifferent atheism. A note concedes the *logical* form of the problem fails, citing Plantinga's *God, Freedom, and Evil*.
- **2. Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists** — Paul Draper [file ch12] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. A different, *explanatory* evidential argument that never claims any evil is pointless. Against theism Draper sets the Hypothesis of Indifference (HI): neither the nature nor the condition of sentient beings on earth results from benevolent or malevolent actions by non-human persons. Let O report our observations and testimony about pain and pleasure; Draper's claim C is that O is antecedently (epistemically) much more probable on HI than on theism — Pr(O/HI) >! Pr(O/theism) — because of the *biological utility* of pain and pleasure in goal-directed organisms. §III then evaluates theodicies (free will and moral advancement, responsibility, the "infinite intellect" defence) to defend the assumption that theistic stories do not significantly raise Pr(O/theism). If C holds, we have a prima facie reason to reject theism.
- **3. Some Major Strands of Theodicy** — Richard Swinburne [file ch13] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Theodicy defined as showing that evils of the kind and quantity found on Earth are neither incompatible with nor render improbable God's existence. A perfectly good being may allow an evil only if he has the *right* to do so and some good is brought about by allowing it that could not be brought about in any better (logically possible) way. Swinburne then works through goods that, he argues, logically require the occurrence or possible occurrence of evils: the satisfaction of persisting desire (which requires temporary frustration), compassion and kindred responses to suffering, significant free choice between good and evil (natural evil supplies the knowledge of how to harm that makes such choice possible — a strand he flags but sets aside here), responsibility, and more. He is explicit that showing the goods *outweigh* the evils cannot be done conclusively in one paper.
- **4. Aquinas on the Sufferings of Job** — Eleonore Stump [file ch14] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil* (theodicy section). Aquinas's commentary on Job does not treat innocent suffering as evidence against God's existence but as a question about how providence operates: Job's friends wrongly suppose providence rewards virtue and punishes sin *in this life*, whereas for Aquinas earthly goods and evils can be justified only by reference to the afterlife, and suffering functions as medicine for a condition that hinders eternal communion with God. Stump's moral: how suffering looks depends on prior views about human happiness and the goal of human life, and those larger issues must be settled before theodicy's prospects can be assessed.
- **5. Epistemic Probability and Evil** — Alvin Plantinga [file ch15] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*; *The Logical Problem of Evil*. Distinguishes the pastoral/existential problem of evil from the epistemic one. Concedes the theist cannot say in detail why God permits particular evils — and calls many theodicies "shallow, tepid, and ultimately frivolous" — but denies that this alone threatens rationality. Examines the deductive (Mackie) and probabilistic atheological arguments, finds the prospects of the probabilistic version bleak on standard accounts of probability, argues those accounts lack the resources for the question anyway, and closes by relocating the issue: what matters is not the *propositional* warrant of theistic belief but its *nonpropositional* warrant.
- **6. The Inductive Argument from Evil and the Human Cognitive Condition** — William P. Alston [file ch16] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Grants that the logical argument "is bankrupt" and attacks Rowe's inductive argument at premise (1): the *agnostic thesis* — our cognitive situation (our grasp of possible goods, their values, and the conditions of their realization) is radically insufficient to justify judging that any particular intense suffering is gratuitous, i.e., that God could have no sufficient reason to permit it. The bulk of the chapter catalogues these cognitive limitations.
- **7. Rowe's Noseeum Arguments from Evil** — Stephen John Wykstra [file ch17] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Rowe's inference — we *see no* outweighing good for sufferings like the fawn's, so probably there *is no* such good — is dubbed a "noseeum" argument. Wykstra's CORNEA (Condition of Reasonable Epistemic Access): an inference from "we see no X" to "there is no X" is licensed only when X has reasonable *seeability* — when, if it existed, we could reasonably expect to see it (a dog in a garage, yes; a flea, no). Given the disparity between a creator's vision and ours, God-purposed goods should not be expected to be seeable, so Rowe's argument fails CORNEA. The chapter then refines CORNEA against Rowe's replies, via a dispute about how defeaters work.
- **8. The Problem of Evil, the Problem of Air, and the Problem of Silence** — Peter van Inwagen [file ch18] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Takes up the strongest evidential argument in Draper-style form (a rival hypothesis on which the observed suffering is far more easily explained) and replies that we are in no position to judge what to expect on theism — we cannot assign the relevant probabilities. The diagnosis is supported by two constructed parallel arguments meant to be obviously faulty in the same way: an "evidential argument from air" addressed to an ancient atomist, and an argument from divine silence.
- **9. The Skeptical Theist** — Paul Draper [file ch19] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Names and examines *skeptical theism*: the thesis (ST1) that humans are in no position to judge directly that God would be unlikely to have a morally sufficient reason to permit the evils we find. Draper's complaint is not ST1 itself but the inference from ST1 to the failure of *all* probabilistic arguments from evil: Draper's own ch. 2 argument, for instance, never needs to judge that God probably lacks a sufficient reason, so it is untouched by ST1. Concludes skeptical theists "have not yet solved the evidential problem of evil."
- **10. Defenseless** — Bruce Russell [file ch20] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Sorts evidential arguments into a matrix (inductive vs abductive routes to "there is gratuitous evil," applied to kinds, instances, amounts, or patterns of suffering) and defends them against the charge that our sample of known goods is unrepresentative: goods "beyond our ken" count against representativeness only if there is reason to believe there are any — just as hypothetical unobservable crows do not undermine the inference from "no blue crows observed" (the zagaloops case marks where suspension of judgment *is* required: when we simply have no idea whether our sample is representative).
- **11. Some Difficulties in Theistic Treatments of Evil** — Richard M. Gale [file ch21] ★ → both evil entries. Distinguishes theodicy (a defence plus reason to think its possible world is actual) from the weaker *defensive skepticism*, and presses difficulties for both: against defensive skepticism, that we may reasonably judge it improbable in the extreme that there are justifying goods outside our ken, and that if some good justified God we would very likely know of it; against theodicies, charges of epistemic laxness and axiological laxness. (His own book argues no free-will defence succeeds against the *logical* problem for moral evil, but that is expressly not this chapter's concern.)
- **12. Reflections on the Chapters by Draper, Russell, and Gale** — Peter van Inwagen [file ch22]. A technical sequel to ch. 8: epistemic probability is best understood as epistemic judgment about non-epistemic (objective) probability, modelled via an "ideal bookmaker"; Draper's evidential challenge is reconstructed in those terms, ch. 8's reply restated against it, and Draper's conditions on how an evidential challenge must be met rejected as "intolerably restrictive."
- **13. On Being Evidentially Challenged** — Alvin Plantinga [file ch23] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. A point-by-point examination of Draper's ch. 2: what O is (person-relative), what C claims, and why Plantinga doubts C is true; then the *total evidence* point — other evidence may outweigh O's disconfirmation — and the closing move of ch. 5: the theist may have nonpropositional grounds for belief better than any propositional grounds an argument from evil supplies against it, so whether an evidential argument renders theistic belief unreasonable depends on whether theism is true.
- **14. The Evidential Argument from Evil: A Second Look** — William L. Rowe [file ch24] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Rowe restates his argument around two cases — E1, the fawn ("Bambi"), and E2, the rape and murder of a five-year-old girl ("Sue"): P (no good *we know of* justifies God in permitting E1 and E2) inductively supports Q (no good *at all* justifies God in permitting them), and Q deductively yields not-G. He catalogues four lines of criticism (P unjustified; the P→Q inference weak; defeaters; Q not entailing not-G), answers the objections to P→Q from Alston and Wykstra, and develops the Bayesian version Howard-Snyder's Introduction describes — one that, in a further variant, bypasses Q and argues from P toward the probable falsity of theism more directly. Notably, Rowe now concedes the bare enumerative induction from P raises Q's probability without, as it stands, making Q more likely than not.
- **15. The Argument from Inscrutable Evil** — Daniel Howard-Snyder [file ch25] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Attacks a presupposition of appeals to particular horrors (Ivan Karamazov's tortured children are the epigraph and test case): that if God exists he *must* have a reason to permit *that* evil — that boy, then and there, by that means. By a series of basketball-coach analogies Howard-Snyder argues God need have no reason at that level of specificity, and that the appeal further presupposes a questionable "threshold" thesis — an exact amount of horrific evil such that slightly less would not serve God's purposes. Distinguishes inscrutable evil (we cannot grasp God's reason) from pointless evil (there is none) and argues the first does not justify belief in the second; closes with a Rowe-style Bayesian argument and the silence of God.
- **16. Some (Temporarily) Final Thoughts on Evidential Arguments from Evil** — William P. Alston [file ch26] ★ → *The Evidential Argument from Evil*. Alston's self-critical "clean-up" chapter: his ch. 6 tried to do two things that did not blend — neutralize Rowe/Russell-style arguments from refractory cases, and mine theodicies for unexcludable *possibilities* of divine reasons. He now drops the theodicy rehearsal and rests the case on the live possibility of divine reasons unenvisaged by us, which he thinks can "take up the slack, … including 100 percent if needed"; also engages Rowe's new Bayesian argument, conceding it improves on the bare P→Q induction while denying it succeeds.
