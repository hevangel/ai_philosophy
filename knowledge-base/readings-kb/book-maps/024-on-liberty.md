## *On Liberty*
- **I. Introductory** [file ch03] ★ → *The Harm Principle and Freedom of Expression*. The subject is civil or social liberty: "the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual." From the old struggle against rulers to the new one: the tyranny of the majority and, deeper, "a social tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression," exercised through prevailing opinion. The essay's "one very simple principle" is asserted here: "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." Scope limits (children; "backward states of society") and the ground: "I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions; but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being."
- **II. Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion** [file ch04] ★ → *The Harm Principle and Freedom of Expression*. "If all mankind minus one, were of one opinion… mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind." Silencing robs the human race whether the opinion is true (exchange of error for truth) or false (clearer perception of truth by collision with error). The chapter closes by recapitulating **four grounds**: (1) the silenced opinion may be true — denying this assumes our infallibility; (2) even error commonly contains a portion of truth, supplied only by collision of adverse opinions; (3) an uncontested true doctrine is held as prejudice, without comprehension of its rational grounds; (4) the doctrine's very meaning is lost, becoming "a mere formal profession." Also: social intolerance stifles without fining or imprisoning, at the price of "the entire moral courage of the human mind."
- **III. Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being** [file ch05] ★ → *The Harm Principle and Freedom of Expression*. The same reasons require freedom to act on one's opinions "at their own risk and peril" — with the chapter's limit case, the **corn-dealer example**: "even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act"; the opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor "ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer." Individuality and spontaneity as components of well-being; the danger of custom and mediocrity.
- **IV. Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual** [file ch06] ★ → *The Harm Principle and Freedom of Expression*. Where the harm principle's boundary runs in practice: the individual "is the person most interested in his own well-being"; society's interest in his self-regarding conduct "is fractional, and altogether indirect." Marks the distinction between the loss of consideration rightly incurred by imprudence and the reprobation/punishment due for infringing others' rights.
- **V. Applications** [file ch07]. The principles applied: trade, poisons, drunkenness and the Maine Law, dealers' interests, gambling houses, polygamy (Mormonism), education, and the state's role — the essay's test of where "harm to others" does and does not license interference in hard cases.

## *The Subjection of Women* (same 1879 volume — a separate work)
- **I** [file ch09]. The thesis: "the principle which regulates the existing social relations between the two sexes — the legal subordination of one sex to the other — is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to human improvement," to be replaced by "a principle of perfect equality." The burden of proof lies on the defenders of inequality, since the arrangement rests on force and custom, not on any trial of alternatives.
- **II** [file ch10]. The laws of marriage as they stand: the wife's legal non-existence, property, custody, and the husband's power — marriage analysed as a relation of legalized dependence.
- **III** [file ch11]. Women's admissibility to all functions and occupations monopolized by men; the alleged natural incapacities argued to be artifacts of the system that produces them.
- **IV** [file ch12]. What equality would do: the moral regeneration of mankind, the doubling of available talent, and the effect on marriage itself as a relation between equals.
