- **I. Introduction** [file ch10] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem and the Possibility of Social Choice*. The motivating frame: "In a capitalist democracy there are essentially two methods by which social choices can be made: voting, typically used to make 'political' decisions, and the market mechanism." Poses the question whether a method exists for passing from individual tastes to a social preference satisfying "certain natural conditions."
- **II. The Nature of Preference and Choice** [ch11]. Ordinal preference orderings; the book's foundational exclusion — "the viewpoint will be taken here that interpersonal comparison of utility" is not available — and choice functions and consistency axioms (the social ordering must be connected and transitive: Axioms I and II).
- **III. The Social Welfare Function** [ch12] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. The formal problem (after Bergson): a *social welfare function* is "a process or rule which, for each set of individual orderings … states a corresponding social ordering of alternative social states." The five conditions are imposed, each presented as an apparently reasonable value judgment expressing "citizens' sovereignty and rationality": **Condition 1** (a set of three alternatives exists that individuals can order in any way — the germ of unrestricted domain); **Condition 2** (positive association of social and individual values — if an alternative rises in every individual's ordering, it does not fall socially); **Condition 3** (independence of irrelevant alternatives — the choice from a set depends only on orderings within it; the rank-order/Borda method's failure is shown by example); **Condition 4** (the function is not *imposed* — no pair's social ranking is fixed regardless of all individual orderings); **Condition 5** (the function is not *dictatorial* — no individual whose strict preferences are always society's, regardless of everyone else). The Benthamite sum of utilities is considered and set aside as requiring the rejected cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility.
- **IV. The Compensation Principle** [ch13]. The Kaldor–Hicks–Scitovsky compensation criteria examined as candidate welfare functions — and shown capable of contradiction (a change and its reversal can both pass the test), sharpening the suspicion that no satisfactory construction exists.
- **V. The General Possibility Theorem for Social Welfare Functions** [ch14] ★ → *Arrow's Theorem…*. The theorem chapter. **Theorem 1 (two alternatives):** majority decision satisfies the conditions and always yields a social ordering — with two alternatives, possibility holds. **Theorem 2 (General Possibility Theorem), as printed:** "*If there are at least three alternatives which the members of the society are free to order in any way, then every social welfare function satisfying Conditions 2 and 3 and yielding a social ordering satisfying Axioms I and II must be either imposed or dictatorial.*" (The extraction prints "Conditions 2 and 8" at this point — an OCR/typographical artifact in the file; the surrounding proof text confirms the operative conditions are the five of ch. III, and the chapter's own summary states the result as: assuming Conditions 2 and 3 and Condition 1's freedom over three alternatives, "either Condition 4 or Condition 5 must be violated.") Arrow's interpretation, verbatim in substance: "If we exclude the possibility of interpersonal comparisons of utility, then the only methods of passing from individual tastes to social preferences which will be satisfactory and which will be defined for a wide range of sets of individual orderings are either imposed or dictatorial." His indicated escape: relax the *range of admissible orderings* — suppose individual orderings are known in advance to satisfy restrictions, and seek functions satisfactory only on that restricted range.
- **VI. The Individualistic Assumptions** [ch15]. What happens under restrictions on tastes: two conditions on individual preference scales (single-peakedness-type assumptions enter here) and what social welfare functions they permit.
- **VII. Similarity as the Basis of Social Welfare Judgments** [ch16]. Complete unanimity and near-unanimity as a basis: if individuals' orderings are sufficiently similar, collective judgments become possible — the domain-restriction route developed.
- **VIII. Notes on the Theory of Social Choice, 1963** [ch17]. Arrow's survey of the literature that grew up around the theorem in the decade after 1951 ("When the first edition of this book was prepared, the relevant literature was summarized in four pages").
