# Book map — 003: Helen Pluckrose & James Lindsay, *Cynical Theories: How Activist Scholarship Made Everything about Race, Gender, and Identity — and Why This Harms Everybody* (Pitchstone Publishing, 2020)

*Book layer · partisan secondary source — the authors' argument, presented as theirs. ★ = chapter feeds a problem entry.*

**Extraction note.** The extracted folder (`data/philosophy_readings/markdown/003 - Cynical Theories - …/`) holds 21 files: cover/title/copyright stubs (ch01–ch05), Contents (ch06), Acknowledgments (ch07), Introduction (ch08), the ten chapters (ch09–ch18, each ~7,000–11,500 words), Notes (ch19, ~17,100 words), Select Bibliography (ch20), and About the Authors (ch21). Text is complete and cleanly split. **Standing caution for the whole book:** this is a work of advocacy by two critics of the movements it describes. In this KB its claims about postmodernism and "Theory" are cited as *Pluckrose and Lindsay's argument, at the strength they state it* — never as the corpus's neutral verdict — and the entry it feeds sets beside it a Carneades lecture that describes postmodernism differently (see the entry's Where the debate stands).

- **Introduction** [file ch08] ★ → *Postmodernism and Its Applied Turn*. Frames the stakes: liberalism (democracy, universal rights, legal equality, freedom of expression, respect for evidence and reason) as the shared common ground now pressured by illiberal movements of right and left; the book takes the left-hand threat, whose root is that "the progressive left has aligned itself not with Modernity but with postmodernism, which rejects objective truth." Defines the book's usage: capital-S "Social Justice" for the specific doctrinal movement vs. lowercase social justice, which the authors say they support; capital-T "Theory" for the approach stemming from postmodernism. Promises the arc: ch. 1 original postmodernism, ch. 2 the applied turn, chs. 3–7 the applied Theories, ch. 8 the reified turn (*c.* 2010), ch. 9 Theory in action, ch. 10 the liberal alternative.
- **1. Postmodernism: A Revolution in Knowledge and Power** [file ch09] ★. The 1960s French theorists (Foucault, Derrida, Lyotard) and the book's analytic core — **two principles and four themes** that the authors say persist through everything that follows: the *postmodern knowledge principle* ("radical skepticism about whether objective knowledge or truth is obtainable and a commitment to cultural constructivism"); the *postmodern political principle* ("a belief that society is formed of systems of power and hierarchies, which decide what can be known and how"); and the themes (1) the blurring of boundaries, (2) the power of language, (3) cultural relativism, (4) the loss of the individual and the universal. Postmodernism characterized as "ultimately a form of cynicism" — despair about progress after the World Wars, collapsed empires, and disillusionment with Marxism.
- **2. Postmodernism's Applied Turn: Making Oppression Real** [file ch10] ★. The mutation of the late 1980s–1990s: deconstructive, playfully hopeless Theory made politically actionable — "applying the inapplicable" — as the principles and themes are put to work, field by field, on oppression conceived as real and systemic; the emergence of Social Justice scholarship as the vehicle.
- **3. Postcolonial Theory: Deconstructing the West to Save the Other** [file ch11]. "Undoubtedly the first emanation of applied postmodernism": alone among the applied Theories it derived directly from postmodern thought, organized around *decolonization*; both principles and all four themes traced through it; closing section calls it "a dangerous, patronizing Theory."
- **4. Queer Theory: Freedom from the Normal** [file ch12]. Queer Theory "presumes that oppression follows from categorization": sex, gender, and sexuality categories treated as wholly constructed in language and "scripted" onto people, with subverting the categories as the field's main objective; traced to Foucault's *History of Sexuality*.
- **5. Critical Race Theory and Intersectionality: Ending Racism by Seeing It Everywhere** [file ch13]. CRT as the authors present it: race as a social construct created to maintain white privilege and supremacy (a claim they trace to Du Bois and partly endorse), extended by intersectionality into a multiplying grid of marginalized identities — "complex, yet so very simple"; closing verdict in the section title: "noble ends, terrible means." (The Carneades lecture *What is Critical Race Theory?* in the corpus presents CRT differently — as a critique of liberal equality's failure to close outcome gaps — and is the entry's counterweight.)
- **6. Feminisms and Gender Studies: Simplification as Sophistication** [file ch14]. The claim that around the turn of the millennium the liberal, materialist, and radical feminisms were "almost wholly displaced" by the intersectional approach, which reads everything through a lens of oppression and complicity — the "death of liberal feminism."
- **7. Disability and Fat Studies: Support-Group Identity Theory** [file ch15]. The "relative newcomers": both principles traced in disability studies (ableism) and fat studies; the authors' label for the shared method is "support-group scholarship" — fields organized around validating a group's lived experience rather than testing claims.
- **8. Social Justice Scholarship and Thought: The Truth According to Social Justice** [file ch16] ★. The second evolution, from *c.* 2010: **reified postmodernism** — the principles and themes, originally held as sceptical tools, "asserted [as] the absolute truth" and fused into one dogmatic methodology ("thou shalt not disagree with Theory"; identity as epistemic authority — "who you are is what you know").
- **9. Social Justice in Action: Theory Always Looks Good on Paper** [file ch17]. The escape from the academy into workplaces, schools, and public life, told through cases (a supermarket worker sacked over a shared comedy skit; the Google memo firing) and sections on "cultures of coddling and victimhood" and an institutionalized case study.
- **10. An Alternative to the Ideology of Social Justice: Liberalism Without Identity Politics** [file ch18] ★. The authors' positive case, stated as a point-by-point antithesis: "Liberalism sees knowledge as something we can learn about reality, more or less objectively; Theory sees knowledges as completely created by humans… Liberalism accepts the correspondence theory of truth…; Theory promotes the idea that truth is a 'language game'… Liberalism believes in progress; Theory is radically cynical about the possibility of progress." Liberalism's self-exposure (it tolerates its own critics) is conceded, then turned: "Liberalism is not perfect. Nevertheless it is the antidote to Theory." Closes with principles for opposition and a warning that Theory fuels the identity politics of the extreme right.
