# From Friction to Understanding: AI Summarization, Intellectual Competence, and the Separation of Knowledge from Presentation

The rise of AI-generated summaries and notes has prompted a debate about the value of reading difficult texts. Some argue that the effort of grappling with dense writing is intrinsically valuable – that friction in reading is part of the intellectual labor. We contend instead that intellectual effort is instrumentally valuable: it should be applied to genuine challenges, not to overcome needless obstacles created by poor presentation. In this view, AI summarization is a welcome tool to eliminate extraneous friction (typos, jargon, fluff, ads) so readers can focus on the substance of what they want to learn. But we must preserve the “desirable difficulties” that build understanding, judgment, and autonomy.

Concretely, readers should learn to summarize (via self-explanation, paraphrase and note-taking) so they can evaluate any summary – AI-generated or not – for completeness and accuracy. They should also use AI summarizers when their goal is simply to extract the key claims or facts, freeing up time for higher-level thinking or creativity. Crucially, we propose a design principle for future writing: separate the knowledge content from the prose presentation. In other words, authors should encode claims, definitions, evidence, logic, and provenance in a structured form, and let the prose be a customizable “view” on that underlying knowledge. Then summaries – whether human or AI – become queries on a single source of truth, rather than hasty distillations of sprawling text.

This essay defends that thesis in stages. We first review cognitive and educational theory on cognitive load and desirable difficulties, showing why easy tasks feel good but harder tasks often yield deeper learning. We distinguish summarization as a learning strategy (a metacognitive skill) from summarization as a substitute for reading, and clarify when AI summaries help versus when they short‑circuit essential learning activities. We then situate the problem in social‐epistemological terms: the “chicken-or-egg” worry of trusting a summary without reading the original, and the logic of epistemic dependence on others (Hardwig) and testimony (Reid, Goldman, Hardwig, Coady). We compare scenarios – reading Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason versus a Kant commentary, or a “three‑page core” in a 300‑page management book – to show that different purposes (research, concept learning, doing philosophy) warrant different approaches. We also consider the new twist that authors now routinely use AI writing assistants: this tends to inflate word count and blur authorial intent, making separation of knowledge from form even more urgent.

Finally, we outline a positive architectural proposal. Inspired by software design (“single source of truth”) and recent work on AI-enhanced textbooks, we suggest that every discipline begin to build a knowledge layer: a database or graph of the core propositions, definitions, evidence, logic, and assumptions that underlie their texts. Human authors would still write narratives and arguments, but those would be generated or rendered from that structured knowledge. Summaries (AI or human) could then be audited against the source data, and personalized to a reader’s goal. This separation of concerns would minimize needless friction (e.g. bypassing verbose exposition) while keeping the interpretive work transparent and goal‑directed.

We acknowledge limits. Poetic, legal, or rhetorical writing rely on style, nuance, metaphor and ambiguity that resist atomic decomposition. Knowledge representations themselves encode interpretive choices and can miss tacit or emergent meaning. There is also the risk of deskilling – if learners never practice summarizing or reading complex prose, those skills may atrophy. And of course AI introduces biases and hallucinations: recent experiments show that misleading AI summaries (even if well-intentioned) can implant false memories and distort understanding. Yet these concerns call for smart design, not categorical rejection.

In what follows we develop each of these points, weaving in theory, empirical findings, and objections, to support our guiding principles. We aim for an essayistic argument framed as a proposal – one that is mindful of recent AI‑in‑education research but grounded in longstanding epistemological ideas. Throughout, we cite relevant literature to back our claims.

## Intellectual Effort: Friction vs. Productive Difficulty

Cognitive science teaches that human working memory is limited, so presenting information well is crucial. Cognitive load theory (Sweller et al.) distinguishes intrinsic load (the inherent difficulty of material) from extraneous load (the avoidable difficulty imposed by poor presentation). When a text is needlessly wordy, disorganized or cluttered, it imposes extraneous load on the reader. Instructional design should reduce that load: “materials should be designed so as to reduce the extraneous load” especially when content is already hard. In other words, AI summarization that strips away fluff, repetitive introduction, or distracting ads is exactly what cognitive load theory recommends – it spares the reader from expendable effort on form, leaving more resources for grasping the intrinsic content.

However, educational psychology also emphasizes desirable difficulties – the idea that effortful learning activities can enhance long-term retention and understanding. Tasks like retrieval practice, self-testing, and elaborative processing require more effort than passive reviewing, but they produce stronger memory and transfer. For example, Bjork and colleagues note that re-reading a text can give the illusion of familiarity without much learning, whereas using flashcards to recall information feels harder but builds deeper knowledge. Similarly, writing or explaining a text in one’s own words engages the learner actively: one classic experiment found that students instructed to write three-line summaries of each page performed at least as well on later tests as students taking notes; both groups outperformed those doing a shallow copying task. The act of summarizing forces identification and re-organization of the main ideas, which enhances comprehension and retention.

From these theories we draw an important distinction: easy processing that feels good (low extraneous load) is not always educationally valuable; difficult processing that matches the learning goal often is. Minimizing extraneous load (clear layout, concise writing, no ads or irrelevant content) is good, but eliminating intrinsic difficulty (by giving away the argument or circumventing analysis) can short‑circuit learning. In practical terms: if our goal is mere comprehension of facts, an AI summary can reduce unnecessary effort. But if our goal is to learn how to think critically, then we should preserve activities like paraphrasing, questioning, and synthesizing – even if they feel like “work.”

Educational psychology provides further nuance. Dunlosky et al. (2013) review many study techniques and rate their utility. They give high utility to practice testing and spaced repetition, and moderate utility to elaborative interrogation and self-explanation. By contrast, naive summarization (like telling students to write a summary) receives a low utility rating, not because summarizing is unhelpful, but because its effectiveness varies widely with context and skill. When students are trained to summarize (identifying key points and articulating them concisely), it can be beneficial – roughly on par with note-taking – but many students do it poorly without guidance. In short, generating a good summary is itself a skill that must be learned and practiced. A rushed or ill-informed summary can misrepresent the text, as Dunlosky warns: “If a summary does not emphasize the main points…or includes incorrect information, why would it be expected to benefit learning?”.

In sum: intellectual effort has instrumental value when it aligns with learning objectives. We should reduce needless friction (cut wordiness, clarify arguments, remove ads), but retain productive struggle (self-testing, explaining, reconstructing arguments). AI can help by automating the mundane tasks, so that human effort can focus on the challenging, goal‑directed tasks. But we do not advocate eliminating all reading or analysis: rather, we propose delegating the grunt work to machines while preserving human oversight and judgment.

## Summarization: Training the Mind and Serving the Reader

Given the above, let’s distinguish two roles of summarization:

Summarization as a learning technique (training oneself). Exercises like writing your own summary of a chapter train you to identify main ideas, make connections, and monitor your understanding. This self‑explanation is a metacognitive skill associated with deeper learning. It is one form of active learning – along with practice problems and discussion – that strengthens mastery.

Summarization as a service to a reader. Here the goal is not to learn by summarizing but to convey information to someone else (or to your future self) succinctly. This includes chapter abstracts, lecture notes, journalistic summaries, or AI-generated bullet points. The skill required is different: one must accurately capture the author’s point while assuming the audience won’t (or can’t) read the full source.

Writing summaries as a learning strategy is effective when done well. As noted, Bretzing and Kulhavy (1979) found that students who wrote summaries after reading outperformed those who merely copied or scanned text. The act of “writing about the important points in one’s own words produced a benefit over and above…selecting important information”. In principle, then, having students generate summaries should embed desirable difficulty into the process. However, Dunlosky et al. caution that the benefits hinge on the quality of the summary; without instruction, some learners may fail to pinpoint the main ideas or may regurgitate details without rephrasing. Thus, learning to summarize effectively is itself a worthwhile skill, much like learning to solve math problems rather than just copying solutions.

When we read with a specific goal (e.g. to get the key points of a business report), however, generating a summary is the outcome, not the means. In this case, the cognitively efficient approach is to skip trivialities and extract content directly. Many business or self‑help books, for instance, are criticized for having low information density: much of the page count may be motivational anecdotes, lengthy intros, or repetition. It’s often quipped that the “meat” of the argument could be boiled down to a few pages or bullet points. (Indeed, many summaries of business books or popular science exist precisely because readers won’t slog through dozens of pages to learn a handful of ideas.) Similarly, a web article might be padded with ads, sidebar widgets, or author branding. This is exactly extraneous cognitive load. A good summary — whether human or AI-generated — removes these layers, delivering only the core claims, evidence, and definitions. For such texts, the goal is knowledge consumption, not skill practice. Why should a busy manager spend time decoding an infomercial disguised as a book if a concise summary gives the same insight?

AI summarizers excel at this kind of filtering. A well-constructed algorithm can ignore stock phrases like “in this era of unprecedented change” or extraneous hypotheticals, and focus on the factual assertions and logic. In terms of cognitive load theory, we are letting AI handle the extraneous load so that the human learner can apply their cognitive resources to the intrinsic content. Indeed, Sweller et al. emphasize that when material is already complex, designers should minimize unnecessary load. This means eliminating not only typos or unclear wording, but whole passages that do not advance the author’s thesis. In practice, human summarizers do this by choosing which paragraphs to ignore. AI can do the same automatically and at scale.

Of course, there are trade-offs. If one’s goal was to develop summarizing skill or to engage deeply with the text, skipping ahead to a summary bypasses those learning opportunities. Moreover, a summary removes the sequence in which ideas were presented, which can also carry meaning. Teaching literature or philosophy, for example, often emphasizes the author’s own words, structure, and style; summarizing might strip away the artistry. But even in those cases, one might accept a summary as a preview (to decide if the text is worth reading) while planning to do the actual hard work of reading later.

In short, summarization can be both training and output. We should encourage students to practice making summaries (as with any study strategy), because that builds comprehension skills. At the same time, we should recognize that in everyday intellectual work, efficiency demands summaries as products — and AI can produce them. The key is to know which mode you are in: if practicing a subject, do it yourself; if informing yourself of facts, let the AI help.

## AI Summaries and Deep Comprehension

Recent research confirms that heavy reliance on AI summaries can alter learning outcomes. Bai et al. (2026) surveyed over six hundred university students using generative AI tools to summarize expository texts. They measured deep reading comprehension — the ability to infer, integrate, and critically evaluate, beyond mere fact recall. They found that frequent use of AI summarizers predicted lower deep comprehension, mediated by reduced elaborative processing. In other words, students who habitually consumed AI‑generated summaries instead of engaging the text did less internal “meaning-making” and therefore understood less well. Importantly, this effect was not uniform: highly motivated readers (those who wanted to understand deeply) showed no significant loss. Motivated students used the AI summaries as a supplement (scaffold), not a substitute, for mental processing.

The interpretation is clear: reading an AI summary can short-circuit the self-explanation and reflection that reading the full text would normally trigger. If the machine has already spliced the narrative into neat segments, the reader faces less pressure to connect the dots or re-state ideas in their own terms – tasks that foster deeper learning. This aligns with classic levels-of-processing theory: information is better retained when the learner engages with it semantically and self-referentially. If a fluent summary is available, readers may skip those deep-processing steps and simply skim the result.

Cognitive offloading theory gives a related perspective. People naturally outsource hard memory or reasoning tasks to external aids (notes, GPS, calculators). This is often adaptive in the moment, but at the cost of weaker internal learning. Studies show that simply knowing information is stored elsewhere leads to poorer recall of the information itself. Likewise, if a language model “reads” the text for you and gives you the answer, your brain does less work constructing the mental model. This can leave your comprehension shallower, a concern especially for complex material that requires inference (e.g. scientific texts, legal arguments, philosophical reasoning).

Moreover, the medium matters. A meta-analysis found that comprehension tends to be better when reading print than screen, especially for complex expository text under time pressure. Replacing the printed page with an AI summary is an even more radical change: the reader is not just on paper vs pixel, but on outline vs source. The object of attention is different, and the reader becomes more of an inspector (checking a summary) than an active constructor (building understanding from raw text). In short, AI summaries best support comprehension of facts and superficial structure, but risk undermining the deep reading processes of integration and critical judgment.

These findings do not imply that AI summaries are inherently bad. They do suggest that uncritical or overuse can be detrimental, especially if the learner is not motivated to engage deeply. But they also point the way to solutions: trained readers can compensate by remaining vigilant and perhaps using summary as a “second opinion” rather than the sole source. For example, a reader might first use AI to get the gist and identify key passages, then read those passages fully to build understanding. In other words, hybrid strategies (scanning by summary, then deep dive) can leverage the strengths of both approaches. As with any tool, it’s how one uses it (and with what purpose) that determines the outcome.

## Epistemic Dependence and the Trustworthiness of Summaries

All readers face an epistemic dilemma: to some extent, we must trust sources we cannot fully check. As John Hardwig famously argued, rational knowledge often requires trusting experts, because no individual can verify all evidence or methods for every claim. The layperson is typically the intellectual inferior in a specialized domain, so “refusing to think for oneself” in those areas – by relying on expert testimony or consensus – is not irrational but necessary. Hardwig goes so far as to say we may know things “known by a community but not by any individual knower”. In practice, this means students accept textbooks, scientific articles, or news reports largely on trust, guided by the reputation of the authors and the process (peer review, editorial oversight, fact-checking) that backs them.

When we turn to AI summaries, however, the usual trust warrants are absent or opaque. A human summary might come with an author’s credentials or a publisher’s name; an AI summary may come with none. And current language models can produce fluent prose that sounds authoritative even when it hallucinates or omits important details. Indeed, a recent study found that AI-generated summaries often leave out the most crucial information and sometimes introduce falsehoods. Readers exposed to a misleading AI summary were significantly more likely to form incorrect memories than those who saw an accurate summary, and this occurred regardless of whether they knew the summary was AI-generated. In short, AI summaries can inadvertently misinform if the model distorts the source content (intentionally or not).

This leads to the obvious question: How can one safely use AI summaries without first reading the original? If you have no way to check the summary against the source, you risk missing crucial points or absorbing errors. This is the “chicken-and-egg” problem: you don’t know if a summary is trustworthy unless you have at least skimmed the source, yet the summary is supposed to spare you the effort of reading.

Social epistemology offers a framework to address this. First, note that we already routinely rely on others for information far beyond our capacity to verify. Standard epistemic practice (following Reid, Goldman, Coady, Burge, etc.) is that testimony is presumptively credible, subject to defeaters like evident conflict or known bias. In ordinary life, we trust journalists, teachers, or colleagues based on general norms of honesty and mutual interest in truth. For example, the Stanford Epistemological Problems of Testimony entry observes that so long as people expect honesty and fear reputational loss, testimony generally “promises great overall epistemic benefits”. Most scientific communication is similarly regulated: replication, peer review, and publicity act as constraints, and surveys suggest serious fraud is rare. The expectation is that mistakes will be caught eventually, so in practice we accept published findings tentatively and adjust if contrary evidence emerges.

Analogously, trusting a summary requires precautionary norms. If the AI tool is well-known (e.g. a reputable academic assistant) and the user remains skeptical, we can treat its output as provisional: a preliminary guide that should be confirmed. Practical steps include checking multiple sources, verifying citations, or sampling the original text. For instance, a student might read the table of contents and introduction of a paper (where main claims often reside), then compare those with the summary’s claims. If any surprising assertion appears in the summary, the student should track it back to the source. This is akin to “proportional verification”: for routine facts one tolerates more abstraction, but for high-stakes claims one demands more direct evidence.

Moreover, our trust in summaries should be goal-relative. If the question is “What is Kant’s idea of the categorical imperative?”, an AI or human summary that gets the broad outline right might suffice for initial understanding. But if one’s goal is to do original Kantian scholarship, one must consult the Critique itself (and the relevant scholarship) to catch subtle context and to see how Kant argues. Historians might read a letter differently than a philosophy student. In that sense, AI summaries are another form of testimonial exchange – one more node in the chain of intellectual transmission. We should apply the same epistemic standards (credibility, plausibility, cross‑corroboration) as we do to any testimony.

As Reid emphasized long ago, some trust is inescapable, but it is rational when grounded in communal safeguards. Hardwig’s insight is still fresh: we must rely on others for expert information. But Hardwig and others also stress that trust is not blind obedience – we calibrate trust based on known competence and track record. Likewise, using AI summaries should be done with oversight: a reader who lacks summarization skills must at least develop critical sense to detect obvious omissions or biases in the summary. One does not have to verify every fact personally, but one should know when to doubt and how to double-check the crucial bits.

In practice, skilled readers often adopt a layered approach. For example, an academic often follows this pattern when surveying a new paper: (a) read the abstract or executive summary, (b) skim headings and topic sentences, (c) read the conclusion, (d) maybe glance at figures or tables, (e) then decide which parts to read in full. Similarly, one could use an AI summary as step (a) or (b), then approach the actual text as needed. This “progressive reading” strategy reduces wasted effort: you avoid reading entire papers that are irrelevant, but you do read carefully those that matter. In some sense, abstracts, book jackets, and review articles have always served as human-made “summaries” for this purpose. We only add AI as a more flexible, on-demand layer.

Thus, we see AI summaries as neither inherently trustworthy nor inherently malicious; they are akin to any second-hand report. We should treat them with informed caution and use them where appropriate. The danger lies in uncritical acceptance: if a summary feels authoritative, there is a temptation to stop questioning. Our responsibility is to retain a probing mindset. In the words of recent researchers: even with a flawed summary, the solution isn’t avoid AI entirely, but ensure “human reading” and verification remain part of the process. Summaries can accelerate understanding, but they should not replace the learner’s judgment.

## Reading Kant and the Layers of Meaning

To illustrate these trade-offs, consider the example of reading philosophical texts – say, Immanuel Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason. Is it better to slog through the original 18th-century German prose (or its English translation), to read a modern human-authored commentary or textbook chapter about Kant, or to ask ChatGPT for an explanation? Each approach has a place:

A history-of-philosophy scholar might want to engage Kant’s own words to trace how his argument unfolds and to evaluate his reasoning. For this purpose, relying on a summary is unsatisfactory; one must see the actual text (and perhaps the footnotes and translator’s commentary). The friction here – the old style and complexity – is part of the challenge of doing original philosophy. (This is akin to what Reid or Rorty lamented about losing tradition; Reid insisted on going back to “common sense” data, which in philosophy often means the original text.)

A philosophy student learning basic Kantian ethics, however, might gain more from a clear, modern exposition. A well-written secondary summary (from a trusted author or an AI) can make accessible what Kant’s difficult language meant. Here the friction of reading Kant’s dense sentences is not serving an essential pedagogical goal; it is an extraneous barrier. Reading a concise summary allows the student to grasp the concept more efficiently. Of course, once the concept is clear, reading short passages of Kant can add nuance. But the initial hurdle can be pragmatically lowered.

A casual reader or interdisciplinary researcher might only need a functional understanding of Kant’s idea for context or comparison. They will almost certainly opt for the summary first. The opportunity cost of going through the original (especially untranscribed) is not justified by the marginal benefit for their purposes.

This graduated approach mirrors how academia treats texts: a survey course uses anthologies and commentaries, while research-level work returns to primaries. AI summarization fits naturally into this scheme as a versatile intermediate. One could imagine an AI “textbook” on Kant that first provides a crisp summary of each section, then, if the reader is interested, points to the relevant quotes in the original. Already, many philosophy teachers encourage students to read primary sources, but often supplement them with secondary literature. AI can be seen as an extension of that tradition.

The key is to recognize the difference between acquiring conceptual knowledge and doing philosophy as a craft. The former benefits from accessible summaries; the latter requires wrestling with ideas. We should neither fetishize the grind of reading for its own sake nor abandon rigor. Summaries (human or AI) are shortcuts through the preliminary stages of learning, but they cannot substitute the judgment and creativity of philosophical thought. As one reviewer puts it, learning is not just “knowing about philosophy” but the activity of thinking philosophically – and that process inevitably involves effortful engagement with arguments.

## The AI Authorship Problem: Inflation and Trust

The arguments above about reading and summarizing applied to traditional human-authored texts. But today’s authors increasingly use AI to produce content in the first place. What are the implications?

First, there is a language-inflation effect. When an author uses AI as a writing partner or text-expander, the resulting prose often becomes more verbose and formulaic. AI tends to pad arguments with generic phrases (e.g. “in today’s world,” “studies show that”) and to blow up short notes into paragraphs. If multiple authors keep feeding such AI-generated text into new drafts or summaries, the result can be a cycle where the actual new knowledge grows slowly while the word count balloons. In practice, this means that an AI summary of an AI-written document may be doubly diluted: the original prose may already contain filler, and the summary may omit the core content or misstate it.

Second, AI authorship blurs accountability. A human author is responsible for the claims in their text; a human editor or publisher verifies sources. If a text is written by “AI,” who certifies its accuracy? Currently, no one. There have even been cases where authors knowingly passed off AI‑generated content as their own without supervision. The provenance and expertise behind each statement becomes uncertain. Contrast that with a scholarly summary: even if it’s not written by the original thinker, it is (at least nominally) the work of some responsible author or editorial team whose background we can check.

These concerns are reflected in recent regulatory discussions. OWASP’s emergent guidelines for language models highlight “hallucination” and misinformation as top risks. An authoritative AI-generated text could propagate an error through thousands of readers. In high-stakes fields (medicine, law, science) the consequences could be serious.

A final twist: if all authoring is done by AI, the very notion of a “document” as knowledge carrier is changing. Tony Bain (2026) provocatively asks, “What if documents are just the presentation layer?”. He imagines a world in which the content – the evidence, data, claims, logic – is stored in structured form, and the text we read (whether written by human or machine) is just a chosen formatting. In that world, we wouldn’t “write” the knowledge every time; we would update a knowledge base, and generate prose from it. This shift is already underway in some domains (see below). The irony is that AI might force us to adopt precisely this separation of knowledge and presentation.

## Separating Knowledge from Presentation: A New Design

Given these trends and problems, we propose an architectural solution: separate the knowledge layer from the presentation layer in all serious writing. This principle is inspired by software engineering (“single source of truth”) and by recent thinking on AI and publishing. The idea is to store knowledge in a structured form (claims, evidence, assumptions, definitions, provenance) and generate any narrative text from that underlying repository.

This is not mere science fiction. Yue Ping (2026) outlines a three‑dimensional framework for AI-based textbooks that explicitly includes a “knowledge layer” beneath a cognitive layer and a technical layer. Similarly, researchers at Stanford Medicine have built “Paper2Agent,” a system that converts a scientific paper (text, data, code) into an interactive AI agent. James Zou (Stanford) explains that instead of static pages, the agent represents the paper’s knowledge in modular form – essentially turning each section into a folder of structured information. Zou envisions papers not just as passive artifacts, but as “active embodiments of knowledge” that can answer questions or even create new hypotheses.

In practical terms, here is what a knowledge-oriented writing pipeline might look like:

Knowledge layer: Core information units – hypotheses, definitions, data points, evidentiary statements, logical inferences – are entered into a database or knowledge graph. Each unit is tagged with source, author, confidence level, and links to related units. Importantly, the units carry their provenance (which study said this, under what conditions?).

Logic/Argument layer: Relationships among claims (if X then Y; X explains Y; alternative explanations; objections, etc.) are encoded. This could involve argument mapping tools or formal logic systems. The result is a web of how the knowledge pieces fit together.

Pedagogical layer: This arranges the knowledge and logic into a curriculum or reading sequence. It can vary by audience: e.g. a tutorial versus an expert monograph. Here one decides what prerequisites to introduce first, where to insert examples, which analogies to draw, etc. These are still abstract rules or templates, not prose.

Presentation layer: Finally, the system generates or displays text, figures, and layouts from the above. Different “views” can be rendered: a textbook chapter, a slide deck, a summary bullet list, or even an interactive dialogue. Crucially, all these outputs are tied back to the same underlying knowledge pieces, so any summary can cite exactly which claims it draws on.

Such an architecture would dramatically reduce needless summarization. If the knowledge already lives in modular form, then generating a concise summary is just a matter of querying the knowledge layer and formatting the answer, rather than scanning a long document. AI could automatically produce personalized summaries: e.g. a 10-year-old’s level, or a summary focusing on financial data, or a TL;DR of methodology for statisticians. The user could also drill down: seeing a summary sentence and clicking to view the original claim and source. In effect, presentation and knowledge become decoupled.

This idea is already emerging in various fields. In journalism, fact-checkers and newsrooms use structured databases (fact boxes, timelines) to support multiple articles. In law, case-ontology databases link statutes, precedents, and commentary. Educational platforms like intelligent tutoring systems try to separate curriculum content from explanation text to adapt to student progress. Tony Bain’s blog (Sept 2026) encapsulates this: “documents became dominant because humans needed information organized… AI makes [the document-as-knowledge] distinction matter. The document is not necessarily the knowledge – it is often a representation designed for a human”. He argues that what we call a “paper” or “report” actually contains evidence, data, code, claims, and interpretation – all elements of an underlying knowledge asset.

If authors commit to this structure, authorship itself changes. Rather than writing from scratch, an author would curate the knowledge: enter new data, correct an existing claim, refine an argument structure. Then they would “render” these choices into prose for human readers. In that sense, the slogan becomes: Author the knowledge and logic; render the prose. This is a reversal of the old model (author prose and hope readers extract the knowledge). It promises two big advantages: first, transparency (any summary or excerpt can be traced back to its source pieces); second, efficiency (updates to knowledge auto-propagate to all formats, removing duplication).

One can imagine a workflow where a researcher writes the “content” (including counterpoints, uncertainties, methodologies) into a knowledge base, then the AI generates the journal article text and the press release and the lecture slides from that single source. Critically, the AI does not decide what to include – it just restates what the author/experts already specified. This preserves human editorial control. The AI becomes an assistant for formatting and expansion, not an independent writer.

Of course, readers cannot currently expect such a knowledge infrastructure for most domains – it is our proposal for the future. But we are moving in that direction. Already tools like semantic publishing, linked data, and interactive documents hint at it. As the Stanford news notes, Paper2Agent effectively does the reverse (flattening the paper into an AI form), but we can aspire to have authors publish the flattened knowledge first, then let both humans and machines build on it.

Importantly, this separation does not eliminate the need for human summarization skills. Even with a knowledge database, deciding which knowledge is relevant to your goal remains a human or at least a goal-specific judgment. A biology professor and a policy maker might both query a database of climate science but come away with different takeaways. Our proposal preserves that flexibility: the presentation layer can be tuned to different intents, but it must be requested. The human must still set the agenda (what question to ask). In other words, we delegate the mechanical summarizing to AI, but not the strategic deciding what matters.

## Limits, Nuance, and Objections

Our argument and architecture have clear limits. We end by acknowledging several:

Literary and aesthetic works. Poetry, novels, speeches – any genre where form is itself the substance – cannot be reduced without loss. The beauty of metaphor, the rhythm of phrasing, the author’s personal voice, or ambiguity that invites interpretation, are not separable from the “knowledge” content. For these, friction (slow reading) can be part of the experience and arguably part of understanding (e.g. savoring a well-crafted sentence). Our proposal is meant for expository, technical, or argumentative texts where information density is the priority.

Tacit and embodied knowledge. Some knowledge is not captured well in text at all. Think of learning to ride a bicycle, use social cues in conversation, or write good code style. These often require imitation, practice, or guidance, not summarization. A knowledge layer cannot easily encode muscle memory or cultural context. Moreover, even factual domains have tacit undercurrents: for example, an expert’s intuition or “feel” for a situation. AI or summaries may miss these. Thus, human mentorship, mentorship and apprenticeship are still needed for many skills.

Ambiguity and uncertainty. Structured knowledge systems tend to prefer clear true/false claims, but real texts often leave matters open or contested. Capturing nuance, caveats, or ongoing debates is challenging. If not done carefully, a summary might present a contested claim as if uncontroversial. We must guard against turning uncertainty into artificial certainty.

Bias and manipulation. If the knowledge layer itself is flawed (biased data, omitted counterarguments, commercial spin), then summaries will simply propagate that bias in compressed form. Especially with AI authoring, there is a risk that vested interests could flood the underlying knowledge bases with subtle propaganda, which then gets smoothly delivered to consumers. Transparency helps here: if sources are visible, readers (and auditors) can detect patterns of omission. But some biases (e.g. skewed dataset) might not be obvious. Vigilance and governance mechanisms will be needed.

Deskilling and dependency. As with calculators and GPS, offloading tasks to AI can erode skill over time. If students always rely on AI to summarize and never practice it themselves, they may become poor judges of a text’s content. This is why we stress learning summarization as a skill: not because everyone must be a professional summarizer, but so they can critically supervise the AI’s work. Educational curricula may need to adapt: for example, instead of grading every student’s summary for content, teachers might have students edit or critique AI summaries as exercises. This way, the skill of evaluation is maintained even if the mechanical summarizing is automated.

Access and equity. A knowledge infrastructure requires resources – data curation, software, stable platforms. If only elite institutions build these, the rest of the world might remain stuck with disorganized texts and low-quality summarizers. We should advocate for open, interoperable knowledge standards (like the open data and open access movements) to avoid creating a “knowledge divide.”

We also emphasize the distinction between normative proposal and empirical fact. We have drawn on empirical studies (e.g. on AI summarization and learning) to inform our argument, but the main thrust – that knowledge and presentation should be separated – is a proposal. There isn’t direct experimental evidence yet for that architecture; rather, we synthesize theory from education (cognitive load, desirable difficulties), epistemology (testimony, expertise), and computer science (separation of concerns) to build the case. We carefully qualify wherever the evidence is thin. For example, the 3‑page rule about business books is illustrative, not statistical: we don’t cite research on that point, we just use it as a plausible scenario of low information density.

Finally, an important subtlety: competence is not automatically verification. Even a competent summarizer or AI can err. Our emphasis on learning to summarize is to build evaluation competence, not naive trust. In other words, knowing how to write a good summary is one step; being able to judge someone else’s summary (which may omit or spin something) is another. Both require understanding how arguments are constructed and what is evidence. We argue that cultivating those skills is part of modern education: we should teach students not only to use AI tools but to scrutinize them.

## Conclusion

Intellectual work is most effective when we minimize unnecessary friction while preserving deliberate challenge. AI summarization, done wisely, can play both roles: it can strip away the irrelevant “friction” of redundant writing and advertisements, letting readers focus on the core ideas; and it can free up human effort for the demanding tasks that AI cannot do (critical analysis, creative thinking, cross‑discourse synthesis). But for AI summaries to be trustworthy, readers must have the competence to assess them. That competence stems from practicing summarization, argument reconstruction, and source checking – in short, from thinking, not just consuming.

Moreover, to truly eliminate avoidable summarization overhead, we must rethink how knowledge is authored. We echo Tony Bain’s vision: AI is exposing “the awkwardness of the current model” where knowledge is flattened into static documents. A better model is to architect information so that documents (papers, books, articles) are views on a shared knowledge base, not the sole repositories. In such a world, a student might seldom need a separate summary, because the information could be retrieved and rendered exactly at their needed level of detail.

This is an ambitious goal, but even incremental steps move us toward it. Already, many educators use concept maps, annotated bibliographies, and structured notes to formalize knowledge. AI tools can accelerate that process. For example, one could use a generative model to parse a lecture transcript into bullet points of claims and examples, or to translate an empirical result into a “scientific claim” entry. Each such step builds the knowledge layer.

To frame it in our own words: Learn to summarize so that you can judge summaries. Use AI to summarize so that you can spend more time thinking. Separate knowledge from presentation so that unnecessary summarization is no longer required. Or as we might put it in principle: we should author knowledge and render prose, rather than author prose and ask every reader to extract the knowledge – while acknowledging that sometimes, of course, the form is the substance (as in poetry or in complex argumentation that can’t be linearly parsed).

The ultimate aim is epistemic efficiency: to allocate our cognitive effort where it pays off, to trust others (and machines) to take care of what they can do reliably, and to maintain human oversight and interpretive capacity. By distinguishing friction from productive difficulty, and by building systems that embody that distinction, we hope to guide a middle path: one neither fearful of AI nor complacent about our own responsibility. In the best case, AI and human readers become a partnership: machines handle routine condensation, and people do the synthesis, judgment, and creativity that machines cannot. In the words of the old analogy: let the machines grind the corn, while we enjoy the bread.
