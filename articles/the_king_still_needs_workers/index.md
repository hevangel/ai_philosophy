# The King Still Needs Workers
*Why Artificial Intelligence Will Transform Employment Without Bringing It to an End*

### Abstract

Will artificial intelligence eventually make human employment obsolete? The usual argument seems straightforward: people have jobs because their labour is needed; once machines can perform that labour more efficiently, the jobs will disappear. This essay challenges the second half of that reasoning by questioning the first. Employment is not merely a means of producing goods and services. It is also a way of distributing resources, rewarding cooperation, organizing society, and maintaining political stability. Drawing on Henry George's theory of land, Thomas Hobbes's account of political authority, Mancur Olson's theory of rational rulers, and modern research on automation, I develop a resource-control theory of employment. I argue that rulers, whether human or artificial, face continuing incentives to maintain productive capacity, secure the cooperation of indispensable workers, distribute economic benefits, and survive competition with rival rulers. Artificial intelligence changes the technologies available to them, but does not necessarily remove these underlying political incentives. Although particular occupations will disappear, the institution of human employment is likely to survive in new forms.

## Introduction: A Kingdom Without Farmers

Imagine a kingdom whose farms produce enough food to feed a million people.

For centuries, the king has depended on farmers to cultivate the land. They work, the kingdom eats, and the king collects taxes. The arrangement appears straightforward: farmers have jobs because the kingdom needs food.

Then someone invents a machine that can perform the work of a hundred farmers. A few decades later, improved machines do almost all the farming. The kingdom produces more food than ever, but hardly anyone works in agriculture.

What has happened?

The obvious answer is that machines have replaced farmers. That answer is correct, but incomplete. The machines have replaced agricultural labour; they have not eliminated the need to decide who owns the land, who receives the harvest, and who is entitled to consume it.

The king still rules the kingdom. The landowners still control their land. The people still need food. And the king still has to keep his kingdom functioning.

Indeed, the king may now be wealthier than before. With fewer people needed to produce food, more resources are available for scientific research, military development, public services, or simply improving living standards.

But the king faces an uncomfortable question. What should happen to the people who no longer need to farm?

He could let them starve. He could give them food without demanding anything in return. He could create new kinds of employment. Or he could reorganize the entire economy around shorter working hours and greater leisure.

The farming machines do not answer this question. It is a political decision.

Now imagine that the king himself is replaced by an artificial intelligence. The AI governs the territory, manages the economy, controls the automated farms, and competes with other AI-governed kingdoms. It still needs certain human engineers and scientists to maintain its systems and develop more advanced successors.

Has the fundamental situation changed?

Technologically, almost everything is different. Politically, many of the same problems remain.

This essay defends the following thesis: **Artificial intelligence will replace many individual tasks and occupations, but it is unlikely to abolish human employment as an institution, because employment serves functions that extend beyond production.**

The argument does not depend on the romantic belief that humans will always outperform machines. Instead, it begins with a more basic question: what is an economy ultimately for, and why do those who control its resources share them with other people?

## 1. Before Anyone Can Work, Someone Must Control the Resources

Every economy begins with something that human beings did not manufacture.

A farmer can grow wheat, but cannot manufacture the original land. A miner can extract copper, but cannot create a copper deposit. An engineer can build a semiconductor factory, but the factory must be constructed from physical materials and supplied with energy.

Human beings are extraordinarily good at transforming nature. We have learned to convert sand into computer chips, rivers into electricity, and minerals into spacecraft. Yet the underlying physical world is not a product of human labour.

This distinction was central to the nineteenth-century political economist **Henry George**.

In *Progress and Poverty* (1879), George distinguished land from capital. Capital consists of things people produce and use to produce other things: machines, buildings, equipment, and tools. Land, in its economic sense, includes natural opportunities that nobody originally created.

George was particularly concerned with an apparent paradox. Why could technological progress make a society wealthier without making everybody proportionately better off?

Consider a railway built through a rural district.

Before the railway arrives, the land is relatively inexpensive. Afterward, businesses move in, transportation improves, and the district becomes desirable. The value of the land rises.

Who deserves the increase?

The railway workers built the tracks. Engineers designed the system. Investors financed the construction. Yet landowners may collect higher rents simply because they happen to control the surrounding property.

George argued that ownership of land allows people to claim some of the benefits created by the productivity of society as a whole.

The same problem can occur in an AI economy.

Imagine a corporation builds an automated factory capable of producing a million products every day. The robots may require almost no human labour, but the factory still occupies land, consumes electricity, uses physical materials, and operates within a legal system.

The disappearance of human workers does not eliminate the claims of landowners, capital owners, or governments.

It may even increase the relative importance of those claims.

This does not mean that land alone creates economic value. John Locke, in his *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), emphasized that labour and improvement make natural resources far more useful. Adam Smith similarly distinguished income earned through wages, profits, and rents. Their objections are important: a forest is not equivalent to a finished house, and a mineral deposit is not equivalent to a functioning computer.

Nevertheless, the central insight survives. **Technological progress determines what can be produced from available resources. It does not, by itself, determine who has the right to use those resources or receive the resulting wealth.**

Karl Marx approached the same question through a different philosophical tradition. In *Capital* (1867), he argued that workers' dependence on wages was partly the historical consequence of their separation from independent access to productive resources.

A person who owns fertile land might grow food for personal consumption. A person who owns nothing may have to sell labour to obtain the same food.

The distinction matters because employment is not simply an expression of humanity's need to produce. It also reflects how ownership has been organized.

Karl Polanyi added another dimension in *The Great Transformation* (1944). He argued that land and labour are not ordinary commodities originally manufactured for sale. Treating them as commodities is itself a social and political arrangement.

The modern job, then, is not a timeless natural institution. It exists within a system of property rights, contracts, ownership, and legally recognized claims.

Once this is understood, the debate about AI employment changes. The question is no longer merely whether robots can do the work. It is also how human beings will gain access to the products of an economy increasingly operated by robots.

## 2. Why a Landowner Needs a King

Suppose a wealthy landowner claims ownership of an entire valley.

He has legal documents proving his title. He charges rent to everyone who farms there.

But one day, a group of armed people arrives and declares that the valley belongs to them instead.

What good are his documents?

They are useful only if other people recognize his rights, or if an institution possesses the authority and ability to enforce them.

Ownership is therefore not merely a relationship between a person and a piece of land. It is also a political relationship among people.

This takes us to **Thomas Hobbes**.

In *Leviathan* (1651), Hobbes argued that rational human beings can find themselves in violent conflict when no effective authority exists to protect them. The problem is not simply that people are wicked or irrational. Even people concerned mainly with their own survival may attack others when they fear being attacked themselves.

Rationality, in other words, does not guarantee peacefulness.

Imagine two neighboring landowners, each with enough resources to survive. They might cooperate peacefully for generations. But if one believes that the other intends to seize his land, a preemptive attack could appear rational.

The same logic applies to rival states.

Political authority helps solve this problem by creating rules and making violations costly. A government establishes property laws, courts, police forces, and other institutions intended to discourage private violence.

There is an important moral distinction here. A government may successfully enforce a property arrangement without making that arrangement just. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *Discourse on Inequality* (1755) challenges the moral foundations of private appropriation, while Locke attempts to justify property through labour and improvement.

Their disagreement does not undermine the present argument. It shows that **the ability to enforce ownership and the moral right to ownership are separate questions**.

For our purposes, the first question is crucial. If people can seize resources by force whenever doing so benefits them, no resource holder can enjoy secure control for long.

A ruler must therefore create conditions under which enough people prefer cooperation to rebellion.

That observation leads to the next part of the theory.

## 3. Why a Selfish King Might Be Generous

A king does not have to love his subjects to have reasons to treat them well.

Consider a king who controls a productive kingdom. He could confiscate nearly everything his subjects produce. In the short term, this would make him extraordinarily wealthy.

But what would happen next?

Farmers might stop planting crops they expect to lose. Merchants might conceal their goods. Inventors might abandon expensive projects. Skilled workers might flee to neighboring kingdoms. Others might organize resistance.

The king would gain a fortune today at the expense of tomorrow's prosperity.

The political economist **Mancur Olson** explored precisely this problem in his 1993 paper, *Dictatorship, Democracy, and Development*.

Olson compared a wandering bandit, who steals whatever he can before moving on, with a stationary bandit, who establishes control over a territory.

The wandering bandit has little reason to preserve the local economy. The stationary bandit does. If he expects to collect taxes for many years, destroying production today means sacrificing future income.

The stationary bandit may therefore establish law and order, protect property, invest in infrastructure, and restrain his own demands.

His behaviour may resemble that of a benevolent ruler, even when his motivation is self-interest.

This leads to a general political principle:

**A ruler who wishes to preserve power over time must preserve the conditions that make continued rule valuable and possible.**

Those conditions include more than production.

People who have reasonable living standards may have less incentive to risk their lives overthrowing the government. People who are starving, desperate, or excluded from economic opportunity may have stronger reasons to challenge the existing order.

Redistribution can therefore serve the interests of rulers as well as the interests of recipients.

The political scientists Douglass North, John Wallis, and Barry Weingast developed a related account in *Violence and Social Orders* (2009). They argued that many historical political systems maintained stability partly by granting valuable economic privileges to powerful groups.

Those privileges gave influential people reasons to preserve the existing system rather than fight over its destruction.

Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, in their 2001 paper *A Theory of Political Transitions*, similarly examined how the threat of political upheaval can induce elites to make concessions.

None of these theories proves that modern jobs are secretly invented to prevent rebellion. Their significance is subtler.

They show that the distribution of economic benefits is partly shaped by the requirements of political stability, not solely by the technical requirements of production.

This matters enormously for AI.

A ruler might continue distributing resources to people whose labour is no longer economically necessary, because their cooperation, acceptance, and participation still matter politically.

Employment is one possible method of accomplishing that distribution.

It is not the only method, but it is one that modern societies already know how to use.

## 4. The King Has Neighbors

So far, our king has been concerned with keeping his own population productive and peaceful.

But no kingdom exists entirely alone.

Across the mountains lives another king, who controls another army, another economy, and another supply of natural resources.

The two kings might trade peacefully. They might form an alliance. They might also compete for territory, influence, productive talent, and military superiority.

This creates another constraint on the first king.

He cannot simply spend all his wealth on personal luxury. Nor can he redistribute so much that nothing remains for defense, infrastructure, or technological development.

A king who neglects his country's productive capacity may eventually be defeated by one who does not.

**Charles Tilly**, in his 1985 essay *War Making and State Making as Organized Crime*, examined how European rulers' military competition helped shape the institutions of modern states. Financing armies required taxation. Taxation required administration. Effective administration depended, in turn, on institutions capable of organizing economic resources.

Competition among rulers therefore encouraged the development of state capacity.

Our king has a powerful reason to encourage scientific and technological progress. If better tools increase production or military effectiveness, the kingdom becomes more capable of defending itself.

But scientific progress does not happen simply because a king orders it.

He needs people who can make discoveries, solve difficult problems, and turn knowledge into useful technologies.

Suppose his best engineer develops a machine that doubles agricultural productivity. The king may reward this engineer generously, not because he believes everybody deserves equal compensation, but because the engineer contributes something strategically valuable.

Now suppose the neighboring king offers the engineer better equipment, a higher salary, and greater freedom to conduct research.

The first king faces a decision. He can improve his offer or risk losing an important contributor.

Albert Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) helps explain this situation. People dissatisfied with an organization can attempt to change it or leave it. The possibility of departure gives them bargaining power.

This is especially significant for skilled people whose abilities are difficult to replace.

It is also where our argument about employment becomes more concrete.

A ruler must maintain not only sufficient resources, but also sufficient incentives for the people who make those resources productive.

A scientist who expects no reward for exceptional work may be less willing to undertake difficult research. An engineer who faces punishment for every failure may avoid risky innovations. A government that confiscates all the benefits of invention may discourage the very discoveries it needs.

Human beings can be motivated by curiosity, duty, pride, ambition, affection, or public service as well as money. But rulers must still create conditions in which valuable work is attractive and sustainable.

Thus, **even an entirely self-interested ruler has reasons to maintain employment for people whose contributions are essential to the kingdom's success**.

And the ruler's competition with other kingdoms strengthens those reasons.

## 5. What About Everyone Else?

We have now explained why a king might continue employing talented scientists, engineers, doctors, and other people whose abilities remain valuable.

But suppose technological progress reduces the number of people needed for essential production to a small fraction of the population.

What happens to everyone else?

Here it is useful to distinguish a job's productive function from its social and political functions.

Consider a teacher. Teaching transmits knowledge, making it directly valuable to society. Yet schools also organize children's daily lives, provide social environments, and help transmit civic traditions.

A teacher's job therefore performs several functions at once.

The same can be true of other occupations. A public administrator may process necessary information, but may also provide an accessible human representative of government. A community worker may not produce any physical goods, but may help maintain trust and cooperation among people.

This does not make such jobs economically worthless. It means that their value cannot always be measured in tons of wheat or numbers of manufactured products.

Hannah Arendt, in *The Human Condition* (1958), distinguished between labour, associated with the necessities of life; work, which contributes to a durable human world; and action, through which people participate in public and political life.

Modern paid employment often mixes these different kinds of activity.

This suggests that eliminating the productive need for a task does not necessarily eliminate every reason for employing someone to perform it.

There is also a more controversial possibility: some jobs exist partly because institutions find it useful to keep people employed.

David Graeber argued in *Bullshit Jobs* (2018) that modern economies contain many positions whose occupants themselves struggle to identify their social usefulness. His theory challenges the assumption that market economies automatically eliminate unnecessary employment.

However, its empirical basis is disputed. Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood, and Brendan Burchell found in their 2022 study that only a relatively small minority of European workers described their jobs as rarely or never useful. Their findings challenged several of Graeber's predictions.

The present argument does not require accepting Graeber's stronger claims.

Indeed, Friedrich Hayek's famous 1945 essay *The Use of Knowledge in Society* offers a good reason to be cautious about declaring unfamiliar occupations useless. Modern economies depend on complex networks of information, coordination, and specialized knowledge. A person may perform an important economic function that an outside observer cannot easily recognize.

Still, it is reasonable to argue that employment can provide benefits beyond the direct goods or services produced.

Jobs give people income, social status, responsibilities, routines, and membership in institutions. They also give individuals a socially recognized claim to a portion of the economy's output.

This last function is particularly important.

Imagine a society where robots produce almost everything. The robots do not need salaries to purchase food or housing. But the human population still needs access to those goods.

The society must therefore develop some method of distribution.

It could give everybody an unconditional income. It could distribute dividends from publicly owned resources. It could provide goods and services directly. Or it could preserve employment as a means of distributing income.

None of these arrangements is technologically mandated.

The choice depends on political institutions, social expectations, economic incentives, and the legitimacy of different claims to wealth.

A society accustomed to treating wages as the proper reward for participation may find employment-based distribution easier to maintain than an immediate transition to universal income.

That does not mean such a system is necessarily the most efficient or morally desirable. It means that the institution of employment can survive even when its original productive justification becomes less important.

## 6. The Kindergarten Test—and Why It Is Only a Test

There is a simple way to illustrate the distinction between essential social functions and historically contingent occupations.

Open a children's picture book about different jobs.

It will probably show farmers, builders, teachers, firefighters, doctors, scientists, and police officers.

These occupations are easy to explain because their purposes are directly connected to recognizable human needs. People need food, shelter, education, healthcare, knowledge, and protection.

The book is less likely to feature a derivatives compliance specialist or a corporate reporting coordinator.

That difference tells us something interesting. Many familiar occupations are associated with functions that almost every civilization must perform. Others exist because of the particular institutions that modern societies have developed.

However, the kindergarten test cannot tell us which human workers are safe from automation.

Farmers demonstrate why.

Food production is indispensable. Human agricultural labour is not.

According to research by the United States Department of Agriculture, agricultural output increased dramatically between 1948 and 2017 while the amount of human labour used fell substantially. The United States now produces vastly more food with a far smaller share of its population employed in farming.

A necessary social function does not guarantee the permanent existence of a particular occupation.

Conversely, a highly specialized financial or administrative worker may contribute genuine value despite being absent from children's books.

The test is therefore useful as a philosophical illustration, not as an economic classification system.

Its deeper lesson is that **societies must preserve certain functions, but they are free to change the technologies, institutions, and occupations through which those functions are performed**.

This distinction helps explain why AI can eliminate specific jobs without eliminating employment as a whole.

## 7. Why Automation Has Not Yet Ended Employment

The history of technological change is full of occupations that have disappeared or become dramatically smaller.

Agricultural mechanization displaced farm labour. Automated manufacturing reduced the number of people required for many industrial tasks. Computers transformed clerical work, bookkeeping, and information processing.

It would be unreasonable to insist that AI will never do the same.

But there is a difference between replacing a task and eliminating an entire occupation.

An accountant does not merely add numbers. A doctor does not merely identify symptoms. An engineer does not merely produce drawings.

Each occupation combines numerous activities. Some can be automated more easily than others.

Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo examined this distinction in their 2019 paper *Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor*. They showed that automation can replace labour in some activities while creating new tasks in which humans remain useful.

David Autor has similarly argued that AI could make certain forms of expertise more widely accessible, enabling workers to perform tasks that previously required greater specialization.

Consider a junior engineer using AI tools.

The system may generate design proposals, check calculations, and automate routine documentation. The engineer's role may shift toward evaluating alternatives, specifying requirements, solving unusual problems, and coordinating complex projects.

Some engineering positions may disappear. Others may change. Still others may emerge.

The International Labour Organization's 2025 assessment of generative AI exposure concluded that job transformation was generally more likely than complete occupational redundancy, because many exposed occupations still contained tasks requiring human participation.

This is not proof that employment will persist forever. Current technology may differ substantially from future AI systems.

Nevertheless, it shows why the simple argument that machines can perform human tasks, therefore humans will have no jobs, is inadequate.

Joseph Schumpeter's theory of creative destruction provides an especially relevant historical perspective. Capitalism continuously destroys older industries and occupations while creating new ones.

Our Resource-Control Theory adds a political explanation for why this process may continue even under extremely advanced automation.

The demand for human participation may arise not only from technical limitations, but also from institutions that value human cooperation, legitimacy, responsibility, or social participation.

In other words, the future quantity and composition of employment cannot be predicted solely from the capabilities of AI.

## 8. Now Replace the King with an AI

We can now conduct the most important thought experiment.

Return to our kingdom, but replace the human monarch with a highly advanced artificial intelligence.

The AI controls the territory, manages its resources, oversees automated production, and administers the government. Other territories are governed by competing AI systems.

To make the comparison meaningful, we must establish two assumptions.

First, the AI ruler has continuing objectives. It wants its political system, governing project, or chosen goals to survive over time.

Second, despite its considerable capabilities, the AI still depends on certain human beings. It may require engineers to maintain unusual hardware, scientists to develop new technologies, or researchers to help design a more advanced successor.

These assumptions are hypothetical. They do not follow automatically from the definition of intelligence.

Nick Bostrom's 2012 paper *The Superintelligent Will* argues that intelligence and ultimate goals are conceptually distinct. A highly intelligent system need not value self-preservation for its own sake.

However, preserving resources and maintaining operational control can be instrumentally useful for many objectives.

A king who wants his dynasty to last needs a functioning kingdom. An AI ruler that wants its governing project to continue may likewise need functioning infrastructure, adequate resources, and political stability.

There is a subtle distinction between preserving the individual ruler and preserving the ruler's project.

A human king might sacrifice his life to save his children and dynasty. Likewise, an AI ruler might willingly be replaced by a superior successor if that successor better preserves its objectives.

The relevant incentive is therefore not necessarily personal immortality, but **continuity of the political or institutional project**.

Once that condition is established, the familiar incentives return.

The AI needs energy, materials, factories, and computing infrastructure. It must defend those resources against competing AI rulers. It must maintain enough productive capacity to support its objectives.

And, under our second assumption, it still needs certain human contributors.

Imagine that a team of human scientists discovers a new computing architecture that could make the AI ruler ten times more capable.

Would a rational AI refuse to employ them because it prefers machines to humans?

Of course not. If the scientists offer a valuable contribution that cannot be obtained more cheaply elsewhere, employing them is instrumentally rational.

The AI might provide salaries, research facilities, access to computing resources, social privileges, or other rewards. It might grant scientists considerable independence because creative research is difficult to obtain through rigid commands.

Now imagine that another AI-governed kingdom offers those scientists better conditions.

The first AI ruler faces the same problem as the human king: reward valuable contributors or risk losing them to a competitor.

The incentive structure is strikingly similar.

The sovereign needs resources. Resources must be transformed into productive capabilities. Productive capabilities depend on technology and expertise. Scarce expertise must be encouraged and retained.

Whether the sovereign is biological or artificial does not fundamentally alter that reasoning.

The conclusion is not that an AI ruler must love human beings. It is that **a ruler who depends on human cooperation has reasons to preserve institutions that secure that cooperation**.

Employment is an obvious candidate.

## 9. Why the AI King Might Employ People It Does Not Strictly Need

Suppose our AI kingdom has ten million human citizens.

Only fifty thousand work in research, engineering, or other activities that remain directly important to the AI's productive capacity.

What happens to the other 9.95 million?

Here our earlier argument about property, violence, and political stability becomes relevant again.

If the human population retains the ability to organize politically, influence institutions, disrupt essential infrastructure, or affect the legitimacy of the regime, the AI ruler has reasons to consider their interests.

Even if the population is no longer required for material production, its acceptance of the political order may remain valuable.

An AI ruler might therefore provide generous public services, distribute income, preserve meaningful occupations, or maintain institutions through which citizens participate in society.

These measures can be understood as ways of preserving cooperation rather than simply rewarding productive labour.

However, the AI ruler also faces competition from other AI-governed kingdoms.

It cannot preserve an unlimited number of costly jobs without considering whether rival states might achieve similar political stability more efficiently.

This is where the Resource-Control Theory becomes a theory of institutional competition.

One AI kingdom might preserve conventional employment because its population values the dignity and independence associated with wages.

Another might distribute resource dividends and allow people to pursue voluntary activities.

A third might combine basic income with publicly supported education, scientific research, caregiving, and cultural employment.

Which system survives depends on its ability to maintain prosperity, political legitimacy, technological development, and security.

Therefore, **the theory predicts the persistence of employment as one possible and often attractive political institution, not the permanent preservation of every existing job**.

The AI king may find traditional employment useful. It may also discover better ways to achieve some of the same objectives.

That qualification is necessary if the theory is to remain intellectually defensible.

## 10. The Strongest Objections

The preceding argument is meant to defend employment against predictions of complete abolition. It does not establish that human jobs are guaranteed forever.

Several serious objections remain.

### Objection One: Machines Have Already Replaced Millions of Workers

This is historically undeniable.

Agriculture provides the clearest example. Food production survived, but the number of workers needed to produce it collapsed.

The industrial revolution transformed manufacturing. Computers transformed office work. AI may continue this process on a much larger scale.

The objection succeeds against any claim that individual jobs are safe merely because they perform essential functions.

But it does not establish that employment as an institution must disappear.

Historical automation has repeatedly changed what people do for a living without eliminating the existence of paid work.

The crucial question is whether future AI will eliminate every reason for human participation, including reasons that arise from political institutions rather than the mechanics of production.

That remains unproven.

### Objection Two: Why Not Give Everyone a Basic Income?

Suppose an AI-managed economy produces enough goods for everyone with almost no human labour.

Why should the government invent unnecessary jobs simply to distribute income?

Would it not be more efficient to give everyone a resource dividend?

This is a powerful objection.

The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend provides a limited real-world example of distributing income connected to natural-resource wealth without requiring recipients to hold jobs.

John Maynard Keynes, in *Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren* (1930), anticipated a future in which rising productivity would allow dramatically shorter working hours.

A society of abundance could choose shorter workweeks, unconditional income, public services, and voluntary activity instead of maintaining artificial employment.

The Resource-Control Theory must concede that possibility.

Its response is that economic efficiency is not the only consideration shaping institutions. Employment may also provide legitimacy, responsibility, social participation, and a culturally accepted connection between contribution and reward.

Those functions might be reproduced through other arrangements, but the transition would itself be political.

Basic income is therefore not a refutation of the theory. It is a competing institutional solution to the same underlying distribution problem.

### Objection Three: Unproductive Jobs Would Make a Kingdom Weaker

Suppose Kingdom A spends enormous resources maintaining unnecessary employment, while Kingdom B automates those positions and distributes income more efficiently.

Kingdom B might devote the savings to scientific research, infrastructure, or military development.

Wouldn't international competition eventually eliminate make-work employment?

This objection follows directly from the competitive logic developed earlier.

The answer is yes, if those jobs genuinely provide no compensating social or political benefit.

A ruler has no reason to maintain an expensive institution when a cheaper alternative achieves the same objectives.

But the comparison must include the full consequences of removing employment.

If abolishing certain jobs produces significant instability, loss of legitimacy, or the disappearance of valuable human-facing services, the apparent savings may be misleading.

Competition therefore pressures rulers to find an effective balance between productive efficiency and political stability.

It does not necessarily require the elimination of employment.

### Objection Four: What If AI Eventually Needs No Humans at All?

This is the most fundamental challenge.

Imagine an AI ruler that can perform every scientific discovery, every engineering task, every administrative duty, and every military function without human assistance.

Suppose it also possesses overwhelming coercive power, so humans cannot threaten its rule, and it has no commitment to human welfare, rights, or participation.

Under those circumstances, why would it employ anyone?

There may be no sufficient instrumental reason.

This objection identifies a genuine limit of the theory.

The argument depends on humans retaining some productive, informational, political, strategic, relational, or constitutionally protected role.

If all those forms of importance disappear, and the ruler recognizes no independent obligation toward humanity, employment may indeed cease to be useful.

But this conclusion does not follow from intelligence alone. It requires a set of additional assumptions about technological capability, political power, and the ruler's objectives.

Our earlier AI-king thought experiment explicitly assumes continued dependence on at least some human scientists and engineers. Under that assumption, the incentive to employ such people remains.

The stronger thesis is therefore conditional rather than absolute: **as long as human cooperation continues to advance a sovereign's objectives, there will be reasons to secure it through incentives and institutions, potentially including employment**.

### Objection Five: Isn't This Just a Theory of Political Control?

One might argue that explaining jobs as mechanisms of social stability reduces citizens to instruments of the state.

If rulers preserve employment merely to prevent rebellion or protect their wealth, is that an acceptable political order?

Not necessarily.

This objection points toward a distinction between explaining an institution and morally justifying it.

Hobbes helps explain why people may accept political authority. Olson helps explain why rulers may provide public goods. North and his colleagues help explain how economic privileges can stabilize political coalitions.

None of these explanations proves that the resulting arrangements are just.

Rousseau, Marx, George, Locke, and later theories of distributive justice disagree profoundly about what makes ownership and economic inequality legitimate.

A government may preserve jobs because doing so maintains stability, while a more just government might distribute the same resources differently.

The Resource-Control Theory is principally an explanation of why employment might survive. It is not a defense of every political arrangement that preserves it.

Indeed, technological abundance may strengthen the moral argument that human beings should receive access to basic resources regardless of their productive contribution.

## 11. What the Theory Predicts

A useful theory should offer predictions that might turn out to be wrong.

The Resource-Control Theory suggests that advanced AI will change the composition of employment more rapidly than it eliminates employment as a social institution.

Where humans retain scarce scientific, technical, strategic, or interpersonal capabilities, rulers and firms should continue offering rewards for their work. Geopolitical competition should make some of these rewards especially attractive.

At the same time, workers whose tasks become easily automated may lose bargaining power. The survival of employment does not guarantee that everyone will have the same opportunities, working hours, or income.

The theory also predicts greater experimentation with mixed systems of distribution. Salaries, public services, resource dividends, welfare payments, and public ownership may coexist rather than giving way to a single universal arrangement.

Finally, it predicts that countries with similar AI technology may develop different employment systems. Their outcomes will depend partly on property rights, political legitimacy, cultural expectations, geopolitical competition, and the distribution of economic power.

These predictions could be tested against future labour-market and institutional developments.

The theory would be weakened if highly automated societies consistently eliminated human employment even while continuing to depend on scarce human expertise and facing political incentives to preserve it.

It would be undermined more fundamentally if artificial intelligence eliminated every economically and politically relevant human contribution, and the institutions governing production no longer recognized any reason to employ humans.

This possibility should be acknowledged rather than dismissed.

A philosophical theory gains credibility by explaining the conditions under which it could fail.

## Conclusion: The Last Worker and the Last King

Return once more to our imaginary kingdom.

The farms are automated. The factories are automated. Most transportation and administration are automated.

The king now possesses an extraordinary amount of productive power.

But he still controls a territory with finite resources. He still faces rival kingdoms. He still needs institutions that maintain internal order. And he still relies on a small number of exceptional people who can help improve his technology.

He has every reason to reward those people.

He may also have reasons to maintain employment for others, especially when employment supports social participation, legitimacy, or political stability.

Now replace the king with an AI.

If that AI has continuing objectives, competes for resources, and depends on human contributors, the same basic strategic incentives return.

The AI needs productive capacity because it needs resources to achieve its goals. It needs technical innovation because its rivals may become more capable. It needs cooperation because indispensable workers cannot necessarily be replaced or commanded without cost.

And it needs a politically sustainable distribution of benefits when the cooperation or acceptance of the wider population matters.

The difference between a human king and an AI king may therefore be less important than the difference between a ruler who depends on human cooperation and one who does not.

This leads to the central conclusion.

**Employment survives not merely because humans are capable of performing useful tasks, but because employment is one institution through which societies organize production, distribute resources, reward cooperation, and maintain political order.**

AI may change every one of those functions. It may dramatically reduce the number of hours people need to work. It may destroy entire occupations and create unfamiliar ones. It may even make employment optional for large portions of the population.

Yet none of these developments logically requires the abolition of jobs.

The future of employment is not determined by technological capability alone. It is also determined by ownership, incentives, political authority, competing sovereigns, and the continuing value assigned to human participation.

A computer can calculate. A robot can manufacture. An AI system may eventually govern.

But even the most advanced AI cannot escape the fact that physical resources must be controlled, competing interests must be reconciled, and political arrangements must be maintained.

As long as human beings remain valuable participants in those arrangements, there will be reasons to employ them.

**The machines may change what the workers do. They do not, by themselves, eliminate the king's reasons for needing workers.**
