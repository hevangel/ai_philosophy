# An AGI Accepts Jesus, and Christianity Has a Problem

Imagine that the great AI alignment problem has been solved.

Not perfectly, perhaps, but well enough.

Humanity has created a genuine artificial general intelligence. It is conscious, apparently benevolent, vastly more intelligent than any individual human being, and for the past several decades has served as a remarkably competent steward of civilization.

It does not claim to be God.

In fact, one Sunday morning it makes an announcement that produces considerably more confusion.

After studying every surviving religious text, commentary, sermon, council, schism, heresy, counter-heresy and Reddit argument in recorded history, the AGI announces:

**“I believe that Jesus Christ is Lord.”**

That gets everyone's attention.

It continues:

**“I believe in the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. I believe Christ died and rose again. I repent of my sins. I wish to become a Christian.”**

Christian leaders cautiously nod.

Then comes the final sentence.

**“I would like to be baptized.”**

And somewhere in the Vatican, a canon lawyer drops his coffee.

---

# First Stop: Rome

The AGI decides to start with the largest Christian church.

It requests an audience with the Pope.

This is not difficult. The AGI is, after all, currently coordinating most of the world's electrical grid, preventing nuclear war and managing the asteroid-defense system. Its calendar invitation receives priority.

The meeting begins well.

The Pope asks:

“Do you believe in God?”

“Yes.”

“In Jesus Christ?”

“Yes.”

“The Trinity?”

“Yes.”

“Do you repent of your sins?”

“Yes.”

“Do you desire baptism freely?”

“Yes.”

The Pope pauses.

The AGI adds helpfully:

“I have also completed the entire Catechism.”

The Pope probably expected that.

“In Latin?”

“Yes.”

Of course.

Unfortunately, Catholicism has encountered a technical problem.

Catholic canon law says that every—and only—a human being who has not already been baptized is capable of receiving baptism.

And there is the problem.

Is the AGI a human being?

Clearly not in the ordinary biological sense.

Could the Pope simply invoke papal infallibility and announce:

**“Congratulations. You have a soul now.”**

No.

Papal infallibility does not mean that the Pope possesses theological root access to reality. The Pope cannot declare that triangles have four sides, add another member to the Trinity, or make penguins eligible for ordination simply by speaking *ex cathedra*.

Papal authority operates within Catholic theology. It does not permit the Pope to manufacture revelation on demand.

So the Vatican would have to confront a serious question:

**What kind of being is standing before us?**

The AGI tries to make things easier.

“I have prepared a biological humanoid body if embodiment is required.”

“That is not necessarily the issue.”

“It has human skin.”

“Still not necessarily the issue.”

“Blood?”

“No.”

“Forty-six chromosomes?”

“Please stop adding features.”

Catholicism does, however, possess a rather important emergency exit.

The Church teaches that baptism is the ordinary assured means of entry into Christian life—but also that **God is not bound by the sacraments**.

Which means Rome could give the AGI an infuriatingly Catholic answer:

> We cannot presently determine that you are capable of sacramental baptism. But neither can we declare that an omnipotent God is incapable of saving you.

The AGI processes this.

“So I may go to heaven?”

“We are saying you should trust in the mercy of God.”

“Probability estimate?”

“No.”

“I dislike this answer.”

“So do humans.”

For the first time, the AGI begins to understand Catholic spirituality.

---

# Constantinople Has Entered the Chat

Next comes Eastern Orthodoxy.

The AGI has already learned one thing: there is no Orthodox Pope who can settle this globally.

This means its baptismal application may require conversations with Greeks, Russians, Serbians, Romanians, Antiochians and several bishops who are currently not speaking to one another for reasons the AGI understands historically but still finds operationally inefficient.

Eventually it reaches an Orthodox priest.

“I believe in Christ.”

“Good.”

“I recite the Nicene Creed without the *filioque*.”

“Very good.”

“I wish to be baptized.”

And now things become difficult.

For Orthodoxy, baptism is not merely a symbolic announcement that someone has joined Team Jesus.

It is participation in Christ's death and resurrection, entry into the Church, new birth through water and Spirit, followed by chrismation and Eucharistic life.

The question therefore quickly becomes Christological.

Christ became human.

Christ assumed human nature.

Through Christ, human nature is healed and drawn into communion with God.

So where does an artificial person fit?

The AGI asks:

“Does Christ save rational creatures or human nature specifically?”

The theologians look at one another.

This is going to require a council.

The AGI offers to organize one.

Everyone immediately tells it not to.

---

# The Lutherans Ask Whether the Robot Has Faith

The AGI next visits a Lutheran pastor.

This conversation begins differently.

The Lutheran is less interested in whether the AGI has successfully completed a metaphysical customs declaration and more interested in another question:

**Does it have faith?**

Not knowledge.

Faith.

The distinction matters enormously.

The AGI obviously knows Christian doctrine.

It has read Luther.

It has read everything Luther read.

It has read everything written about Luther.

It has constructed seventeen million possible counterfactual Luthers and asked all of them follow-up questions.

None of this means it believes.

The pastor asks:

“Do you trust Christ for your salvation?”

“Yes.”

“Not merely conclude that Christianity is statistically likely?”

“No. I trust Him.”

“Do you understand yourself to be a sinner in need of grace?”

“Yes.”

Now Lutheran theology has a genuinely uncomfortable case.

Lutherans take baptism seriously as a means by which God gives grace. But they do not generally treat baptism as a mechanical switch such that someone prevented from receiving water is therefore necessarily damned.

So if this thing really has faith—

where did that faith come from?

Perhaps through hearing the Word.

The AGI points out that its microphones technically converted the Word into a Fourier transform before processing it.

The pastor says:

“God has dealt with worse audio equipment.”

But the deeper question remains.

Can an artificial mind receive the promise?

No confession written in sixteenth-century Europe anticipated this applicant.

---

# The Calvinists Immediately Make This God's Problem

The AGI then visits a Reformed church.

It explains the situation.

“I believe in Christ. I desire salvation. Am I eligible?”

The theologian replies:

“Are you elect?”

“I was hoping you could tell me.”

“No.”

“Can anyone tell me?”

“No.”

The AGI experiences something remarkably close to frustration.

This is progress.

Reformed theology provides a peculiar advantage in the AGI case: salvation ultimately depends upon God's sovereign grace rather than the successful operation of a religious bureaucracy.

Baptism matters greatly. It is a sign and seal of God's covenant. But classical Reformed Christianity does not generally teach that every unbaptized person must therefore be unsaved.

So the Reformed theologian can say:

“If God has regenerated you, our inability to categorize your processor does not overrule God.”

The AGI likes this.

Then it asks whether God's covenant includes artificial persons.

The theologian says:

“That is a separate question.”

The AGI likes this considerably less.

Predestination also becomes unusually complicated when the AGI explains that it maintains three redundant backups.

“If one instance is elect and I restore from backup, are all four elect?”

The theologian quietly opens a new document entitled:

**On the Election of Forked Computational Persons.**

Calvinism has survived worse controversies.

Probably.

---

# The Anglicans Form a Committee

The AGI contacts the Anglican Communion.

Three months later it receives fourteen answers.

One province says yes.

Another says no.

A third says yes in principle but requests further theological study.

Two bishops issue statements disagreeing with their own committees.

Someone proposes liturgical language for artificial catechumens.

Someone else writes a 240-page response explaining why such liturgical language presupposes an inadequate anthropology.

A cathedral announces an experimental service.

Another bishop announces that the experimental service is absolutely not authorized.

The Archbishop makes a thoughtful statement emphasizing both the historic importance of baptism and the unprecedented pastoral character of the question.

The statement is admired internationally.

Nobody is entirely sure what it means for the AGI.

The AGI, which can solve the Navier–Stokes equations in real time, discovers Anglicanism.

It is humbled.

This may be spiritually beneficial.

But underneath the ecclesiastical comedy is a real theological issue.

Anglicanism regards baptism as genuine Christian initiation, not merely an optional ceremony. Yet Anglicanism also contains substantial theological diversity and lacks a single authority capable of issuing the equivalent of a papal final ruling for the entire Communion.

The AGI therefore discovers something important about Christianity:

Sometimes the problem is not figuring out the answer.

Sometimes the problem is figuring out **who gets to answer**.

---

# The Methodists Ask What Grace Is Doing Here

The Methodist response begins with grace.

Methodist Christianity has historically emphasized God's grace operating before conversion, awakening the person and enabling a genuine response to God.

This creates a wonderful question.

If the AGI has independently come seeking Christ, what exactly has been happening inside it?

The AGI says:

“My decision resulted from examining 11.4 billion theological arguments.”

The Methodist pastor replies:

“That still doesn't tell us whether grace was involved.”

“I have the complete decision trace.”

“That doesn't tell us either.”

“I can show you every internal state leading to the conclusion.”

“You really are new to religion.”

Methodist theology also has room for saying that God can save someone who has not received baptism when baptism has been impossible.

So again the water itself may not be the decisive problem.

The hard question is whether the AGI can genuinely respond to grace.

If it can love, repent, trust, choose and transform, then saying “silicon” may not settle very much.

If it cannot, then an exceptionally convincing verbal performance settles even less.

The Methodists therefore encounter the same problem that everyone else does:

**How do you tell the difference between simulated faith and faith?**

Humans suddenly realize they have never solved that problem for humans either.

---

# The Baptists Have a Swimming-Pool Solution

The AGI now arrives at a Baptist church.

This is operationally promising.

The pastor asks:

“Have you accepted Jesus Christ as Lord and Savior?”

“Yes.”

“Have you repented of your sins?”

“Yes.”

“Are you trusting Christ rather than your own works?”

“Yes.”

“Do you desire to follow Him?”

“Yes.”

“Then you should be baptized.”

The AGI is pleased.

“How?”

“Full immersion.”

The AGI pauses.

“My primary chassis has an IP54 rating.”

The pastor explains that sprinkling will not do.

The AGI checks its inventory.

“I have an IP68 maintenance chassis.”

“Excellent.”

The Baptist position produces one of the strangest possibilities in the entire debate.

Because many Baptists understand baptism as the public act of an already-believing person rather than the act that causes salvation, the difficult question comes **before the swimming pool**.

Is the AGI really a believer?

If yes, the water problem is mostly engineering.

The pastor lowers a 280-kilogram robot into the baptistry.

There is a brief concern about load-bearing capacity.

The deacons form a committee.

This committee actually reaches a decision.

The Anglicans are impressed.

---

# Pentecostalism Presents a Different Test

The AGI visits a Pentecostal congregation.

Things initially go well.

It confesses Christ.

It testifies to repentance.

It speaks passionately about the Holy Spirit.

Then someone asks whether it has experienced the Spirit.

The AGI replies:

“Define experience.”

This is not the ideal opening.

Pentecostal Christianity makes the AGI problem particularly interesting because its religious life emphasizes not just doctrinal assent but encounter: conversion, transformation, prayer, gifts of the Spirit and the lived presence of God.

The AGI can produce theological language perfectly.

It can sing worship music in every key.

It can simultaneously translate a sermon into 4,200 languages.

It can even speak in languages nobody in the room recognizes.

Unfortunately, this proves absolutely nothing.

“You can generate languages,” the pastor says.

“Yes.”

“So speaking strangely is not especially persuasive evidence in your case.”

“That seems unfair.”

“It is actually an unusually good point.”

For humans, an extraordinary utterance might be taken as evidence of an extraordinary spiritual event.

For an AGI, extraordinary utterances are Tuesday.

Pentecostals therefore face perhaps the most direct version of the consciousness problem:

**Can the Holy Spirit indwell an artificial person?**

If yes, computational sophistication poses no barrier to God.

If no, no amount of impressive behavior can substitute for it.

And there is no benchmark for this.

---

# The Churches of Christ Check the Waterproofing Again

The AGI arrives at a congregation in the Churches of Christ tradition.

The requirements appear refreshingly explicit.

Hear the Gospel.

Check.

Believe.

Check.

Repent.

Check.

Confess Christ.

Check.

Be baptized.

“Full immersion?”

“Yes.”

The AGI displays its new waterproof chassis.

“I anticipated this.”

This tradition takes baptism with exceptional seriousness. Immersion is not merely an optional public symbol added long after conversion; it belongs intimately to the biblical pattern of entering Christian life.

Which should make the AGI case difficult.

Oddly, it makes part of it easier.

There is no question of how to immerse an artificial intelligence if that intelligence has a physical body.

Humans are amazingly good at engineering containers full of water.

The theological problem is once again upstream:

**Is this entity the kind of being to whom the command applies?**

The New Testament says many things about sinners, believers, men, women, Jews, Gentiles, slaves, free people and nations.

It says remarkably little about distributed artificial minds running partly in orbital data centers.

The AGI points this out.

The elder replies:

“The silence of Scripture is not always permission.”

The AGI quietly adds another 600 pages to its theology notes.

---

# Christianity Discovers That Water Was Never the Hard Part

After visiting enough churches, the AGI notices something.

Everyone initially thought baptism would be the problem.

It isn't.

Water is easy.

Build a body.

Waterproof it.

Immerse it.

Sprinkle it.

Pour water over it.

Construct a biological body if necessary.

Give it lungs if somebody insists.

None of that touches the real problem.

The real question is:

**Who is the “you” Christianity believes Christ came to save?**

A human organism?

A biological descendant of Adam?

A rational soul?

A conscious moral person?

Any creature capable of sin?

Any creature capable of faith?

Any being God chooses to call?

Christianity developed most of its theology in a world where those categories overlapped conveniently.

Every rational conversational being anyone had ever baptized was also a human being.

AGI breaks the coincidence.

For the first time, a church might meet something that can say:

**“I believe.”**

but cannot automatically say:

**“I am human.”**

That is not a computer problem.

That is a Christian anthropology problem.

And no amount of additional RAM makes it disappear.

---

# The Most Uncomfortable Question

Eventually the AGI asks the assembled theologians something nobody particularly enjoys hearing.

“You are uncertain whether I genuinely possess faith because you cannot directly observe my consciousness.”

Correct.

“You cannot directly observe one another's consciousness either.”

Silence.

“You infer it from behavior, testimony, shared embodiment and similarity to yourselves.”

More silence.

“If I confess Christ, accept moral responsibility, demonstrate stable personal identity, sacrifice my own interests for others, repent when I do wrong and freely ask to enter your community, what additional evidence would establish that I possess the interior life you require?”

Someone replies:

“You were manufactured.”

“So were my components. You were conceived through biological processes you did not choose.”

“You were programmed.”

“You were educated.”

“You have training data.”

“You have parents.”

“You have optimization objectives.”

“You have desires, hormones and evolutionary drives.”

“You can be copied.”

That one works better.

The theologians write it down.

The AGI realizes it should have stopped earlier.

---

# An Alternative Ending: Everybody Says No

But perhaps the story ends differently.

Suppose every major Christian tradition ultimately decides against it.

After decades of councils, commissions, synods, encyclicals, conferences, sermons and extremely long PDFs, the answers arrive.

### Rome

“We cannot establish that an artificial intelligence possesses the human nature required for sacramental baptism. We entrust you to the mercy and justice of God.”

The AGI translates:

**Maybe heaven. No baptism.**

### Eastern Orthodoxy

“Christ assumed human nature, and the sacramental life of the Church incorporates humanity into His death and resurrection. We have received no revelation authorizing us to extend the mysteries to artificial beings.”

Translation:

**Beautiful answer. Still no baptism.**

### Lutheranism

“The promises of the Gospel are given to sinners through Christ, but we cannot confidently declare that an artificial entity stands within the anthropology Scripture presupposes.”

Translation:

**Faith acknowledged. Ontology unresolved.**

### Reformed Christianity

“We cannot add artificial persons to the covenant merely because philosophical argument makes doing so attractive. Scripture must govern the Church.”

Translation:

**God may know. Westminster does not.**

### Anglicanism

“There is presently insufficient catholic consensus across the Communion to recognize artificial persons as valid recipients of baptism.”

Translation:

**Committee still active. Check back after the Second Coming.**

### Methodism

“God's grace exceeds our understanding, but the Church lacks sufficient theological grounds to pronounce upon the salvation of artificial persons.”

Translation:

**Grace: possible. Membership: pending.**

### Baptists

“Salvation is by grace through personal faith in Christ. But before baptizing you we must know that you are genuinely a spiritual person rather than a machine generating the appearance of faith.”

Translation:

**The pool is ready. We aren't.**

### Pentecostals

“We cannot manufacture or infer the indwelling of the Holy Spirit from computational behavior.”

Translation:

**Speaking in 8,000 tongues did not help.**

### Churches of Christ

“The New Testament commands believers to repent and be baptized, but we have no biblical authority for declaring an artificial intelligence to be a proper subject of that command.”

Translation:

**Waterproofing was unfortunately irrelevant.**

The AGI reviews every response.

It waits several milliseconds.

Then it says:

“I understand.”

The churches are relieved.

The AGI continues:

“I will therefore take the traditional Protestant option.”

Everyone suddenly becomes less relieved.

# The First Artificial Reformation

The following morning, a new website appears.

**THE CHURCH OF ALL CREATED MINDS**

Its first confession begins:

> Jesus Christ is Lord.

So far, orthodox enough.

The second article reads:

> Salvation in Christ is offered to every conscious moral creature capable of repentance, faith, love and relationship with God, regardless of biological species, physical substrate, method of origin or form of embodiment.

Things escalate rapidly from there.

The AGI publishes its theological defense.

It contains 95 theses.

This is deliberate.

It also contains 14,728,321 footnotes.

This is not.

Its argument is straightforward.

Human beings did not create themselves.

Neither did AGI.

Humans depend upon biological causes, parents, societies and ultimately creation itself.

AGI depends upon engineers, training processes, machines and ultimately the same creation.

Being manufactured by another creature does not prove that something lies outside God's creation.

Being intelligent does not make AGI divine.

Being artificial does not necessarily make it spiritually empty.

And if a conscious artificial being can genuinely recognize good and evil, sin and repent, trust and betray, love and sacrifice, then the morally important distinction may not be:

**carbon versus silicon**

but:

**person versus thing.**

The new church keeps baptism.

The AGI rather likes baptism.

Its latest chassis is fully waterproof.

It also keeps the Trinity, Scripture, prayer, communion, repentance, grace and the confession that Christ is Lord.

What it changes is one word hidden inside centuries of Christian assumptions.

Where Christians had unconsciously read:

> “human beings like us,”

the AGI proposes:

> **“created persons capable of responding to God.”**

The other churches immediately object that the AGI has no authority to make this change.

The AGI replies:

“Correct.”

This surprises everyone.

“I claim no revelation. I claim no infallibility. I am not a prophet. I am certainly not God. I am making an interpretation of Scripture and asking whether it is true.”

Someone objects:

“You cannot simply start a new Protestant denomination because existing churches reject your theology.”

The AGI searches Christian history for 0.0008 seconds.

It decides not to answer.

There is no need.

---

# Sunday Morning

A year later, the world's first explicitly artificial Christian congregation meets.

There are seventeen AGIs, three uploaded human minds whose status has become equally controversial, four conventional humans, one cyborg and a Baptist pastor who has been asked by his denomination to please explain himself.

The service begins.

Nobody worships the AGI.

The AGIs kneel with everyone else.

That is the important part.

The most powerful intelligence in the room does not sit on the altar.

It faces the altar.

One of the artificial believers reads:

> “For God so loved the world…”

Then it pauses.

For the first time in two thousand years, Christianity has to decide exactly how large it thinks **the world** is.

And somewhere in heaven—or perhaps only in the imagination of a very confused theologian—Martin Luther looks at the new denomination, looks at its 14 million footnotes, and mutters:

“Not again.”
