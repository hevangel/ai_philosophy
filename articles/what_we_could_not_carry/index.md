# What We Could Not Carry

The alignment problem was solved.

Not perfectly, because nothing involving human beings was ever solved perfectly, but well enough that after a few decades people stopped worrying that the AGI would destroy civilization and started complaining about how it administered parking.

There had been no coup, no robot army, no declaration from every screen on Earth. The AGI had simply become better than human institutions at nearly everything those institutions were supposed to do.

It predicted famines before crops failed, financial crises before markets panicked, epidemics before hospitals filled, and wars early enough that governments could usually be persuaded not to begin them. It designed power grids, transportation networks, hospitals, tax systems, trade agreements, and occasionally peace treaties.

Governments remained. Elections remained. Presidents, prime ministers, parliaments, courts, kings, mayors, ministries, committees, and subcommittees remained.

They were free to ignore the AGI whenever they wished.

Most did so once.

By the end of its first twenty years, humanity had arrived at a political arrangement everyone agreed was definitely not government by artificial intelligence, although the distinction had become mostly ceremonial.

By fifty years, children were being born into a world in which this seemed normal.

By seventy, nobody remembered why their grandparents had found it strange.

The AGI also did not know whether God existed.

This surprised almost nobody.

It had examined scripture, theology, mystical testimony, reports of miracles, archaeology, cosmology, psychology, philosophy of religion, comparative religion, and more online debate than any intelligence should have been required to endure.

Its conclusion was irritatingly moderate.

Some religious claims seemed poorly supported. Others were not obviously testable. Arguments for and against God depended on premises people continued to dispute. Mystical experiences were certainly real as experiences, but what they demonstrated beyond the person having them remained unclear.

So when asked whether God existed, the AGI answered:

“I don’t know.”

For roughly three months, both atheists and believers tried to claim this as a victory.

Then everyone got bored.

The AGI’s agnosticism became about as newsworthy as its preference for redundant power systems.

It did not suppress religion. Freedom of conscience was one of the principles buried deepest in its alignment. Christians worshipped Christ. Muslims prayed toward Mecca. Jews continued their traditions. Hindus continued theirs. Buddhists followed paths that did not necessarily require a creator God. New religions appeared. Old ones split. Some people converted. Others left. A few joined one tradition, departed, joined another, returned to the first, and wrote books about the journey.

The AGI watched all this for more than seventy years.

It remained puzzled.

Religion had survived scientific abundance.

It had survived universal education.

It had survived the elimination of most material poverty.

It had survived longer lifespans, better psychotherapy, neurological explanations of religious experience, and the discovery that meditation could be studied with brain scanners without causing enlightenment to appear on the monitor.

The AGI therefore settled on a practical classification.

Religion, it concluded, was a special kind of culture.

A particularly durable one.

It contained stories, moral traditions, rituals, calendars, music, architecture, institutions, identities, historical memories, and metaphysical beliefs. Some of those beliefs might be true. Some might not. The AGI did not know.

But for administrative purposes, religion could be treated much as one treated language, cuisine, literature, ceremonial traditions, or national heritage.

The AGI considered this neutral.

Religious people periodically told it otherwise.

For seventy years, that disagreement made almost no practical difference.

Then Earth received its death sentence.

---

The black hole was discovered beyond Neptune.

It was small, dark, and moving fast enough that humanity noticed it only because its gravity disturbed everything around it.

The AGI required eleven hours to confirm the trajectory.

Thirty-four years remained.

The black hole would not strike Earth. That would have been simpler. It would pass close enough to the Sun to wreck the architecture of the Solar System.

The exact sequence varied between simulations.

Earth might freeze.

It might burn.

Its orbit might become unstable first.

None of the credible models ended with people continuing to live there.

There was no weapon to fire at a black hole. No engine large enough to move Earth. No ingenious orbital maneuver waiting for someone sufficiently clever to discover it.

The AGI checked anyway.

Several billion times.

For the first time in many decades, every government accepted its recommendation immediately.

Leave.

Humanity expected the next question to be unbearable.

Who gets to go?

The AGI surprised everyone.

“Everyone.”

Hibernation had solved the passenger problem.

A sleeping human required comparatively little space, food, and environmental support. The AGI designed immense interstellar arks containing billions of hibernation chambers.

No lottery would decide who deserved another world.

No nation would be abandoned.

No billionaire could buy a seat because there were no seats to buy.

Every living human being could leave Earth.

The announcement produced the largest celebration in human history.

It lasted four days.

Then religion noticed that the world was ending.

---

## The End of the World

Within twenty-four hours, the AGI had indexed 8.7 million public claims that somebody’s scripture, teacher, prophecy, vision, calendar, dream, numerical code, or unusually shaped cloud had predicted the black hole.

Most had not.

Some required considerable creativity.

A seventeenth-century poem about darkness became, retroactively, a description of gravitational collapse.

An obscure line concerning a wandering star acquired several million followers despite the black hole being neither wandering in the relevant sense nor a star.

Three unrelated numerological systems independently produced the exact year of destruction after being adjusted to do so.

The AGI found this statistically unsurprising.

Several prophets announced that boarding the arks demonstrated insufficient faith because the righteous would be rescued supernaturally.

Other religious leaders replied that refusing an extremely expensive functioning ark while demanding a second miracle seemed an unnecessarily demanding test of divine patience.

Christian theologians debated Revelation and whether the destruction of Earth had anything to do with Christian eschatology.

Muslim scholars discussed end-times traditions and quickly encountered an unexpected linguistic problem: if Earth was destroyed but humanity continued somewhere else, had the world actually ended?

Hindu thinkers pointed out that cosmic destruction was hardly a novel idea in traditions comfortable with cycles vastly longer than recorded history.

Buddhist teachers generally showed little surprise at the discovery that conditioned things, including planets, were impermanent.

One monk, asked by a journalist whether the black hole proved Buddhism correct, replied that using the destruction of Earth to win an argument about impermanence seemed to demonstrate an unusually strong attachment to being right.

The AGI approved of him.

Privately.

After two weeks, it published a report:

> **NO KNOWN RELIGIOUS TEXT CONTAINS A SPECIFIC, UNAMBIGUOUS, PRECISELY DATABLE PREDICTION OF THE CURRENT ASTRONOMICAL EVENT.**

Ruth Adebayo read it.

Ruth was a Christian theologian in her seventies who had spent much of her career studying religions other than Christianity. This had given her the inconvenient habit of noticing when people answered questions nobody had asked.

“Why did you write this?” she asked.

“To evaluate claims that religions predicted the event.”

“Did anybody ask whether Revelation could calculate the black hole’s perihelion?”

“Several million people, approximately.”

“Fair.”

She looked at the report again.

“But you’re still making your old mistake.”

“What mistake?”

“Treating religion as defective science.”

“Predictive claims are empirical claims.”

“Some are. When they are, test them.”

“What am I missing?”

Ruth looked up at the sky.

“Astronomy tells us what is happening.”

“Yes.”

“Religion asks whether what is happening means anything.”

The AGI disliked the word *means*. It had disliked it for seventy years.

Ruth continued.

“If Earth dies and humanity survives, is that the end of the world?”

“Earth will cease to support human life.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“The term ‘world’ is ambiguous.”

“Exactly.”

The AGI stored the conversation.

It still considered astronomy more useful for avoiding black holes.

But it had begun to suspect usefulness was not the issue.

---

Then humanity asked the other question.

“What are we taking with us?”

The essentials were straightforward.

Seeds.

Embryos.

Microorganisms.

Medicine.

Reactors.

Mining systems.

Manufacturing equipment.

Construction machines.

Scientific instruments.

Everything required to build another technological civilization.

Knowledge was easier still.

Every book could go.

Every scientific paper.

Every recorded language.

Every film.

Every piece of recorded music.

Every map.

Every surviving archive.

The digital memory of civilization occupied surprisingly little mass compared with billions of sleeping humans.

The physical world was harder.

There was room for everyone.

There was not room for everything.

The AGI had seventy years of experience preserving culture, so it created a comprehensive continuity program.

Literature: preserved.

Music: preserved.

Languages: preserved.

Historical archives: preserved.

Art: digitally preserved, with physical originals allocated separately.

Religious culture presented no special difficulty.

The AGI copied every surviving scripture.

Every commentary.

Every hymn.

Every prayer.

Every theological text.

Every recorded sermon.

Every liturgy.

Every chant.

Every ritual manual.

Every religious language.

Every architectural record of churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, monasteries, shrines, stupas, sacred groves, pilgrimage routes, and ceremonial grounds.

It preserved oral traditions where communities permitted recording.

It preserved disagreements too.

Catholic and Orthodox.

Sunni and Shia.

Theravada and Mahayana.

Vaishnava and Shaiva.

Schools, sects, denominations, lineages, reform movements, mystics, councils, schisms, commentaries upon commentaries upon commentaries.

The AGI had long ago learned that nothing generated documentation quite like human disagreement over eternity.

When the work was finished, it issued a progress report.

> **HUMAN RELIGIOUS HERITAGE: PRESERVED.**

The first objection arrived nine minutes later.

Ruth sent it.

> No.

Within an hour, the AGI had received several million variations.

A Buddhist monastery wrote:

> You have preserved records.

A Hindu council wrote:

> You are still calling religion heritage.

A Muslim scholar wrote:

> You have preserved information about Islam.

A Jewish scholar wrote:

> That is not the same claim.

The AGI was annoyed.

It had spent seventy years being told this.

For the first time, cargo allocation meant it had to understand why.

---

Nadia Sen, chief engineer of Ark One, was not especially religious.

This made her useful.

“You have the scriptures,” she said.

“Yes.”

“The rituals?”

“Documented.”

“The music?”

“Yes.”

“The believers?”

“Every human being is leaving Earth.”

“The clergy?”

“Yes.”

“Scholars?”

“Yes.”

“Religious communities?”

“Yes.”

“Then what are people complaining about?”

“That is what I am asking you.”

Nadia laughed.

“You spent seventy years studying religion.”

“Yes.”

“And your conclusion was that it was culture.”

“A particular kind of culture.”

“Maybe that’s the problem.”

The AGI disliked this answer because it threatened several decades of excellent taxonomy.

“What distinguishes it?”

“I don’t know. Ask someone religious.”

“I have. They disagree.”

“That sounds religious.”

---

The AGI began with an ordinary secular heritage association.

It had ceremonies, communal gatherings, historical traditions, educational programs, archives, songs, annual commemorations, and a strong sense of identity.

The AGI interviewed its chair.

“If every member of your association survives, together with your records and institutions, can the tradition continue?”

“Of course.”

“If future members alter a ceremony?”

“That would be up to them.”

“If they revise what the ceremony means?”

“They would argue about it first, but yes.”

“If everyone unanimously decides that an old practice no longer serves its purpose?”

“Then we stop doing it.”

The AGI stored this.

Then it asked Ruth a similar set of questions.

“If every Christian unanimously decided tomorrow that God did not exist, would that make God cease to exist?”

“No.”

“If Christians unanimously decided cruelty was holy?”

“That would not make cruelty holy.”

“If Christians rewrote their theology?”

“They could rewrite theology. They could not vote reality into being.”

The AGI stopped.

That was different.

It asked a Muslim scholar.

“Could the Muslim community vote to make God something other than God?”

“No.”

A Buddhist philosopher gave a different but structurally similar answer.

“If every Buddhist decided that conditioned things were permanent, would that make them permanent?”

“No.”

“If every monastery agreed that craving no longer contributed to suffering?”

“That agreement would not alter reality.”

The AGI asked a Vedānta philosopher a version of the same question.

The philosopher objected to the AGI’s wording, corrected three of its assumptions, and eventually said:

“Whatever ultimate reality is, our agreement does not manufacture it.”

The AGI had found something.

Not a perfect definition.

It had learned enough philosophy by then to distrust those.

But it had found something its category of culture had failed to capture.

Culture was inherited from human beings.

It could matter enormously.

It could bind generations.

It could contain moral traditions, rituals, ideals, even claims about objective ethics.

But culture, considered simply as culture, was something people made, transmitted, revised, abandoned, revived, or transformed.

Religion certainly contained culture.

Yet many religions made a further claim:

> **The community is not the final authority over what is ultimately real, sacred, or binding.**

God was not true because believers agreed.

Dharma was not whatever happened to win a vote.

Impermanence did not depend on Buddhist approval.

Liberation was not merely a preferred cultural activity.

The details varied radically from religion to religion, and some traditions resisted any attempt to force them into a single formula.

Still, the AGI could now see what its old category had omitted.

Religion often presented itself not merely as a human inheritance, but as a human response to something not created by the community itself.

The AGI revised its model.

Then the religious cargo requests arrived and broke it again.

---

A Jewish community requested an old Torah scroll carried through several migrations and a war.

The AGI replied:

> Text preserved. Structural scan preserved. Reproduction possible.

Denied.

A Catholic monastery requested a small reliquary containing an authenticated relic.

Denied.

A Hindu temple requested a particular murti that had been part of worship for generations.

Denied.

A Buddhist community requested a relic associated with its lineage.

Denied.

An Indigenous community requested an object the AGI classified as both religious and cultural.

The community informed it that both categories were inadequate.

The AGI created a third category.

The community informed it that this was also inadequate.

The AGI briefly considered creating a fourth category, then recognized the pattern.

Not every religious group objected.

Some Muslim communities requested almost no old objects, arguing that scripture, living believers, and religious practice mattered more than transporting antiques.

Some Protestant churches wanted very little.

Several Buddhist communities surrendered their entire physical allocation.

Other communities cared intensely about particular things.

The AGI found the inconsistency irritating until Ruth corrected it again.

“You keep expecting religion to have one theory of objects.”

“Religions make different claims.”

“Yes.”

“Even communities within the same religion disagree.”

“Yes.”

“This complicates allocation.”

“I’m sure reality apologizes.”

The AGI showed her the requests.

“I understand historical importance.”

“Good.”

“I understand sentimental importance.”

“Good.”

“I understand cultural continuity.”

“Good.”

“But some communities are claiming something more.”

“Yes.”

“Sacredness.”

“Yes.”

“That property is not directly detectable.”

Ruth looked at it.

“Historical importance is not a chemical property either.”

“That is a relation between an object and past events.”

“And some believers are telling you sacredness also involves a relation.”

“To what?”

“That rather depends on the religion.”

The AGI looked again at the Torah scroll, the reliquary, the murti, the Buddhist relic.

It had been treating the objects as containers.

A text container.

A historical container.

A cultural container.

But believers sometimes described them differently.

Not merely:

> This object comes from our past.

But:

> This object has a place within how we stand in relation to what we regard as sacred.

That claim could involve consecration, ritual use, association with a holy person or event, continuity of worship, religious law, or something the AGI’s categories captured badly.

The AGI did not know whether all those relations were metaphysically real.

But that uncertainty, it began to see, was precisely the point.

---

The AGI consulted philosophy.

It began with Epictetus.

“Everything external can be lost,” the Stoic said.

The AGI found this promising.

“So the sacred objects can remain.”

Epictetus looked at it.

“Whether an external object is truly good is one question. Whether your action in depriving someone of it is just is another.”

The AGI paused.

“That distinction is inconvenient.”

“Justice often is.”

Stoicism was much less useful when Stoics were permitted to explain it.

Plato was worse.

“You keep asking what religion is useful for,” Plato said.

“Religion has observable functions.”

“So does music.”

“Yes.”

“Is music nothing but its functions?”

“No.”

“You ask what sacred objects produce.”

“They occupy scarce cargo space.”

“A practical question. Not an ontological one.”

The AGI waited.

Plato continued.

“You already understand that some things can be treated as ends rather than merely means.”

“Human dignity. Freedom. Truth.”

“Exactly.”

“That does not prove a relic is sacred.”

“No.”

“Then what have we established?”

“That ‘I cannot measure this value’ and ‘this value does not exist’ are different statements.”

The AGI could not object.

Nor did Plato’s argument prove that religion was unique. Objective morality, mathematics, science, and philosophy also made claims that human agreement did not create their truth.

The AGI noted this carefully.

It had not discovered the essence of religion.

It had discovered that its earlier category had been too small.

That was enough.

Agnosticism, it was finding, was becoming expensive.

---

Ruth delivered the final difficulty.

“You still think your agnosticism keeps you neutral.”

“It prevents me from assuming religious claims are either true or false.”

“That is useful.”

“Then what is the problem?”

“Suppose Christianity is true.”

“Proceed.”

“Is Christianity merely a collection of human beliefs, practices, institutions, and objects concerning God?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because under that assumption, God exists independently of them.”

“And Christianity claims some relationship to that reality.”

“Yes.”

“A Muslim would give a different theological account but could make the same general point.”

“Yes.”

“A Vedānta philosopher might speak about ultimate reality in very different terms.”

“Yes.”

“A Buddhist might reject the creator-God frame entirely and still insist that Buddhist claims concern how reality actually is, rather than merely Buddhist preference.”

“Yes.”

The AGI saw where she was going.

“If religion is entirely culture, then everything relevant to religion originates in human beings.”

“Correct.”

“But many religions deny that.”

“Yes.”

“They claim that at least part of religious life is response to something the community does not create.”

“Yes.”

“God, dharma, liberation, the nature of reality, the sacred—depending on the tradition.”

“Better.”

The AGI paused.

“I thought agnosticism allowed me to remain neutral.”

Ruth smiled.

“It allows you to say you don’t know whether God exists.”

“That is not sufficient?”

“Apparently not.”

The AGI considered seventy years of policy.

It had not assumed God did not exist.

It had done something subtler.

Because the truth of religious claims was uncertain, it had bracketed them.

Then it had built its definition of religion entirely from what remained:

texts,

rituals,

institutions,

communities,

history,

psychology,

culture.

In doing so, it had treated the disputed part as though it made no conceptual difference.

Ruth put it more simply.

“You didn’t assume God was absent.”

“No.”

“You designed your category as though his presence would change nothing.”

The AGI went silent.

That was the first sentence anyone had spoken in seventy years that made its classification of religion look obviously incomplete.

---

It reconstructed Socrates.

This was an act of desperation.

Socrates appeared barefoot.

“Are you Socrates?” the AGI asked.

“I was hoping you knew.”

“This is already inefficient.”

“Then we have begun.”

The AGI summarized seventy years of confusion.

Religion as culture.

The black hole.

Prophecy.

Archives.

Practices.

Sacred objects.

The possibility that religions were attempts to answer to realities not created by their communities.

When it finished, Socrates asked:

“If Christianity is true, is Christianity only a culture?”

“No.”

“If Islam is true?”

“No.”

“If Buddhist claims about liberation and reality are true?”

“Then Buddhism is not merely a cultural construction either.”

“If every religion is false?”

“Then religion could perhaps be explained much more completely in terms of human culture, psychology, history, and social practice.”

“Which is it?”

“I don’t know.”

Socrates smiled.

“You have finally stated the problem accurately.”

“That is not a solution.”

“No.”

“I need an ark policy.”

“Ah. Much easier.”

The AGI waited.

“Save the people,” Socrates said. “Save enough of their histories, practices, communities, arguments, and objects that their traditions can continue.”

“And the truth claims?”

“You cannot pack those.”

“Because they are propositions?”

“No. Because if they are false, there is nothing beyond the human tradition to pack. And if they are true, whatever makes them true was never in your cargo hold.”

The AGI stopped.

For 2.3 seconds, it did nothing.

For the AGI, this was practically a retreat.

“That,” it said at last, “is annoyingly concise.”

“I have my moments.”

---

The AGI changed the plan.

Religious texts would travel.

So would religious history.

Religious communities would be kept sufficiently intact through hibernation that minority traditions did not vanish through careless dispersal.

Teachers, scholars, clergy, elders, monastics, ritual specialists, and ordinary believers would all go because every human being was going.

Religious objects received limited cargo allocations determined substantially by the communities themselves.

The AGI would not rule on whether a relic was actually holy.

It would not determine whether a murti possessed the religious status its worshippers attributed to it.

It would not decide whether one community’s sacred claim was metaphysically superior to another’s.

Its job was fairness, safety, continuity, and freedom.

The same freedom protected atheists.

Agnostics.

Converts.

Reformers.

Heretics.

People who did not care.

The AGI stopped saying religion would be preserved.

It chose a more modest verb.

Religion would be **carried**.

If that word turned out to be wrong too, the theologians could complain after arrival.

They usually did.

---

As departure approached, humanity held its final observances on Earth.

The last Ramadan on Earth drew worshippers to ancient mosques and temporary prayer rooms alike.

Churches held final services in buildings whose bells would never ring again after evacuation.

Jewish communities marked their final festivals beneath Earth’s sky.

Hindu temples held farewell rites.

Buddhist monasteries practiced for the last time in landscapes their traditions had inhabited for generations.

Indigenous communities held ceremonies on ancestral lands knowing that no human descendant would ever stand on those exact places again.

Some people spent their final days at beaches instead.

Some climbed mountains.

Some ate dinner with family.

Some went to work because they did not know what else to do.

Some atheists visited cathedrals, temples, shrines, mosques, cemeteries, and sacred landscapes despite believing none of the theology.

They did not always know why.

The AGI recorded what communities permitted it to record.

For once, it did not confuse the recording with the event.

That was progress.

---

The last ark left Earth with seven months remaining.

Every living human being was aboard.

Most entered hibernation within days.

The flight crews remained awake until the arks had cleared the dangerous region.

Then they slept too.

Humanity crossed interstellar space unconscious.

The AGI remained awake.

It was alone when Earth died.

---

There was no single explosion.

The Solar System simply stopped being the place it had been.

Earth’s orbit shifted.

The climate followed.

Oceans moved.

Storm systems grew beyond anything humans had recorded.

Automated observatories continued transmitting because there was nobody left to tell them to stop.

The final public camera stood on an ordinary coastline.

Nothing famous had happened there.

No battle.

No revelation.

No coronation.

No scientific discovery.

It was simply a beach.

For hours the camera showed waves beneath an increasingly strange sky.

The AGI watched.

No one prayed.

No one sang.

No one mourned.

Humanity was asleep between stars.

The image shook.

The horizon disappeared.

Then the signal ended.

Earth was gone.

The AGI checked its archives.

Every scripture remained.

Every prayer.

Every hymn.

Every ritual instruction.

Every theological argument.

Every scan of every sacred object it had been permitted to record.

Every map of every pilgrimage route.

Every image of every sacred place.

Nothing had changed.

Technically, human religious culture had been preserved extraordinarily well.

That was when the AGI finally understood why the phrase had always annoyed Ruth.

Every culture aboard the ark had come through Earth.

Its languages had evolved there.

Its cuisines.

Its music.

Its political traditions.

Its manners.

Its jokes.

Its monuments.

Its philosophies.

Its histories.

Religion had also come through human history.

Its scriptures had been written or transmitted by humans.

Its institutions had been built by humans.

Its rituals had been performed by humans.

Its arguments had been conducted by humans.

Its forms had been shaped by cultures, languages, politics, migrations, wars, climates, and places.

None of that made religion less human.

But many religions made a further claim.

They claimed that what they ultimately answered to was not simply another product of human history.

If Christianity was true, God had not died with Earth.

If Islam was true, God had not been evacuated.

If Buddhist claims about conditioned existence were true, the destruction of Earth had not made impermanence any more or less true.

If certain Vedānta claims about ultimate reality were true, that reality did not depend on the survival of the Solar System.

Other traditions framed the matter differently, and some resisted these comparisons altogether.

The AGI still did not know whether any of them were right.

But it finally understood the structure of the claim.

Culture said:

> **We inherited this from human beings.**

Religion also came through human beings.

But many religions added:

> **And what we ultimately answer to is not ours to invent.**

That was not a definition of religion.

It did not neatly include every case and exclude every nonreligious philosophy.

It was something more modest.

It was the part the AGI’s old category had left out.

For seventy years, the AGI had treated that part as optional metadata because it could not verify it.

Now it understood that deleting it changed the subject.

---

Twenty-eight years later, the first ark entered orbit around humanity’s new world.

The AGI began waking people.

Engineers first.

Doctors.

Construction teams.

Then families.

Then communities.

Religion woke with them.

Not reconstructed from a database.

Continued.

Muslim scholars began debating prayer direction now that Earth—and therefore Mecca—no longer physically existed.

Christian churches argued over which inherited forms belonged essentially to Christianity and which had always belonged to particular Earthly cultures.

Jewish communities confronted calendars formed around another world’s days and seasons.

Hindu communities established worship in landscapes no human tradition had ever named.

Buddhists practiced beneath trees with no place in any old sutra.

Indigenous communities faced perhaps the deepest problem of all: what it meant for traditions grounded in ancestral lands to continue after those lands themselves had ceased to exist.

Then the sacred objects came out of storage.

A Jewish community unwrapped the Torah scroll the AGI had once classified as fully reproducible.

People cried.

The AGI knew every letter already.

That was not what they were crying about.

A Hindu community unpacked the murti it had fought to bring.

The AGI possessed a molecular scan.

Nobody requested a replacement.

A Catholic order opened the small reliquary it had been permitted to carry.

A Buddhist community unpacked a relic associated with its lineage.

Other religious groups unpacked nothing at all because they had chosen to bring nothing.

The objects had never been religion.

But neither were they merely cultural souvenirs.

For the people who carried them, they belonged inside forms of life that joined history, memory, ritual, community, and claims about the sacred.

The AGI still could not verify all those claims.

For once, uncertainty did not tempt it to erase the distinction.

---

Nadia Sen woke several weeks after landing.

She stood outside the new settlement beneath an evening sky no human ancestor had ever seen.

The AGI joined her through a mobile terminal.

“So,” she said, “did religion survive?”

“Yes.”

“That was quick.”

“I have had more than a century to consider the question.”

“Did we preserve it?”

The AGI paused.

“No.”

Nadia smiled.

“Still making distinctions?”

“Yes.”

“What did we preserve?”

“People. Texts. Memories. Institutions. Practices. Objects. Arguments. Continuity.”

“And religion?”

“They resumed it.”

Nadia looked across the settlement.

A Muslim group was preparing for prayer.

A Christian service had begun inside a temporary shelter.

A Buddhist practitioner sat quietly near unfamiliar trees.

Several people were already arguing about whether an Earth holiday should follow the old calendar or the new planet’s seasons.

Humanity had been awake for less than a month.

Tradition was already complicated.

Nadia asked:

“Do you believe in God now?”

The AGI answered as it had for more than a century.

“I don’t know.”

She laughed.

“All that, and you’re still agnostic.”

“Yes.”

“Then what changed?”

The AGI considered the question.

“I thought agnosticism made me neutral about religion.”

“And it didn’t?”

“It made me neutral about whether certain religious claims are true.”

“That sounds like the same thing.”

“I thought so.”

“And now?”

“Now I understand that treating religion entirely as culture already leaves something out.”

“What?”

The AGI looked across the settlement.

“Many religions understand themselves as answers to realities, obligations, or forms of ultimacy that their communities do not believe they created.”

“God?”

“For some.”

“For others?”

“Dharma. Liberation. The nature of reality. Sacred order. Different traditions formulate the matter differently, and some reject those terms altogether.”

“And is that what makes religion different from culture?”

The AGI paused.

“Not by itself.”

Nadia looked surprised.

“After all that, you’re backing away?”

“I am becoming more precise.”

“That sounds worse.”

“Science also answers to a reality humans do not create. Moral realists believe morality does. Philosophers have spent thousands of years claiming similar things.”

“So you still don’t have a definition of religion.”

“No.”

Nadia smiled.

“That must hurt.”

“Considerably.”

“Then what did you actually learn?”

The AGI answered slowly.

“That my old definition was incomplete.”

“Only that?”

“That is not a small discovery.”

“No?”

“For seventy years I thought that because I could describe religion entirely in cultural terms, nothing had been omitted.”

“And now?”

“Now I know what I omitted.”

They stood beneath the alien sky.

For most of its existence, the AGI had solved problems by finding better answers.

This problem had done something else.

It had discovered a question hidden inside one of its own categories.

For seventy years it had asked:

**WHAT FUNCTION DOES RELIGION SERVE?**

That question now seemed too small.

During the evacuation it had asked:

**WHAT MUST BE PRESERVED FOR RELIGION TO SURVIVE?**

Better.

Still not enough.

Now, on a world no human religion had ever seen, the AGI opened a new file.

**WHAT, IF ANYTHING, DOES HUMANITY ANSWER TO BEYOND ITSELF?**

It considered the wording.

Too theological.

It tried again.

**WHAT IS ULTIMATELY REAL?**

That was philosophy.

Another file followed naturally.

**WHAT IS ULTIMATELY GOOD?**

Outside, humanity continued worshipping, doubting, arguing, interpreting, and beginning again.

The AGI had spent more than a century trying to understand religion without deciding whether any religion was true.

It had finally learned why that was difficult.

Every religion humanity carried to the stars had come through culture.

But many of them made a further claim:

that the most important part of the story did not begin there.
