# From Expression to Reception: Generative AI and the Transformation of Artistic Reception

*An essay on aesthetic agency, generative criticism, and transformative reception*

## Abstract

The familiar picture of artistic communication begins with a maker who has something to express and ends with an audience that receives and evaluates the finished work. Although this picture has never described every artistic practice, generative artificial intelligence makes its limitations especially visible. A person can now request an artwork principally in order to experience it, remain uncertain about its content, criticize the result, and use that criticism to produce another version. The same person may also transform a pre-existing artwork into a preferred variation. This essay argues that generative AI enables a distinctive configuration of **reception-directed production**, in which prospective enjoyment, curiosity, and judgment help initiate and govern artistic production. It develops two related concepts: **generative criticism**, where evaluation becomes an instruction for revision, and **transformative reception**, where engagement with an existing work produces a new work. Drawing on Dewey, Kant, Jauss, Iser, Barthes, Duchamp, Eco, Jenkins, and Lessig, the essay distinguishes this development from earlier practices of commissioning, adaptation, and participatory culture. It concludes that expanded audience agency is aesthetically significant but neither guarantees artistic authorship nor makes satisfying personal preference the measure of artistic value.

## 1. The Direction of Artistic Communication

One influential way of imagining art is as communication from an artist to an audience. A poet wishes to express an experience; a composer shapes an emotion into music; a filmmaker constructs a vision for viewers. The work moves, conceptually, from an originating intention through an artifact toward its reception. Audiences may admire, reject, misunderstand, or reinterpret the result, while critics explain what succeeds or fails. The artwork itself usually remains beyond their immediate control.

This picture is useful but incomplete. Artists often discover what they mean through making rather than beginning with a fully formed message. They are also the first viewers, readers, and listeners of their own works. Patrons have long commissioned art for their pleasure; performers alter scores; readers write fan fiction; audiences influence subsequent editions and sequels. It would therefore be historically false to say that creation and reception were once strictly separate.

Nevertheless, the distinction between roles has often been sustained by practical barriers. A reader who disliked a novel's conclusion could imagine a different one, but producing a convincing alternative demanded literary skill, time, and perhaps collaborators. A viewer who thought a film's pacing defective could publish a review, but could rarely create a watchable recut, let alone generate new scenes. The difference between judging and altering a work was not absolute; it was frequently substantial.

Generative AI can reduce that distance. A person may request a detective story because they want the experience of reading an unfamiliar mystery. After reading it, they may ask for subtler clues, a less predictable suspect, or a more ambiguous conclusion. The human initiates production without knowing precisely what will be produced. Their experience of the first result then informs the next act of production.

The thesis defended here is deliberately narrower than the claim that AI has invented participatory art. **Generative AI makes it increasingly practicable for reception itself to organize production, and for the audience's evaluative response to become a direct input into subsequent works.** This is a change in the distribution and accessibility of aesthetic agency, not proof that all prior art followed one rigid model.

## 2. The Prospective Audience as Initiator

Consider a person who asks an AI system to write a mystery with an isolated setting, morally complicated characters, and a solution that is surprising but retrospectively intelligible. The requester is not primarily trying to communicate a private vision to strangers. They want to encounter a story they do not yet know. Indeed, knowing the ending in advance would defeat part of their purpose.

This example separates two kinds of intention. An **expressive intention** concerns what the maker wishes to convey through a work. A **receptive intention** concerns the experience someone hopes to have when encountering a work. The two may coexist, but neither entails the other. The mystery requester can specify a genre and desired qualities while leaving the plot's decisive details to the system. Their initiating intention is real, yet their knowledge of the final content is incomplete.

Call this arrangement **reception-directed production**: the generation of an artifact is initiated and guided primarily by the prospective recipient's anticipated aesthetic experience. The recipient may also be the commissioner, selector, and editor. These roles need not amount to full artistic authorship. Asking for a story, choosing among outputs, rewriting passages, and developing a distinctive artistic conception involve different degrees and kinds of control.

John Dewey's *Art as Experience* (1934) helps explain why this arrangement is philosophically intelligible. Dewey resists treating art as merely an object detached from the experiences of making and perceiving it. Artistic activity involves both doing and undergoing: actions produce consequences that must be felt, noticed, and incorporated into further action. AI-assisted iteration can intensify the feedback between these moments. The user acts by requesting a work, undergoes the experience of receiving it, and acts again in response. Yet Dewey also gives weight to the integrated experience of making. Delegating execution may preserve some forms of aesthetic agency while reducing others. His philosophy thus supports the continuity between production and reception without making every form of automation an aesthetic gain.

Reception theory contributes a second insight. Hans Robert Jauss (1982) describes how works are encountered within a historically formed *horizon of expectations*. Readers approach a genre with assumptions about what it will provide, and important works may unsettle those assumptions. Generative systems permit expectations to shape a work's initial specification: a reader can ask for a tragic ending, unfamiliar narration, or a particular emotional register. Wolfgang Iser (1978), meanwhile, emphasizes the reader's active participation in realizing a literary work through its gaps and indeterminacies. AI extends this activity in a new direction. The reader may not only fill imaginative gaps while reading; they may request another textual realization that changes the gaps themselves.

There is, however, an essential difference between shaping the conditions of an experience and controlling its content. A requester who specifies every plot development may become less capable of being surprised. Reception-directed production is most interesting when the human retains meaningful uncertainty. The desired outcome is not necessarily an obedient reflection of existing preferences; it can be a work that exceeds the requester's imagination.

## 3. When Criticism Becomes Generative

The second transformation concerns criticism. In its familiar public form, art criticism interprets and evaluates a work, offers reasons for judgments, and invites others to agree or disagree. A critic might argue that a film's ending betrays its characters, that a painting's composition lacks balance, or that a novel resolves its moral conflict too easily. These judgments may influence later artistic practice, but the critical act does not ordinarily alter the object under discussion.

Generative AI makes possible a different sequence. A viewer who dislikes a film's ending can describe the defect to a system capable of producing an alternative scene. They can watch the result, identify new problems, and ask for another revision. The judgment becomes a production instruction. I call this **generative criticism**.

The phrase must be used carefully. Not every command to change a work is criticism in the intellectually substantial sense. “Make it happier” may express a preference without providing an interpretation or justification. A serious critical judgment might instead explain that a happy ending undermines the story's established moral stakes, or that an abrupt tragedy has not been prepared by earlier scenes. Generative criticism is most developed when an evaluative reason guides a proposed transformation and the resulting version can itself be assessed against that reason.

Its philosophical importance lies in the shortening of a feedback loop. Evaluation, which previously often stood outside production, can become part of a continuous practice of making, receiving, and remaking. The audience member need not master the technical means of production to initiate that loop. But the loop does not eliminate the distinction between the person who specifies a change and the system that executes it. Nor does it establish that the requester is the sole author of the result.

Marcel Duchamp's “The Creative Act” (1957) offers a useful antecedent. Duchamp treats the spectator's contribution to the realization of art as indispensable: an artist's intention does not exhaust a work's significance. Roland Barthes's “The Death of the Author” (1967) likewise challenges the idea that the author's intention possesses final authority over meaning. In *S/Z* (1970), Barthes's contrast between readerly and writerly texts complicates the opposition between consumption and production. Yet none of these arguments implies that a reader's interpretation literally changes the original artifact. Generative criticism adds a material possibility to an interpretive one: a judgment can now produce a distinct successor work.

## 4. Transformative Reception and the Identity of the Work

A third development arises when the audience begins not with a blank request but with existing art. Someone may admire *Romeo and Juliet* while wishing to explore a version in which the lovers survive. Another may want a familiar painting reimagined with different lighting or a symphony arranged for a smaller ensemble. With AI, the receiver may become the initiator of a new variant.

This practice can be called **transformative reception**: an encounter with an existing work leads the recipient to generate a modified or derivative work responsive to their own interpretation, curiosity, or preference. It is important not to confuse this with mere interpretation. Believing that a tragedy could have ended differently is one activity; producing a new text in which it does end differently is another.

The distinction raises a question about artistic identity. Does the new version count as the same artwork, a performance of it, an adaptation, or a separate derivative work? No universal answer follows from the presence of AI. A corrected punctuation mark, a new musical arrangement, a rewritten final act, and an image that borrows only a color palette involve different relations to their sources. The original does not disappear because a new version exists. Where a work is conceived as variable or interactive, however, the range of legitimate realizations may be part of its identity from the outset.

Umberto Eco's *The Open Work* (1962/1989) illuminates this distinction. Eco examines works that invite multiple realizations and interpretations, while refusing the idea that openness means the absence of form or constraints. A generative variation may be invited by a work's structure, or it may depart so radically that it is better understood as another work. The technological ease of transformation cannot decide the philosophical question of identity.

Nor is the underlying cultural practice new. Henry Jenkins's *Textual Poachers* (1992) shows how fans appropriate, reinterpret, and rewrite stories they love. Lawrence Lessig's *Remix* (2008) distinguishes a culture organized around reception from one in which users can also rewrite and recombine media. These histories matter because they identify the true novelty more precisely. AI does not invent the wish to remake culture. It can make sophisticated forms of remaking accessible to people who previously lacked the time, collaborators, or technical ability to execute them.

## 5. Aesthetic Agency Is Not the Same as Authorship

The three developments—reception-directed production, generative criticism, and transformative reception—suggest an expansion of **aesthetic agency**. By this I mean a person's practical capacity to shape the conditions under which they encounter, evaluate, select, and revise aesthetic objects. Aesthetic agency includes more than passive consumption but need not include the skills or achievements traditionally associated with artistic making.

This distinction avoids two opposite mistakes. The first is to say that a prompt writer is automatically the artist simply because they caused an image or story to exist. Causal contribution alone is insufficient: a patron, publisher, or software designer may also be causally necessary. Artistic authorship depends on more specific questions about conception, control, decisions, execution, responsibility, and social recognition. The second mistake is to say that a person who does not personally draw, compose, or write has no meaningful creative agency. Commissioning, directing, selecting, arranging, and editing can be aesthetically consequential even when they do not settle who deserves the title of artist.

Kant's *Critique of Judgment* (1790) adds a further complication. For Kant, a judgment of beauty arises from subjective pleasure but speaks as though others ought to be able to share it; it is not merely a report that an object gratifies a private appetite. A person who generates an artwork for personal enjoyment may later share it, inviting others to find it beautiful or moving. The transition from private satisfaction to public appreciation is philosophically significant. It shows why aesthetic reception cannot be reduced without remainder to consumer preference.

Indeed, the distinction between the agreeable and the beautiful creates a challenge for personalized art. If a user repeatedly modifies every disconcerting feature until a work matches their tastes, the result may become more agreeable while losing the capacity to demand reflection or change their judgment. Reception-directed production therefore expands control without guaranteeing richer aesthetic experience.

## 6. Three Objections

### Objection One: Personalization Domesticates Art

A strong objection is that art often matters because it resists us. A tragic ending, an unsettling image, or an unfamiliar musical structure may be valuable precisely because it frustrates our expectations. If AI allows every audience member to replace discomfort with reassurance, does it not turn art into personalized entertainment?

The concern is real. Jauss's account of changing horizons of expectation and Dewey's account of experience both suggest that valuable encounters can transform the recipient rather than merely satisfy an antecedent desire. Yet the objection does not refute reception-directed production as such. A person can ask to be challenged, surprised, or confronted with an unfamiliar perspective. They can also deliberately refrain from revising a difficult work. Greater control creates a temptation to avoid resistance; it does not logically require that avoidance.

The defensible conclusion is conditional: **AI expands the audience's power to shape aesthetic experience, but the quality of that experience depends partly on how the power is exercised.** A mature aesthetic practice may include the ability to leave an unwelcome ending intact.

### Objection Two: Revision Destroys the Common Work

A second objection concerns shared culture. If everyone reads a personally rewritten novel or watches a different film ending, public criticism may lose its common object. People could become enclosed within private variations, unable to debate what a work actually does.

This is not merely nostalgia for a fixed canon. Publicly shared works allow disagreements to be about something held in common. When critics dispute the ending of a novel, their disagreement is intelligible partly because they refer to the same ending.

The answer is not to deny this loss. Instead, we should distinguish the **source work** from its **derivative branches** and preserve clear attribution and version histories where possible. A public discussion can still concern the source, while other discussions compare adaptations and ask what their differences reveal. Transformative reception may enrich a culture of interpretation if it retains its points of reference; it may impoverish that culture if every variant erases its origin.

### Objection Three: The Audience Has No Right to Rewrite

A third objection is ethical and legal. The technical ability to transform an artwork does not establish the right to do so. Copyright, licensing, contractual restrictions, moral rights, and the interests of living creators may limit copying, adaptation, and distribution. A private experiment and a commercially circulated derivative work can raise very different issues.

These constraints do not make the philosophical account irrelevant. They show that **capacity, authorship, and permission are separate questions**. An audience member may have the technical capacity to generate a new version without acquiring legal authority to publish it or moral entitlement to present it as the creator's own. Responsible transformative reception requires attention to provenance, consent where appropriate, and the distinction between homage, criticism, appropriation, and substitution.

There is also a question of labor. The apparent ease of AI production depends on systems developed and maintained by many people, and potentially on training materials whose use is contested. The user's expanded agency should not make those contributions invisible.

## 7. From a Linear Model to a Recursive Practice

The familiar linear picture runs from artist to work to audience to critic. The AI-enabled alternative is better represented as a loop: a prospective audience member requests a work; the system produces an artifact; the human receives and evaluates it; that evaluation may become a revision request; a new artifact is generated and received. At any point, the person may accept a version, share it, return to the source, or stop.

The loop is not a universal definition of art. Many artworks will continue to be made by human artists for audiences who cannot and do not wish to change them. Some works are valued partly because of the artist's bodily practice, historical situation, or distinctive achievement. Others are valuable as records, testimony, or gifts whose origins cannot be replaced by an aesthetically similar simulation. The proposed model describes an additional configuration of practice, not the disappearance of earlier ones.

Its central philosophical consequence is a shift in where initiative can reside. The desire to **receive** an aesthetic experience can become a sufficient practical reason to initiate its **production**. The act of **judging** can become a way of directing its **revision**. And the act of **interpreting** an inherited work can become the starting point for a new, materially different version.

This redistribution of initiative does not dissolve every distinction among artist, audience, and critic. It makes those distinctions increasingly dependent on the particular action being performed rather than on a person's fixed social identity.

## Conclusion

Generative AI is often discussed as a challenge to the artist: can a machine create, and does human authorship survive when execution is automated? Those questions matter, but they overlook an equally important change on the receiving side. A person may now initiate an artwork because they wish to experience it, encounter something they did not anticipate, criticize it, and convert that criticism into a new version. They may also remake works they already know, creating derivative branches that express alternative possibilities.

The best account of this development is neither that audiences have suddenly become artists nor that artistic production has collapsed into consumption. It is that **reception has become a more direct site of aesthetic agency**. Its most distinctive possibilities lie in the conjunction of uncertainty and control: the audience can guide what is made without knowing in advance everything that will be experienced, and can revise what has been received without necessarily claiming ownership of the source.

That possibility is both emancipatory and risky. It can democratize experimentation and enable new forms of criticism, but it can also reward conformity to preference, weaken shared objects of discussion, and obscure questions of attribution and permission. A philosophy of AI art must therefore ask not only who made the work, but also who initiated it, who receives it, who can revise it, and what is gained—or lost—when these roles converge.
