# From Spectator to Co-Producer: Generative AI and the Transformation of Artistic Reception

## Executive Summary

Generative AI changes the philosophy of art not simply because machines can now produce convincing images, stories, and music, but because it alters the structure of relations among artist, audience, critic, editor, and curator. The familiar model of artistic communication begins with an artist who makes or expresses something, proceeds through an artwork, and ends with an audience that receives and evaluates it. That model was never absolute: readers interpret texts, spectators help constitute aesthetic experience, fans rewrite stories, and artists appropriate earlier works. Dewey, Iser, Jauss, Barthes, Duchamp, Eco, Jenkins, Bourriaud, Lessig, and Rancière all, in different ways, weakened the idea of a passive receiver. Dewey argued that the beholder must actively recreate an experience; Iser distinguished the author's text from its realization by the reader; Jauss located works within historically changing horizons of expectation; Barthes wanted the reader to cease being merely a consumer; Duchamp explicitly described artist and spectator as the two poles of the creative act; and Rancière rejected the equation of spectatorship with passivity.

Generative AI nevertheless introduces a significant new condition. A recipient can now ask for an artwork because of an aesthetic experience the recipient wishes to have, receive an unpredictable result, criticize that result, convert the criticism immediately into instructions for revision, select among alternatives, and then publish the favored version for other audiences. This essay calls the first phenomenon **reception-driven creation**, the second **generative criticism**, and the third **audience-led revision**. The central thesis is that generative AI does not abolish the distinction among artist, audience, and critic, but turns roles that were often institutionally separated into positions that one person can occupy sequentially within a single recursive process. Contemporary philosophy of AI art has begun examining precisely such questions of artistic agency, aesthetic reception, aesthetic bias, and appreciative skill, although this particular synthesis remains underdeveloped. The 2026 special issue of *The Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism* includes work on AI creativity, aesthetic bias, appreciative deskilling, artistic agency, and the appreciation of AI art, demonstrating that reception is becoming as important a philosophical problem as authorship.

The argument must nevertheless resist an overly technological conclusion. AI did not invent active audiences, remixing, fan fiction, appropriation, or artist-as-curator practices. Duchamp's readymades challenged the identification of artistry with manual fabrication; Jenkins documented fans becoming cultural producers from materials supplied by mass media; Bourriaud analyzed art constructed from already circulating cultural objects; and Lessig contrasted “read-only” cultural consumption with participatory “read-write” culture. Generative AI is philosophically distinctive primarily because it reduces the practical distance between reception, judgment, and production.

The strongest version of the thesis therefore does not say that every person who prompts a model is an artist. It says that generative systems make **aesthetic reception itself operational**: a response such as “the ending is emotionally false,” “the harmony is too sentimental,” or “the image should feel colder and less symmetrical” can cease to be commentary upon a finished work and become causal input into the production of another one. This transformation raises difficult questions about authorship, derivative works, personalization, aesthetic value, echo chambers, and even the identity of artworks themselves. Current U.S. copyright policy, for example, distinguishes AI assistance from human authorship and treats human selection, arrangement, and creative modification differently from purely machine-determined expressive material; this legal distinction parallels, without settling, the philosophical problem of how creative agency should be distributed.

## Thesis and Conceptual Framework

A conventional theory of artistic communication is easily represented as **artist → artwork → audience**. The artist intends, expresses, constructs, or selects; the artwork embodies the result; the audience encounters and judges it. No major philosophy of art has ever fit this diagram perfectly, but it captures an influential social organization of artistic roles. Generative AI enables a different circuit: **prospective audience → generation → reception → criticism → revision → selection → publication → further reception**. The initiating human may begin not with something already formulated for expression but with a desire to encounter something not yet known: a mystery that will genuinely surprise its requester, a song with a particular emotional trajectory, or an image whose atmosphere can be specified without determining its details. This is the core of reception-driven creation.

Three modes of artistic initiation should therefore be distinguished. **Expression-driven creation** begins from something the maker wishes to articulate: an idea, emotion, perception, argument, formal problem, or vision. **Curation-driven creation** begins from selection and arrangement: existing or generated material becomes significant through choosing, sequencing, contextualizing, or presenting it. Duchamp's readymades are the paradigmatic historical challenge to the assumption that art requires artisanal manufacture, since designating and presenting ordinary manufactured objects destabilized the traditional skilled-maker model.

**Reception-driven creation**, by contrast, begins from a desired encounter: “I want to see, hear, or read something of this kind, but I do not yet know exactly what it will be.”

The categories overlap. A novelist using AI may have an expressive project, curate outputs, and also preserve enough unpredictability to become a genuine first reader. The distinction concerns the organizing purpose, not an exclusive psychological state. This is important because it prevents the argument from collapsing AI use into either traditional authorship or passive consumption.

Reception-driven creation also produces **generative criticism**. Ordinary criticism can describe, interpret, compare, evaluate, or justify a judgment. Generative criticism does something further: it supplies a revision criterion. “The protagonist's conversion is unearned” becomes “rewrite the preceding scenes so that the conversion is psychologically prepared.” “The visual composition is too balanced” becomes “preserve the subject but introduce asymmetry and visual tension.” Criticism thereby acquires a productive function without ceasing to be criticism.

Finally, **audience-led revision** occurs when a recipient transforms a work or generates a successor version according to the recipient's own aesthetic judgment. This practice has clear predecessors in adaptation, arrangement, parody, fan fiction, collage, sampling, and remix. Jenkins's account of fandom already presented media fans as active producers and manipulators of meanings rather than passive consumers; Bourriaud later described contemporary artists working directly with cultural products already in circulation. AI's novelty lies less in inventing transformation than in making transformation conversational, rapid, iterative, and available across multiple media.

## Reception Theory and Historical Precedents

The philosophical foundation for this view is stronger than it might initially appear.

| Thinker | Central idea | Relevance to AI reception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kant | Taste is subjective yet claims a kind of communicable or universal validity | Explains why a creator-recipient may want others to endorse an initially personal aesthetic judgment |
| Dewey | Aesthetic experience joins doing and undergoing; the beholder actively recreates the work | Weakens the producer/receiver opposition before AI |
| Jauss | Reception occurs within a historically formed horizon of expectations | AI can turn audience expectations into inputs for production |
| Iser | The work emerges between text and reader; readers realize indeterminate possibilities | AI lets the reader affect not only realization but subsequent textual form |
| Barthes | The reader should cease being merely a consumer and become productive | Anticipates the collapse of rigid producer/consumer roles |
| Duchamp | Artist and spectator are two poles of the creative act; selection can itself be artistic | Precedent for curatorial agency and reduced importance of manual execution |
| Eco | “Open” works invite plural realization while still possessing constraints | Helps distinguish interpretation from unrestricted alteration |
| Jenkins | Fans appropriate mass-media texts and become cultural producers | Direct predecessor of audience-led rewriting and fan fiction |
| Bourriaud | Postproduction uses already existing cultural products as artistic material | Frames preexisting art as material for further making |
| Lessig | Read/write culture contrasts participatory cultural production with read-only consumption | Technological precursor to AI-assisted remix |
| Rancière | Spectators observe, select, compare, interpret, and appropriate | Rejects passivity as the defining condition of spectatorship |
| Lopes; Vlaad & Paul | AI redistributes artistic agency and requires a theory of reception as well as production | Provides the contemporary bridge to generative systems |

The classical lineage is especially clear in Dewey. *Art as Experience* does not treat the perceiver as a terminal destination for an already complete aesthetic object. For Dewey, perception itself demands active organization; the experiencer reconstructs relations comparable to those involved in production. His broader theory makes “doing” and “undergoing” interdependent aspects of experience. AI radicalizes this reciprocity: undergoing the generated result can trigger another literal act of doing.

Iser makes the same movement in literary terms. He describes the literary work as possessing an “artistic” pole supplied by the authorial text and an “aesthetic” pole realized by the reader; textual gaps invite active completion. Generative AI introduces a novel reversal. A reader who fills an interpretive gap may now ask the system to materialize that filling as a new passage, ending, point of view, or entire variant. What was formerly a mental realization can become an externally instantiated successor text.

Jauss's “horizon of expectations” offers both support and warning. Works enter historically structured fields of genre conventions, prior works, and audience expectations; aesthetically significant works may achieve distance from those expectations rather than merely fulfilling them. Thus a model that perfectly supplies whatever a user already prefers may be aesthetically conservative. Reception-driven creation becomes philosophically interesting not when AI acts as a perfect gratification machine, but when it creates a productive relation between expectation and surprise.

Barthes and Rancière further erode the passive-audience picture. Barthes's distinction between readerly and writerly texts explicitly associates literary value with turning readers from consumers into producers, while Rancière argues that spectators already act by observing, selecting, comparing, translating, and appropriating what they encounter. AI adds neither interpretive intelligence nor freedom from nothing; it gives those already active receptive practices a new capacity to generate artifacts.

## Role Convergence in Generative AI

The most useful taxonomy therefore treats “artist,” “audience,” “critic,” “editor/reviser,” and “curator” as functional roles rather than permanently separate persons. The artist initiates or shapes production; the audience undergoes the resulting experience; the critic interprets and evaluates; the editor or reviser changes material in response to judgment; the curator selects and frames one possibility among alternatives. Generative AI allows a single participant to move through these roles in minutes.

**The recursive process:**

1. **Prospective audience** — desires an experience.
2. **AI generation** — produces a work.
3. **Audience** — receives the result.
4. **Critic** — interprets and evaluates it.
5. **Reviser / editor** — turns judgment into instructions.
6. **AI regeneration** — creates a successor version.
7. **Curator** — selects and frames a version.
8. **Public audience** — encounters the selected work and may initiate further revision or remix.

This recursive structure helps clarify three kinds of case. In AI image generation, a viewer can specify an atmosphere rather than a completed composition, inspect outputs, diagnose undesirable features, alter composition or style, and select among generations. Contemporary scholarship already notes that generative image systems complicate simple “tool” metaphors because their outputs are not controlled continuously like a brush; Lopes therefore distinguishes the intentional human artist from the partially unpredictable generative process rather than straightforwardly identifying the system as an artist.

In AI-assisted fiction, the case is even clearer. Someone might request a detective story while explicitly asking not to know the murderer in advance. The commissioning user causes the work to exist yet remains epistemically situated like a reader. After reading, the same person can criticize pacing, characterization, or resolution and generate a revision. Authorship, reception, and editing have not become identical concepts; they have become roles occupied sequentially by one agent.

In AI music, a listener might request a composition with a certain instrumentation, tempo, or emotional movement, hear an unfamiliar result, and revise it from the standpoint of listening rather than score-writing. The 2026 *JAAC* special issue includes separate studies of generative AI and musical creativity, signaling that the musical case raises its own questions about interpretation, performance, style, and agency rather than merely reproducing debates about images.

Pre-AI practices show why the correct claim is acceleration and convergence rather than historical rupture. Duchamp's readymades made selection and designation aesthetically central; fan fiction allowed audiences to rewrite beloved fictional worlds; remix culture turned existing recordings and images into materials; Bourriaud's “postproduction” characterized artists as users of already circulating cultural forms. Generative AI combines these operations with an interactive production mechanism responsive to ordinary-language evaluation.

## Objections and Replies

The **first objection concerns authorship**. If the model determines most expressive detail, perhaps the user is only a customer. This objection succeeds against an indiscriminate claim that every prompt constitutes artistry. Contemporary legal analysis makes a similar distinction: the U.S. Copyright Office states that, with currently available systems, prompts alone ordinarily do not provide sufficient control over expressive elements for copyright authorship, while sufficiently creative human selection, arrangement, or modification may be protectable. Philosophically, however, copyright authorship and artistic agency are not identical. The better thesis is scalar: some AI interactions involve minimal agency; others involve sustained conception, evaluation, revision, selection, and framing. Reception-driven creation names a structural possibility, not an honorific automatically bestowed on every user.

A **second objection says that preference satisfaction is not aesthetic value**. If users continually modify a tragedy until nobody dies, remove dissonance from difficult music, or beautify every disturbing image, AI may produce more pleasure while producing less art of consequence. Kant is useful here because judgments of beauty are not mere reports of what one privately happens to like; they characteristically reach toward communicability or shared validity. Jauss adds that aesthetically important works may challenge rather than simply satisfy an existing horizon of expectation. Reception-driven creation therefore needs a normative distinction between customization and aesthetic achievement. A work generated to please its first recipient may still fail as art.

A **third objection concerns repetition and aesthetic echo chambers**. This is no longer speculative in the philosophical literature. Declos argues that generative systems can encode aesthetic biases favoring features such as conventional beauty, symmetry, spectacle, and polished imagery, potentially homogenizing taste and producing “aesthetic bubbles.” Nannicelli separately argues that overreliance on AI may threaten appreciative skills when appreciation ceases to track rich forms of human agency. Vlaad and Paul propose “discerning openness” as an alternative both to dismissing AI art categorically and to accepting it naïvely. These criticisms strengthen rather than refute the present thesis: if receivers increasingly influence what gets generated, then the quality of reception becomes ethically and aesthetically consequential. A narrow receiver may generate a narrow cultural world.

The **fourth objection is legal and ethical**. Audience-led revision can involve copyrighted source works, recognizable styles, voices, characters, or protected expressive material. The mere technical capacity to transform a work does not determine whether copying, adaptation, publication, or commercialization is legally permissible. U.S. copyright authorities currently distinguish human-authored material, AI-generated material, and human creative modification, while a separate part of the Copyright Office's AI study addresses training on copyrighted works and licensing questions. Ethical issues can also exceed copyright: attribution, consent, cultural appropriation, deception, and respect for living artists remain relevant even where a particular use is legally permitted. A philosophy of reception-driven art therefore cannot equate capacity to revise with entitlement to revise publicly.

## Ontology, Criticism, and Aesthetic Judgment

The deepest consequence concerns the ontology of the artwork. Traditional works can certainly exist in versions—performances of a symphony, editions of a novel, stagings of a play—but generative systems make branching variation potentially constitutive of ordinary reception. A generated work may be less like a fixed terminal object and more like a node within a version tree: version A generates criticism; criticism generates B and C; one recipient selects B, another transforms C, and later audiences branch again.

Eco's *The Open Work* is an important precursor because it examines works deliberately structured to permit multiple realizations and stresses interaction between work and interpreter. Yet openness does not mean that every transformation remains numerically the same artwork. The very problem AI exposes is where to draw the boundary between interpretation of X, realization of X, revision of X, adaptation from X, and a new work Y. Eco's account of openness is valuable precisely because it keeps plurality from collapsing into limitless identity.

This suggests a distinction between **fixed-work ontology** and **generative-work ontology**. In the former, the authoritative work is comparatively stable and audience alterations are adaptations or derivatives. In the latter, variation may be specified as part of the work's identity from the beginning. An AI-native narrative might be designed so that every reader receives a different plot while recurring structural rules constitute the work. In such cases, asking “What is the artwork?” may require referring not to any one output but to a rule-governed generative system, interaction protocol, or family of realizations. Contemporary AI aesthetics is already treating ontology as a distinct field of inquiry: the 2026 *JAAC* special issue explicitly separates ontological questions about what AI art is from appreciative questions about its properties and human-value questions about its effects.

The same shift affects criticism. A conventional critic typically addresses a work shared with other readers: “Here is why this ending fails.” A generative critic may instead transform that claim into “Here is the ending I think the work should have had.” Critical argument and counterexample can therefore converge: rather than merely describing a preferable possibility, the critic can instantiate one. This is generative criticism in its strongest form. The resulting object does not prove the criticism correct, but it can function as an aesthetic experiment. One can compare original and revision and ask which better realizes a stated value.

Kant's problem of aesthetic judgment thus returns in a new technological setting. A person begins with a private encounter, modifies an artifact according to that judgment, and then offers the result to others hoping that the preference proves shareable rather than merely idiosyncratic. Generative AI does not eliminate the social test of taste. Paradoxically, it may make that test more important: when everyone can produce a version optimized to personal preference, the philosophically significant question becomes why anyone else should care.

## Research Gaps and Future Questions

The most important research gap is therefore not whether AI “can make art” in the abstract. Recent scholarship already treats AI art as a field encompassing ontology, creativity, appreciation, aesthetic bias, human agency, artistic merit, and aesthetic flourishing. What remains comparatively undertheorized is the recursive unity of reception and production: the possibility that a recipient's response becomes the mechanism by which the next artwork is generated.

Several questions follow. At what point does an audience member become a co-author rather than a commissioner or curator? Is generative criticism genuinely criticism if it produces alternatives without publicly articulating reasons? What differentiates aesthetically valuable self-personalization from hedonic optimization? Can systems be designed to preserve productive surprise rather than merely model preferences? When an artwork branches indefinitely, which relations make later outputs versions, adaptations, or independent works? And if audiences increasingly generate exactly what they want, how can artistic cultures preserve the encounter with unwanted difficulty, unfamiliarity, and resistance that has historically expanded taste? Declos's concern about aesthetic bubbles, Nannicelli's concern about appreciative deskilling, and Vlaad and Paul's defense of discerning openness indicate that these are becoming central rather than peripheral questions for AI aesthetics.

The resulting thesis can therefore be stated precisely: **generative AI transforms artistic reception by making reception productive in a new operational sense**. The audience does not merely interpret what has already been made. It can initiate works for its own future experience, turn judgments into revisions, move among the roles of recipient, critic, editor, and curator, and offer selected results to further audiences. This development does not erase artists or make all consumers creators. It changes the topology of artistic agency from a largely directional relation into a recursive one. **The work can now return to its receiver, and the receiver's response can return immediately to the work.**

## Recommended Bibliography

The following twelve sources form a compact scholarly core. They combine reception theory, aesthetics, participatory culture, and recent AI-art philosophy.

1. **Kant, Immanuel.** 1790. *Critique of Judgment*. Especially the “Analytic of the Beautiful” on judgments of taste and their claim to shared validity.
2. **Dewey, John.** 1934. *Art as Experience*. Especially the account of doing/undergoing and the beholder's active recreation of the artwork.
3. **Duchamp, Marcel.** 1957. “The Creative Act.” Essential for the claim that artist and spectator constitute two poles of artistic creation.
4. **Jauss, Hans Robert.** 1982. *Toward an Aesthetic of Reception*. Use “horizon of expectations” to distinguish challenging art from simple preference fulfillment.
5. **Iser, Wolfgang.** 1972. “The Reading Process: A Phenomenological Approach.” Provides the artistic/aesthetic poles and the reader's active realization of textual possibilities.
6. **Barthes, Roland.** 1970/1974. *S/Z*. Use the readerly/writerly distinction and the ambition to transform the reader from consumer into producer.
7. **Eco, Umberto.** 1962/1989. *The Open Work*. Provides the strongest classical framework for plural realizations without making interpretation completely unconstrained.
8. **Jenkins, Henry.** 1992. *Textual Poachers: Television Fans and Participatory Culture*. Central precedent for audiences appropriating media and becoming producers through fan fiction and related practices.
9. **Bourriaud, Nicolas.** 2002. *Postproduction: Culture as Screenplay—How Art Reprograms the World*. Useful for treating existing cultural objects as materials for subsequent production.
10. **Rancière, Jacques.** 2009. *The Emancipated Spectator*. Provides the strongest philosophical rejection of the spectator/passivity equation.
11. **Lopes, Dominic McIver.** 2026. “AI Art and Artists: What They Are, What They Could Be, What They Should Be.” *The Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism* 84(2): 247–258. Especially useful for distinguishing intentional artistic agency from the generative system's partially autonomous operation.
12. **Vlaad, Sofie, and Elliot Samuel Paul.** 2026. “Appreciating AI Art: Aesthetic Vices, Virtues, and Values.” *The Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism* 84(2): 287–300. Particularly important because it explicitly shifts the AI-art debate toward reception and develops “discerning openness” as an aesthetic virtue.

Lessig's *Remix* is the most useful supplementary source if the essay expands beyond twelve references: his contrast between read-only and read/write culture provides an unusually clear bridge between older participatory media and AI-enabled audience transformation. Declos's 2026 “AI and Aesthetic Bias” and Nannicelli's 2026 “Artificial Intelligence, the Hedonic View of Appreciation and the Threat of Appreciative Deskilling” are the strongest additions if the objections concerning personalization, homogenization, and diminished appreciative capacities are expanded.
