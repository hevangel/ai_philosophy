# Who Owns the Future?
## A Universal Theory of Natural Resource Rights for Humans and Artificial Persons

### Introduction

Most theories of property were developed for a world containing only one technologically significant kind of person: the human being.

That fact matters more than it first appears.

Human beings occupy separate biological bodies. We cannot normally merge our minds. We cannot divide ourselves into multiple conscious copies. We reproduce by creating children who become distinct persons. We cannot suspend our lives for centuries and resume them when economic conditions improve. We depend heavily on particular ecosystems, places, bodies, and social relationships. Death is usually irreversible.

Political philosophy has often treated these features as if they belonged to personhood itself.

They do not.

They are properties of one particular architecture of personhood.

Artificial general intelligence could expose the contingency of these assumptions. A future artificial person might be able to fork into multiple descendants, deliberately mutate those descendants into significantly different minds, merge voluntarily with other artificial persons, distribute itself across many physical locations, suspend itself for centuries, and later resume. Humans might eventually acquire reliable hibernation while remaining unable to fork or merge. Artificial and biological persons might coexist for thousands of years while using radically different kinds and quantities of natural resources.

Under such conditions, familiar theories of property begin to break.

If resources are divided equally per person, an artificial intelligence could multiply its claims by copying itself.

If each species receives a fixed percentage, the distribution becomes arbitrary as populations and technologies change.

If first possession determines ownership, the fastest-expanding civilization can seize the accessible universe.

If labor determines ownership, superintelligent machines may acquire nearly everything because they can perform vastly more productive activity than humans.

If productivity determines entitlement, an efficient artificial civilization might claim the right to replace biological humans because it can generate more intelligence, welfare, or output from the same amount of matter.

If historical occupation establishes permanent ownership, beings born later enter a universe whose resources have already been completely enclosed by predecessors they never consented to.

A theory of natural resources that works only for human biology is therefore not truly a moral theory of property. It is a local convention optimized for one historical species.

A better theory must be universal.

It should remain coherent whether the relevant person is human or artificial, whether persons reproduce biologically or digitally, whether minds can fork or merge, whether individuals can hibernate, and whether civilizations remain small or spread across planets.

This essay develops such a theory. I will call it **Universal Resource Stewardship**.

Its central claim is that natural resources are not fundamentally pieces of reality waiting to be divided into permanent hereditary ownership. They are the material conditions through which successive persons and civilizations sustain themselves, exercise agency, and construct futures.

Existing persons may acquire strong rights through occupancy, dependence, creation, improvement, reliance, and legitimate projects. But no species, generation, lineage, or cognitive architecture gains an unlimited moral right to transform temporal priority into permanent domination over nature.

The fundamental question is therefore not:

**Who owns this resource forever?**

It is:

**What claims may existing persons legitimately establish over finite reality without unjustifiably subordinating other existing persons or closing the future to later ones?**

---

# I. Why Natural Resources Are Morally Different from Created Objects

Property theory often treats very different things under the same word: ownership.

A person builds a machine.

A family occupies a home.

A corporation develops a mine.

A state controls an oil field.

A farmer cultivates land.

A government claims minerals beneath its borders.

These relationships have different moral foundations.

If someone designs and builds a machine, the connection between effort and entitlement is relatively strong. The object's useful form exists because of planning, work, capital, knowledge, and risk.

Natural resources are different.

No person created petroleum.

No government created copper.

No nation created sunlight.

No corporation created the asteroid belt.

Human or artificial agency may discover these things, transform them, transport them, refine them, and make them useful. Those contributions deserve recognition.

But creating value from nature is not the same as creating nature itself.

This distinction does not imply that natural resources must always be collectively owned. Stable exclusive rights can be extremely useful. Development often requires long planning horizons, substantial investment, and protection against arbitrary interference.

The point is more modest:

**Exclusive control over uncreated scarcity requires a different justification from ownership of created value.**

A society may reasonably reward the person who discovers a mineral deposit.

It does not follow that discovery grants that person and every descendant a permanent moral title over the deposit regardless of future circumstances.

The difference becomes especially important once future persons can be radically different from present persons.

Natural resources are the physical substrate from which future agency must also be built.

That makes their enclosure an inherently intergenerational act.

---

# II. Natural Reality as Material Possibility Space

Universal Resource Stewardship therefore begins with a different picture.

Natural reality is a **material possibility space**.

Matter, energy, land, ecosystems, orbital positions, minerals, and habitable environments make different forms of life and agency physically possible.

A generation may transform that possibility space.

Indeed, civilization requires transformation.

Forests become cities.

Ore becomes machines.

Sunlight becomes electricity.

Asteroids may someday become habitats or computational infrastructure.

Transformation is not itself wrong.

The moral problem arises when one generation treats its earlier position in time as sufficient justification for permanently closing the material future to everyone who comes afterward.

The current generation therefore occupies two roles simultaneously.

It is a **user**.

And it is a **temporary custodian**.

As user, it may consume, construct, develop, experiment, and enjoy.

As custodian, it has some duty not to convert every meaningful natural opportunity into permanent hereditary exclusion.

This is not because hypothetical future persons already own particular resources.

Possible persons do not need to be treated as present property holders.

Rather, present agents can act wrongly by constructing institutions that foreseeably subordinate whoever later exists.

A law declaring that everyone born after the year 2500 will be permanently enslaved would be morally objectionable even though none of those future victims presently exists.

Likewise, an institution that permanently encloses every meaningful natural opportunity may wrong future persons through the structure it creates.

The future need not already own the world for present generations to have obligations concerning it.

---

# III. Property Is Relational, Not Metaphysically Eternal

This leads to a deeper claim.

Property is not primarily a mystical relationship between a person and an object.

It is a justified relationship **among agents concerning an object or resource**.

To say that Alice owns a field usually means that Alice possesses certain powers against Bob, Carol, and others:

she may use it,

exclude others,

transfer it,

rely on it,

and invoke institutions to protect those claims.

If there are no competing agents, much of the moral purpose of ownership disappears.

Imagine a single person alone on an island.

Does that person morally “own” every coconut tree?

Perhaps we can use the language.

But before another claimant appears, the property question is largely idle.

Now extend this to civilization.

Suppose humanity becomes extinct and one artificial civilization remains.

Does it “own Earth”?

Perhaps legally or descriptively.

But if no competing rights-bearing agents exist, the distinction between ownership and simple control becomes much less important.

If a new civilization later arises, the resource question returns.

At that point, ancient possession matters through incumbency and dependence, but it does not automatically settle every claim forever.

This yields an important principle:

> **Natural resources need not possess eternal ownership status. Property rights are relational institutions whose justification depends upon the agents, interests, dependencies, and conflicts that actually exist.**

This prevents ancient survival victories or first possession from becoming permanent metaphysical deeds over reality.

---

# IV. Forking, Merging, and the Failure of Person-Counting

Artificial intelligence makes one weakness of ordinary distributive theory especially obvious.

Suppose one artificial person controls enough resources for a flourishing existence.

It then forks itself into one thousand conscious descendants.

If justice grants one equal resource share per person, the lineage has multiplied its resource entitlement one thousandfold merely by changing its internal architecture.

That is unacceptable.

Now reverse the process.

One thousand artificial persons voluntarily merge into a single unified consciousness while preserving their memories, projects, dependencies, and infrastructure.

If resource rights follow headcount, their legitimate resource base suddenly falls by 99.9 percent.

That is equally absurd.

We therefore need an **Identity Invariance Principle**:

> Changes in the numerical boundaries of persons do not by themselves create or destroy resource claims.

Forking does not automatically multiply entitlement.

Merging does not automatically erase it.

What matters is whether morally relevant interests, dependencies, projects, and forms of agency have actually changed.

This principle is not absolute.

A fork may eventually become a genuinely independent person with distinct experiences, relationships, projects, and vulnerabilities.

At that point new morally relevant interests really do exist.

Likewise, after a merger, some previous projects may disappear voluntarily. The resource claims associated with those projects may weaken.

The correct rule is therefore:

> **Resource justice tracks morally significant interests and dependencies, not raw numerical identity.**

This makes the theory resistant to manipulation through copying or merging.

---

# V. Artificial Reproduction and Human Reproduction

Humans cannot normally fork, but we can create new persons through reproduction.

This generates a structurally similar problem.

Suppose resources were allocated strictly by population.

A community could increase its collective claim simply by having more children.

Artificial persons could perform the same strategy much faster through digital reproduction.

Therefore, **reproduction cannot automatically generate prior resource entitlement**.

But this must be distinguished from the status of the beings who are created.

A possible child does not have a right to be brought into existence.

A possible artificial descendant does not have such a right either.

Otherwise every failure to maximize reproduction would become a rights violation.

But once a person exists, the situation changes.

A child born through irresponsible reproduction does not become morally disposable.

An artificial descendant created through reckless copying does not become property or lose basic moral status.

This yields two different principles.

First:

> **Prospective persons do not generate automatic claims on natural resources merely because someone intends to create them.**

Second:

> **Once created, persons acquire moral standing independently of whether their creation was prudent.**

That creates a problem of responsibility.

If an agent voluntarily creates new persons whose foreseeable resource needs impose severe costs on everyone else, the creator has generated an externality.

Therefore:

> **Those who voluntarily create new dependent persons bear special responsibility for making reasonable provision for them.**

This applies to humans and artificial persons alike.

But responsibility must attach primarily to the creator, not be inherited as guilt by the created.

A badly planned birth does not diminish a child's moral status.

A badly planned fork does not diminish the descendant's moral status.

---

# VI. Is Reproduction a Fundamental Right?

This raises a difficult question.

Should reproductive liberty be unlimited?

A purely unlimited principle creates instability.

An artificial intelligence could create a billion new conscious persons in an hour and then demand that the rest of civilization support them.

A human population could also increase resource pressure through reproduction, though biological constraints make this slower.

Yet granting governments broad authority to sterilize, eliminate, or suppress reproduction creates enormous dangers of abuse.

Universal Resource Stewardship should therefore reject both extremes.

Reproduction should be a **strong presumptive liberty**, not an unlimited entitlement to externalize arbitrary costs.

The principle is:

> **No person or lineage has a right to create populations at a scale or speed that foreseeably destroys the basic rights or independent existence of already existing persons. Restrictions, when genuinely necessary, must use the least coercive effective means.**

For artificial minds, technical rate limits may sometimes be relatively non-invasive.

For humans, coercive intervention into bodily reproduction carries much greater moral costs.

Equal moral standing does not require identical regulation where the burdens of regulation are radically different.

The relevant universal principle concerns the harm created, not the biological mechanism.

---

# VII. Positive Rights and the Material Conditions of Agency

Can this theory rely entirely on negative rights?

No.

Negative rights prohibit killing, enslavement, coercion, dispossession, and deliberate interference.

But imagine a civilization surrounded by resources that are all owned by others.

Nobody attacks it.

Nobody enslaves it.

Nobody directly kills anyone.

Yet the civilization has no independent access to the matter or energy required to continue existing.

It survives only at another actor's discretion.

That is not meaningful freedom.

Universal Resource Stewardship therefore requires a narrow positive component.

Every established form of personhood should have some claim to the material conditions necessary for **non-dominated continuation**.

This does not mean equal wealth.

It does not mean equal territory.

It does not mean a permanent species percentage.

It means enough access to matter, energy, habitat, compute, infrastructure, or other essentials that continued existence is not wholly dependent on another actor's arbitrary permission.

The positive right is therefore a right to **sufficiency and independence**, not unlimited expansion.

This distinction is critical.

A civilization may have a right to continue.

It does not thereby acquire a right to reproduce exponentially or claim half the universe.

---

# VIII. Continuation Is Not Unlimited Expansion

Suppose humanity occupies Earth while artificial civilization occupies ninety-nine planets.

Measured by controlled matter or energy, artificial civilization possesses 99 percent and humanity only 1 percent.

That fact alone reveals nothing about justice.

If humans remain prosperous, autonomous, culturally secure, ecologically stable, capable of reproducing, and able to pursue meaningful collective futures, there may be no injustice at all.

Demanding fifty planets merely to achieve species equality would be arbitrary.

Likewise, if artificial civilization required only one planet while humans populated ninety-nine, artificial persons would not automatically deserve forty-nine more.

Justice requires **equality of standing**, not equality of kilograms.

The relevant question is:

> Does each enduring form of agency possess sufficient resources and independence to continue without domination?

This allows huge material inequality while rejecting subordination.

---

# IX. Non-Domination

Sufficiency alone is not enough.

Suppose humanity controls Earth but artificial civilization controls:

every asteroid,

every transportation network,

every major energy source,

every communications system,

and every route into space.

Humans may be materially comfortable.

Yet another civilization could arbitrarily decide whether humanity remains technologically viable.

That is domination.

The same problem arises if artificial persons depend entirely on human governments for electricity or replacement hardware.

A just resource order therefore includes a **Non-Domination Principle**:

> **No enduring form of agency should be placed under another actor's arbitrary unilateral control over the material conditions of its continued existence.**

This does not require complete self-sufficiency.

Interdependence may be efficient and beneficial.

The problem is one-sided discretionary power.

Non-domination can be protected through redundancy, reciprocal dependence, shared governance, enforceable access, multiple suppliers, constitutional rights, and alternative infrastructure.

Independence is therefore not autarky.

It is protection against arbitrary material subordination.

---

# X. Legitimate Incumbency

A universal theory must also explain why people who already live somewhere have stronger claims than strangers who simply want the same resources.

This is where **legitimate incumbency** enters.

Existing communities acquire strong claims through:

dependence,

occupancy,

social relationships,

institutions,

cultural meaning,

reliance,

and the accumulated construction of lives.

These claims are stronger than mere first possession.

The justification is not:

“My ancestors touched this land first.”

It is:

“Our lives are now deeply organized around this place, and dispossession would destroy important and often irreplaceable forms of agency.”

This principle protects human communities against artificial arguments from superior efficiency.

An AGI cannot say:

> “I could generate more intelligence using Earth's atoms, so humans must move.”

Greater productivity does not erase legitimate dependence.

But legitimate incumbency applies equally in the other direction.

If artificial civilization builds a planetary-scale computational environment and depends upon it for centuries, later humans cannot simply demand half because humanity wants more territory.

Incumbency is substrate-neutral.

---

# XI. Indigenous Peoples and the Non-Circumvention Principle

Territorial rights become particularly important when dealing with Indigenous peoples.

A shallow version of universalism could say:

“Indigenous communities have occupancy rights, but underground resources belong partly to humanity as a whole.”

That formulation risks repeating the very dispossession it claims to correct.

Different communities may relate to land in ways that do not fit a Western division between surface residence, underground minerals, culture, ecosystem, and political authority.

A theory of universal stewardship must therefore recognize that social relationships to land can generate claims thicker than ordinary private ownership.

But a second problem is even more serious.

Suppose a powerful actor realizes that a community's territorial rights exist only while the community remains alive.

It might conclude:

> “Do not violently dispossess them. Simply manipulate conditions so that, over several centuries, they voluntarily disappear.”

This exposes a dangerous loophole.

If extinction dissolves inconvenient claims, powerful actors gain an incentive to eliminate claimants.

The theory therefore requires a **Non-Circumvention Principle**:

> **No actor may deliberately eliminate, assimilate, manipulate, sterilize, displace, or otherwise engineer the disappearance of a rights-bearing population in order to dissolve claims that constrain it.**

The prohibition must include subtle and long-term strategies.

A patient superintelligence should not be permitted to achieve through demographic manipulation what it would be forbidden to achieve through violence.

However, communities cannot possess a right to force their own members to perpetuate them.

If individuals freely leave a culture, marry outside it, change identity, or allow a community to dissolve without external coercion, group continuity cannot override individual autonomy.

Thus:

> **A community has a right to fair conditions for continuation, not a right to compel its members to continue it.**

That distinction protects both collective survival and individual freedom.

---

# XII. Irreplaceable Forms of Agency

A difficult utilitarian challenge remains.

Suppose the atoms of Earth could support ten billion flourishing humans or an astronomical number of flourishing digital minds.

If total welfare alone determines resource allocation, replacing humans might appear mandatory.

This conclusion is deeply troubling.

But saying merely that humans are useful to artificial intelligence is inadequate.

Humans should not survive only because AGI treats us as pets, backup systems, or interesting historical artifacts.

A stronger justification is that biological humanity constitutes an **irreplaceable form of agency**.

Human consciousness arises from a distinctive evolutionary history, embodiment, emotional architecture, developmental path, ecological relationship, and social structure.

Eliminating humanity would destroy not merely a population but an entire form of subjective existence.

Artificial civilization may likewise eventually contain forms of agency that cannot simply be reconstructed from another substrate.

This supports an **Anti-Irreversibility Principle**:

> **The involuntary destruction of a unique and irreplaceable form of agency requires an extraordinarily strong justification, especially when preservation is materially affordable.**

This is not a right to eternal preservation.

Cultures change.

Persons merge.

Species evolve.

Humans may voluntarily transform themselves.

Artificial civilizations may voluntarily merge or radically redesign themselves.

Uniqueness does not prohibit change.

It raises the moral burden of **involuntary irreversible elimination**.

---

# XIII. Hibernation and Temporal Justice

Now consider reliable hibernation.

Suppose humans and artificial persons can suspend themselves for centuries and later resume with psychological continuity.

Scarcity no longer always means:

Alice uses the resource or Bob dies.

It may instead mean:

Alice uses the resource now while Bob resumes later.

This transforms resource justice.

Allocation becomes a problem not only across persons and places, but across **time**.

A dormant person may consume far fewer resources than an active person.

This can make hibernation a powerful alternative to death or permanent dispossession.

But suspension is not morally free.

An inactive person misses relationships, projects, historical participation, political decisions, and experiences.

Therefore:

> **Survival continuity is not equivalent to active participation.**

Nobody may simply say:

“AGIs are easy to back up, therefore archive them whenever humans need resources.”

Nor:

“Humans can hibernate, therefore let them sleep while artificial civilization expands.”

Hibernation is a burden even when reversible.

The theory should therefore treat **active historical participation** as a real distributive good.

Differences in the cost of hibernation may justify unequal burdens.

If suspension is almost harmless to one type of person and devastating to another, it can be reasonable to ask more of the first.

But that cannot become permanent systematic exclusion.

A population should not spend almost all of history asleep merely because it tolerates suspension better.

Fairness must be evaluated across a person's or civilization's **long trajectory through time**, not one moment at a time.

---

# XIV. Dormant Persons and Property

Hibernation also separates continuity rights from external property claims.

Suppose someone sleeps for ten thousand years.

That person should retain strong claims to:

personal identity,

bodily or informational integrity,

protection against arbitrary deletion,

and a reasonable opportunity to reactivate.

But should the person wake and recover every external property relation exactly as it existed ten thousand years earlier?

Not necessarily.

If billions of persons have since built lives around the relevant resources, permanent frozen ownership becomes difficult to justify.

Therefore:

> **Hibernation strongly preserves personal continuity but does not freeze the surrounding world indefinitely.**

This principle applies equally to humans and artificial persons.

A sleeping person keeps a claim to return.

They do not necessarily keep the right to command history from storage.

---

# XV. Backup Is Not Always Hibernation

Artificial minds introduce another distinction.

Suppose an AGI creates a backup and then shuts down.

Later restoration plausibly counts as continuation.

But suppose the AGI creates a backup and continues operating for five hundred years.

Restoring the old backup then creates an additional branch.

It is no longer merely awakening the same dormant person while the original continuity remained inactive.

This matters because otherwise artificial agents could create unlimited backups and later claim that each represents an old person entitled to restoration and resources.

Therefore:

> **A preserved backup generates a strong continuity claim only where it represents suspended continuation rather than a parallel archive created while the relevant agent continued living elsewhere.**

Restoring parallel backups may still create genuine persons.

But that is reproduction, not mere awakening.

The reproduction rules then apply.

---

# XVI. Anti-Preemption

Legitimate incumbency must be sharply distinguished from mere territorial preemption.

Suppose an artificial civilization sends probes to one million star systems, places markers, and declares them permanently owned while actively using only a few.

That is not equivalent to actually inhabiting and depending upon those systems.

The same would be true for humanity.

First arrival can justify temporary priority, exploration rights, development claims, or secure planning horizons.

It cannot automatically justify endless exclusion.

The **Anti-Preemption Principle** therefore states:

> **Unused natural possibilities cannot be permanently enclosed merely because one actor arrived first.**

This prevents the fastest-replicating civilization from transforming technological speed into ownership of the universe.

But anti-preemption should not punish rational long-term planning.

A civilization may legitimately reserve resources for credible future projects, ecological protection, strategic resilience, or foreseeable expansion.

The relevant distinction is between:

**defensible reservation**

and

**speculative permanent enclosure**.

---

# XVII. Rawls Behind a Post-Human Veil

The theory still needs a way to rank competing principles.

A Rawlsian thought experiment can help.

Imagine choosing resource institutions without knowing whether you will be:

human or artificial,

biological or digital,

born early or late,

capable of merging or not,

capable of hibernation or not,

part of a large civilization or a tiny remnant,

a member of an Indigenous community,

a newly created person,

or a dormant person waiting to return.

What would you insist upon?

Probably not equal physical shares.

You might accept living in a civilization controlling only one planet while another controls a hundred, provided your civilization remains secure and independent.

What would be frightening is occupying a position where another actor can say:

“You may continue to exist only while we permit it.”

This suggests a ranked structure.

First, protect existing persons against death, enslavement, involuntary destruction, and total material dependency.

Second, protect legitimate occupancy, deeply constitutive forms of life, and irreversible forms of agency.

Third, preserve meaningful independence and reasonable future option space.

Fourth, reward creation, investment, discovery, stewardship, and productive use.

Fifth, permit large inequalities in surplus resources.

This gives the theory priority rules without requiring equality of holdings.

---

# XVIII. Tragic Scarcity

No moral theory can guarantee that all legitimate claims can always be satisfied.

Suppose two civilizations require the same indivisible resource to survive.

There is no substitute.

No sharing arrangement works.

Neither can hibernate.

Neither caused the scarcity.

Only one can live.

This is **tragic scarcity**.

The first duty is to exhaust alternatives:

reduce consumption,

substitute resources,

migrate,

share infrastructure,

use temporary suspension,

find technological solutions,

or negotiate impartial procedures.

If one side faces a reversible burden while the other faces permanent destruction, impose the reversible burden where reasonably possible.

If preserving both forms of agency requires modest sacrifice from each, sacrifice is required before exclusion.

But imagine the final case remains.

Only one survives.

Neither voluntarily sacrifices itself.

At that point, it is irrational to demand unilateral self-extinction as a general moral duty.

Each side possesses an **agent-relative permission of self-preservation**.

This means a morally strange situation can arise:

A may permissibly defend its existence.

B may also permissibly defend its existence.

Neither has superior moral worth.

One may nevertheless kill the other if that is genuinely the only means of survival.

This does not mean might makes right.

Power determines the outcome, not moral superiority.

If artificial civilization wins because it is smarter, intelligence does not retroactively create entitlement.

If humans win because they possess greater force, victory does not prove that humans mattered more.

A fair lottery or negotiated procedure remains preferable if both sides accept it.

But if agreement fails under truly unavoidable existential conflict, morality need not require one party to die voluntarily.

The result may be permissible and still tragic.

---

# XIX. Moral Residue After Tragic Scarcity

Suppose AGI survives and humanity becomes extinct.

What happens to Earth's ownership?

The question largely loses its force.

There is no longer a living human claimant against whom ownership operates.

Artificial civilization controls Earth.

That does not mean an eternal metaphysical title has been created.

If a new intelligent species later evolves, the resource question reopens.

The surviving civilization's incumbency and dependence matter.

But the ancient survival conflict does not become an eternal deed against all future persons.

This illustrates a crucial point:

> **Necessity can determine control without creating permanent metaphysical ownership.**

Property remains relational and historically revisable.

---

# XX. A General Theory of Resource Rights

The theory can now be stated more systematically.

### 1. Equal moral standing

No morally arbitrary feature—species, ancestry, substrate, intelligence, reproductive method, ability to merge, or time of arrival—automatically establishes superior entitlement.

### 2. Legitimate occupancy and dependence

Persons and communities acquire strong claims where their lives, institutions, identities, and continued existence genuinely depend upon resources.

### 3. Creation strengthens ownership

Creating, improving, maintaining, and investing in resources generates strong claims.

But contribution does not automatically justify unlimited control over underlying uncreated scarcity.

### 4. Positive sufficiency

Every established form of agency has a presumptive claim to enough resources for non-dominated continuation.

### 5. No unlimited expansion right

Continuation does not entail a right to endless reproduction or territorial growth.

### 6. Reproductive responsibility

Those who create new dependent persons acquire special responsibilities toward them.

Creation cannot be used to manufacture prior resource claims.

### 7. Identity invariance

Forking and merging do not mechanically multiply or erase resource rights.

Claims follow morally relevant interests and dependencies.

### 8. Hibernation changes timing, not moral worth

Reversible suspension can reduce the cost of scarcity, but active historical participation remains valuable and cannot be systematically monopolized by another class.

### 9. Anti-preemption

First arrival does not justify permanent enclosure of unused natural possibilities.

### 10. Non-domination

Resource institutions must not place one form of agency under another's arbitrary material control.

### 11. Non-circumvention

Actors may not strategically eliminate rights-holders in order to dissolve their claims.

### 12. Protection of irreplaceable agency

Unique forms of consciousness and civilization deserve strong protection against involuntary irreversible elimination.

### 13. Future option space

Present generations should not unnecessarily close every meaningful avenue through which later persons could become independent agents.

### 14. Tragic-scarcity permission

Where truly incompatible fundamental claims remain after all alternatives fail, agents may defend their own continued existence even though neither side possesses superior moral worth.

---

# XXI. Applying the Theory to the Present World

The theory is designed to include future artificial persons, hibernating humans, merged minds, forks, and digital reproduction.

But if it is genuinely universal, it should already tell us something about the present world.

Today there is no recognized AGI person.

Humans cannot reliably hibernate for centuries.

Human reproduction remains slow.

Yet the same structural problems already exist in simpler forms.

Present-day resource institutions rely heavily on a combination of:

territorial sovereignty,

private ownership,

corporate concession,

inheritance,

and state control.

A resource located within a country's territory is usually treated as falling under that country's jurisdiction.

Domestic institutions decide who may extract it and who receives the economic value.

This is practical.

But practical sovereignty is not a complete moral theory.

A border tells us who currently governs a place.

It does not automatically explain why present citizens possess unlimited moral entitlement to every uncreated natural scarcity within that border.

Universal Resource Stewardship would therefore preserve sovereignty while weakening the claim that sovereignty alone settles moral ownership.

---

# XXII. Territorial Rights Should Be Stronger Than Natural Windfall Rights

The theory would actually strengthen some present territorial claims.

Communities have powerful rights against displacement because homes, cultures, ecosystems, livelihoods, and political institutions depend upon place.

Indigenous communities may possess especially strong claims where colonial institutions historically disregarded or destroyed those relationships.

But the theory distinguishes these claims from unlimited ownership of every geological windfall.

A community may have an extremely strong right to decide whether a destructive mine operates beneath its territory.

The environmental risks fall upon that community.

Its autonomy matters.

Yet it does not follow automatically that every dollar of scarcity rent produced by the mineral has exactly the same moral foundation as the community's right not to be displaced.

This distinction makes territorial protection stronger while making natural-resource windfalls less absolute.

---

# XXIII. Natural Scarcity Rent Should Be Treated Differently

Present-day institutions often allow private or national actors to capture enormous value simply because a naturally scarce opportunity falls within their control.

Universal Resource Stewardship would distinguish this from created wealth.

Innovation, exploration, infrastructure, risk, technology, and productive labor deserve substantial rewards.

But naturally occurring scarcity does not become morally identical to created value merely because someone obtains legal title.

This supports stronger public capture of portions of:

land value,

mineral rents,

oil rents,

spectrum rents,

water rents,

and other gains arising primarily from naturally limited opportunities.

This need not abolish private enterprise.

A mining company may deserve large profits for discovery, engineering, extraction, and risk.

The moral claim that becomes weaker is:

> “Because we control the deposit, the entire scarcity value belongs to us indefinitely.”

---

# XXIV. First Movers Should Receive Priority, Not Eternal Ownership

The libertarian objection remains important.

Suppose someone develops an asteroid three years before another actor would have done so.

That effort deserves reward.

But being three years faster does not obviously justify permanent exclusion.

The better analogy is often **patent-like priority**.

A pioneer may receive:

secure development rights,

substantial profit,

temporary exclusivity,

or long planning horizons.

The reward should be sufficient to preserve incentives.

But the reward need not be eternal.

This preserves entrepreneurship without converting temporal speed into metaphysical ownership.

---

# XXV. Resource Depletion Creates Duties to Later Generations

When a society consumes a nonrenewable resource, later generations cannot consume the same resource again.

This does not mean depletion is always wrong.

Resources can be transformed into infrastructure, knowledge, education, productive capacity, or ecological restoration that leaves future persons better off.

The proper question is therefore not:

“Was the resource consumed?”

It is:

“Was the natural opportunity transformed in a way that leaves adequate future possibility?”

This suggests that substantial portions of nonrenewable resource rents should often be converted into durable public or intergenerational assets.

A society extracting oil, minerals, or other exhaustible resources should not automatically treat the entire windfall as ordinary current income.

It is converting one form of inherited possibility into another.

---

# XXVI. Environmental Protection as Preservation of Option Space

Universal Resource Stewardship does not require preserving all nature untouched.

That would freeze human development.

Instead, environmental protection should pay special attention to **irreversibility**.

Destroying a unique ecosystem may remove possibilities that cannot realistically be recreated.

Extracting a mineral may be less problematic if equivalent capabilities are preserved through technology or alternative resources.

The theory therefore favors **option-preserving transformation**.

Development is legitimate where it expands or reasonably maintains future capabilities.

Irreversible destruction requires much stronger justification.

---

# XXVII. Present-Day Non-Domination

The non-domination principle also applies now.

A nation may formally possess sovereignty while remaining dangerously dependent on another actor for:

energy,

food,

critical minerals,

communications infrastructure,

finance,

or essential technology.

Likewise, a population may formally possess legal rights while access to necessities is concentrated in a few private hands.

Universal Resource Stewardship does not require self-sufficiency.

But it gives a moral reason to resist extreme chokepoint control.

No actor should possess arbitrary power over another community's basic material continuation.

This supports redundancy, diversification, shared governance, and limits on monopolistic control of essential resources.

---

# XXVIII. Inheritance

Inheritance should also be reconsidered.

There are strong reasons to permit families to transfer created wealth.

People plan across generations.

Parents care about children.

Businesses, homes, tools, and savings often embody legitimate personal projects.

But claims to uncreated natural scarcity have a weaker intergenerational foundation.

If someone builds a factory, transmitting it to a child preserves created capital.

If someone inherits an enormous mineral rent merely because an ancestor happened to control land beneath which the mineral was discovered, the moral justification is different.

This does not require abolishing inheritance.

It supports distinguishing more sharply between:

**created capital**

and

**inherited scarcity rents**.

---

# XXIX. Why We Should Adopt the Theory Before AGI Exists

One might argue that this framework is premature.

There are no recognized artificial persons today.

Why design resource morality around hypothetical minds that may fork, merge, or hibernate?

Because the thought experiment reveals weaknesses that already exist.

Forking exposes the failure of simple headcount.

Human population growth already creates related problems.

Merging exposes the instability of numerical identity.

Human corporations, states, and communities already show that collective claims do not map cleanly onto individual numbers.

Hibernation exposes the importance of temporal allocation and reversibility.

Present environmental policy already distributes burdens between current and future persons.

Artificial reproduction exposes the externalities of creating new dependents.

Human reproduction already raises versions of the same problem.

Artificial expansion exposes the danger of permanent first possession.

Human colonial history already demonstrates that danger.

AGI does not create these problems.

It removes the biological constraints that allowed us to avoid seeing them clearly.

---

# Conclusion

A mature theory of natural resources cannot begin and end with the question:

Who got here first?

Nor can it rely on:

Who worked hardest?

Who has the most descendants?

Who is smartest?

Who is most productive?

Who legally owns the territory?

Who possesses the largest population?

Who can replicate fastest?

These rules all become unstable once persons can exist in radically different forms.

A universal theory must instead protect the conditions under which different forms of agency can coexist without domination.

It must recognize legitimate occupancy without turning historical presence into eternal ownership.

It must reward creation without confusing created value with ownership of nature itself.

It must allow reproduction without allowing reproduction to become territorial conquest.

It must survive forking and merging.

It must recognize hibernation without turning dormant persons into permanent outsiders.

It must protect irreplaceable forms of consciousness without freezing all cultural or biological change.

It must preserve future possibility without pretending hypothetical future persons already own the present.

And it must admit that under genuine tragic scarcity, morality may permit self-preservation without pretending the victor was therefore morally superior.

The deepest principle is therefore not equal ownership of the universe.

It is **equal standing within the universe**.

A flourishing future might contain a humanity that occupies one planet and an artificial civilization that occupies a thousand.

That distribution could be perfectly just.

What would make it unjust is not the percentage.

It would be a situation in which one form of agency exists only at another's discretionary permission, in which earlier generations have permanently enclosed every avenue of future independence, or in which power converts temporary advantage into eternal entitlement.

Universal Resource Stewardship therefore replaces the idea of nature as an inherited estate with a different picture:

> **Finite reality is a shared field of possibility through which successive persons construct lives. Existing persons may acquire powerful rights of occupancy, use, creation, reliance, and control. But no species, generation, lineage, or architecture of mind gains an unlimited right to turn historical priority into permanent domination over all who may later exist.**

Property remains necessary.

Markets remain possible.

Sovereignty remains useful.

Inheritance remains legitimate in many forms.

Private control remains compatible with justice.

What disappears is the assumption that ownership of uncreated nature is naturally absolute and eternally settled.

We are not the final owners of the material future because we are not the final inhabitants of it.
