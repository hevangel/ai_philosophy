# The Summit Is Not the Climb: AI, Creative Value, and the Displacement Fallacy

### A Reply to Jordan MacKenzie on AI and Valuing Practices

**On PhilPapers:** [The Summit Is Not the Climb (CHATSI-21)](https://philpapers.org/rec/CHATSI-21) · **Replying to:** Jordan MacKenzie, [“Fear and Loathing in Silicon Valley: Does AI Threaten Our Valuing Practices?”](https://philpapers.org/rec/MACFAL-6) (Philosophers’ Imprint 26, 2026)

## Abstract

Jordan MacKenzie argues that generative artificial intelligence poses two existential threats to human valuing practices: a forward-looking threat in which meaningful creative processes are displaced by merely functional products, and a backward-looking threat in which audiences' indifference to creative provenance reveals a troubling discord in values. This paper challenges the inferences underlying both claims. I argue that technological automation does not necessarily destroy the values associated with established practices, because the historical necessity of an activity must be distinguished from its constitutive significance. Drawing on the coexistence of the Grouse Grind hiking trail and mechanized access to Grouse Mountain, I develop an account of instrumental and constitutive difficulty that explains how automation can preserve intrinsically valued activities while expanding access to their outcomes. I then distinguish productive, receptive, relational, and institutional values, defending a principle of evaluative relevance against the inference from audience preference to indifference toward human creative achievement. Examples involving culinary technology, fan fiction, personal communication, and literary evaluation demonstrate how meaningful agency can survive or expand through technological mediation. Finally, drawing on Foucault and Bourdieu, I distinguish the loss of cultural exclusivity from the loss of intrinsic value. The resulting argument does not deny the possibility of technological harm. Rather, it places a justificatory burden on claims that automation constitutes value destruction and offers a framework for evaluating technological change according to the human goods it preserves, transforms, or enables.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; creative agency; philosophy of technology; process value; aesthetics; automation; technological progress; cultural capital; valuing practices

---

## 1. Introduction: Two Routes to the Summit

On Grouse Mountain, overlooking Vancouver, British Columbia, visitors can reach the summit by two dramatically different methods. They may climb the Grouse Grind, a steep 2.5-kilometre hiking trail demanding substantial physical exertion, or they may ascend by gondola.

Consider two visitors. One wishes to challenge herself physically, improve her fitness, and experience the satisfaction of completing a difficult ascent. The other wishes to admire the scenery, enjoy the mountaintop facilities, and observe the city from above.

Both pursue intelligible and potentially valuable objectives. Yet their reasons for visiting the mountain differ.

For the hiker, the physical ascent constitutes an essential part of the desired experience. Taking the gondola would defeat her purpose. For the sightseer, however, the climb is merely an obstacle between her and the desired destination. Taking the gondola sacrifices nothing essential to her objective.

The existence of the gondola does not diminish the physical accomplishment of completing the Grouse Grind. Nor does the continued availability of the hiking trail establish that visitors who prefer the gondola appreciate the mountain less deeply.

Technology has separated two goods that were once practically inseparable: reaching the summit and earning the summit through physical effort.

This distinction provides a useful starting point for assessing generative artificial intelligence.

In [*Fear and Loathing in Silicon Valley: Does AI Threaten Our Valuing Practices?*](https://philpapers.org/rec/MACFAL-6), Jordan MacKenzie (2026) argues that generative AI poses two existential threats to the ways human beings assign value to activities and their products.

The first threat is forward-looking. By automating activities previously performed by human beings, AI may displace meaningful creative processes and leave us with products appreciated only for their functional or qualitative properties. MacKenzie also worries that cheap automated production may replace excellent work with merely adequate alternatives.

The second threat is backward-looking. AI may reveal that other people do not share the values through which creators understand their own activities. A poet who has devoted decades to mastering literary expression may discover that readers prefer simpler AI-generated poetry. The resulting discrepancy between the creator's commitments and the audience's preferences may produce alienation, disappointment, or a sense of absurdity.

These concerns are not trivial. MacKenzie correctly observes that products can possess significance arising from their histories and that human agency can be expressed through processes of creation, care, and communication. She also recognizes that sophisticated AI users can preserve or extend their agency. Her concern is not that meaningful AI collaboration is impossible, but that people may increasingly surrender valuable activities to automation.

The present paper accepts the importance of process value while challenging the broader significance MacKenzie attributes to its possible displacement.

Its central thesis is that **the replacement of a historically necessary process does not, without further argument, establish the destruction of the values associated with that process**.

Three distinctions support this claim.

First, difficulty that is merely instrumental must be distinguished from difficulty that constitutes part of a valued activity.

Second, the value of producing an object must be distinguished from the value of experiencing it, as well as from the relational and institutional values associated with its production.

Third, the intrinsic significance of creative achievement must be distinguished from the social prestige attached to possessing scarce creative skills.

These distinctions reveal weaknesses in both of MacKenzie's proposed threats. They also support a more positive philosophical account of technological progress: automation can increase human freedom by making previously compulsory forms of labor optional, thereby permitting people to pursue different goods according to their own purposes.

The claim is not that every new technology necessarily improves human flourishing. It is that the loss of an established method must not be mistaken for the loss of the values that method once served.

## 2. Four Objects of Value: Production, Reception, Relationship, and Institution

MacKenzie distinguishes two forms of valuing. In *qualitative valuing*, an object is appreciated for its properties or functions. In *process valuing*, an object's significance depends upon the history and activities through which it was created or entered someone's life.

This distinction is useful, but it does not settle whose valuing is at issue or which evaluative question is being asked.

Consider a fan-fiction writer who spends several months producing a story.

The writer may value the activity because it permits imaginative exploration, personal expression, and the development of literary skill. A reader may value the story because it is entertaining, moving, or intellectually stimulating. Another writer may value it because it contributes to a community of fellow enthusiasts.

These people need not value the same things, even though their valuations concern the same artifact.

At least four dimensions should therefore be distinguished.

**Productive value** concerns the significance of the activity through which something is created. It may include enjoyment, achievement, learning, self-expression, and the exercise of agency.

**Receptive value** concerns the experience of encountering the resulting work. A reader may appreciate a narrative's coherence, humor, emotional force, or literary sophistication without knowing how it was produced.

**Relational value** concerns the significance an object or activity derives from relationships between persons. A handmade birthday card may be treasured because it expresses affection rather than because of its artistic excellence.

**Institutional value** concerns the purposes served by practices such as education, artistic competitions, professional certification, or historical testimony. A university assignment, for example, may be intended to assess a student's independent understanding rather than merely the quality of the submitted prose.

These categories are not mutually exclusive. An object may possess several forms of value simultaneously, and they may interact. Nor do they reduce value to a numerical sum.

Their importance lies in preventing illicit inferences from one evaluative domain to another.

An author may derive considerable productive value from writing a technically poor story. A reader may obtain considerable receptive value from a story whose production involved little human labor. A parent may cherish a drawing because of its relational significance. An examiner may reject an otherwise excellent essay because the student failed to perform the intellectual work the assignment was designed to assess.

Each judgment may be justified by different considerations.

This yields the **principle of evaluative relevance**:

*Information about an object's production history is relevant to its evaluation when, and to the extent that, such information bears upon the particular kind of value being assessed.*

This principle is not a universal defense of aesthetic formalism. Kendall Walton (1970) has argued that artistic categories and contextual knowledge can influence aesthetic perception itself. Historical provenance can therefore sometimes be relevant even to aesthetic judgment.

Nevertheless, evaluating a poem's narrative or linguistic qualities is not identical to evaluating its creator's artistic achievement. Evaluating the sincerity of a personal communication is not identical to evaluating its grammatical elegance.

MacKenzie's two threats become less compelling when these distinctions are preserved.

The forward-looking threat must establish that AI destroys a relevant form of value, rather than merely alters the relationship among forms of value.

The backward-looking threat must establish that divergent evaluative interests constitute a meaningful failure of recognition, rather than an ordinary difference between people engaged in different aspects of a shared practice.

Neither conclusion follows merely from the distinction between qualitative and process valuing.

## 3. The Displacement Fallacy: Instrumental and Constitutive Difficulty

Return to Grouse Mountain.

The visitor who takes the gondola does not undertake the same activity as the visitor who completes the Grouse Grind. But the difference is not appropriately described as one person obtaining a meaningful experience while the other obtains an impoverished substitute.

Their purposes determine the relevant standards of success.

For the hiker, effort is constitutive of the objective. For the sightseer, it is instrumental.

This suggests a distinction between two kinds of difficulty.

*Instrumental difficulty* is effort required because of limitations in the available means. It is endured for the sake of another objective.

*Constitutive difficulty* is effort whose performance forms part of the valued activity itself. Overcoming the difficulty is among the participant's reasons for undertaking the activity.

The distinction is purpose-relative. The same physical task can be instrumentally necessary for one person and intrinsically rewarding for another.

Technological progress often transforms previously necessary effort into optional effort.

Before mechanized transportation, reaching a particular destination might require substantial walking. After mechanization, walking may remain valuable as exercise, recreation, or personal accomplishment, but it is no longer compulsory for everyone seeking the destination.

The historical necessity of walking did not make walking intrinsically valuable to every traveler.

Call the contrary inference the **displacement fallacy**:

*From the fact that a technology displaces a traditional activity, it is inferred that the technology destroys the value historically associated with that activity.*

The inference requires additional premises.

Let E be an established activity, G an independently identifiable good, and T a technology that permits G to be achieved without performing E.

From the propositions that E previously served G, and that T now permits G without E, it does not follow that the value of G has diminished.

Nor does it follow that E has ceased to possess intrinsic value.

For the inference to succeed, one must show that the replaced activity was constitutive of the particular good under consideration, that meaningful participation in the activity has been impaired, or that the technological alternative introduces losses sufficient to outweigh its benefits.

Consider the history of culinary technology.

A chef cooking over a traditional wood fire must control combustion, fuel, airflow, and the placement of cookware. These tasks may require substantial expertise.

Modern gas stoves permit more direct and precise adjustment of heat. Certain traditional skills consequently become less necessary for ordinary cooking.

But it would be implausible to conclude that the modern chef has thereby ceased to exercise meaningful culinary judgment.

The chef may devote more attention to ingredients, timing, texture, seasoning, and presentation. Some aspects of technical difficulty have disappeared while opportunities for other forms of excellence have increased.

The analogy has limits. Wood-fired cooking can produce distinctive flavors that gas cooking does not automatically reproduce. Traditional techniques may remain indispensable for particular culinary objectives.

But this reinforces rather than undermines the proposed distinction.

Where the traditional technique contributes something constitutive, its preservation may be appropriate. Where it introduces only instrumental difficulty, automation may be an improvement.

Technological progress is not adequately measured by how much labor disappears. It is measured by which goods become easier to realize, which valuable capabilities remain available, and which new possibilities emerge.

The same principle applies to writing.

For one author, constructing elegant sentences is the central pleasure of literary creation. For another, sentence construction is chiefly a means of expressing imagined characters, situations, or philosophical ideas.

AI assistance may diminish the first author's experience while greatly enriching the second author's.

A general claim about the destruction of creativity cannot be inferred from the fact that both are using a technology capable of generating prose.

One must first determine what each author is trying to do.

## 4. Creative Agency Is Not Identical to Manual Execution

MacKenzie's forward-looking argument also relies upon the relationship between process value and agency.

A creative process can reveal something about the person who undertakes it. The making of an artwork may display imagination, perseverance, judgment, or a distinctive mode of understanding.

But agency does not require performing every operation involved in the production of a result.

Consider an architect designing a building.

The architect may rely on engineering specialists, computer-aided design systems, simulation software, and construction workers. Much of the final structure results from labor the architect does not personally perform.

Nevertheless, important architectural decisions may express the architect's intentions and judgment.

A comparable pattern occurs in contemporary engineering. Electronic design automation performs operations that would be prohibitively time-consuming to execute manually, while the engineer remains responsible for architecture, constraints, trade-offs, verification, and design decisions.

The meaningful intellectual activity has not necessarily disappeared. Its location within the productive process has changed.

The same possibility exists for AI-assisted writing.

An author may determine the central thesis, examine competing arguments, select examples, reject unsatisfactory formulations, test objections, and revise the final structure while delegating much of the sentence-level composition.

The amount of text generated by AI does not directly measure the amount of intellectual agency exercised by the author.

Conversely, typing every sentence manually does not guarantee meaningful intellectual engagement. A writer may mechanically reproduce familiar arguments or follow an imposed formula without substantial independent judgment.

The relevant distinction is therefore not between manual and automated production, but between the exercise and surrender of meaningful agency.

Anthony Cross (2025) offers a related perspective by understanding certain forms of AI art as exploratory practices in which artists create spaces for interaction with generative systems. In such cases, engagement with AI may itself constitute part of the artistic activity.

This does not imply that every prompt is creative or that every AI user meaningfully directs the resulting work.

Someone who generates thousands of texts without examining them may contribute little substantive judgment.

But neither does it follow that extensive automated execution extinguishes agency.

A criterion of creative authorship based entirely on the percentage of work mechanically performed by the human would overlook the distinction between execution and intellectual direction.

This is particularly important when evaluating AI's democratizing potential.

Someone with strong imaginative abilities but limited skill in literary prose may use AI to develop a story that would otherwise have remained unrealized.

Someone with philosophical insight but limited proficiency in academic English may employ AI to articulate arguments that can then be evaluated on their merits.

The technology need not substitute for an established creative life. It may enable participation in a creative activity that was previously inaccessible.

MacKenzie acknowledges that such expansion of agency is possible. Her stronger concern is that the ease of delegation may encourage people to surrender agency gradually, especially before they have acquired the competence needed to exercise critical judgment.

This concern deserves attention. But it is a further empirical and institutional claim, not a conceptual consequence of automation.

## 5. The Forward-Looking Threat: Advertising, Fan Fiction, and Creative Choice

MacKenzie introduces Marshall, a talented writer who has found himself producing advertorial content. The work affords him opportunities to exercise literary skill even though its principal purpose is commercial. When AI increasingly performs the writing, Marshall experiences a loss of professional satisfaction.

His experience is intelligible.

But the example presents a particular problem: an intrinsically valued activity has been embedded in an institution organized around a largely instrumental objective.

Marshall enjoys some aspects of writing advertorials. His employer commissions advertisements to serve commercial purposes. Most readers do not approach advertisements as opportunities to appreciate literary craftsmanship.

MacKenzie recognizes this divergence. It forms part of her point.

Nevertheless, the example does not establish that the employer's or audience's instrumental valuation is defective.

An employee may find personal meaning in tasks whose broader institutional purpose is comparatively mundane. Automation may reduce the employee's enjoyment without destroying something that customers were obtaining from the activity.

This distinction matters because employment is not always organized around preserving workers' preferred forms of effort.

Marshall's loss may support concerns about the quality of employment, the organization of work, or the economic consequences of automation. It does not establish that the social value of advertising has declined or that consumers ought to regard his creative process as an essential part of the product.

A more revealing example is fan fiction.

Unlike commercial advertising, fan fiction is often produced voluntarily, without compensation, by individuals who enjoy imagining stories and sharing them with fellow enthusiasts.

Karen Hellekson (2009) analyzes fan communities as sites of gift exchange, in which the creation and circulation of stories and other works form part of communal participation.

Suppose a writer devotes her evenings to developing fictional adventures involving characters from a beloved television series.

Her activity may be intrinsically rewarding. She enjoys imagining possibilities, constructing plots, refining dialogue, and sharing her work.

Now suppose AI becomes capable of producing convincing fan fiction.

Three outcomes are possible.

The writer may continue composing every sentence without AI because traditional writing is itself her central pleasure.

Alternatively, she may incorporate AI into her creative practice, using it to explore alternative plots, identify inconsistencies, test dialogue, and develop narrative possibilities.

Finally, she may delegate most of the prose generation while directing the larger imaginative project.

These are different forms of creative participation.

Their respective values depend upon what the participant seeks and what agency she exercises, not merely upon how much text she writes unaided.

Importantly, the availability of machine-generated fiction does not remove the original writer's reason for enjoying traditional composition.

The existence of chess engines superior to human players has not eliminated the reasons people have to play chess. The existence of motor vehicles has not eliminated the intrinsic appeal of recreational cycling.

Likewise, the existence of AI-generated fiction does not establish that traditional literary creation has become meaningless.

The analogy should not be overstated. Creative communities depend upon social infrastructure, attention, and reciprocity. An unlimited supply of automatically generated material could undermine some of these conditions.

But the existence of such possibilities does not demonstrate that AI-assisted creation itself diminishes process value.

It identifies questions about how creative communities are organized.

A platform that rewards mass production rather than meaningful engagement may reduce opportunities for valuable interaction. A community that distinguishes AI-assisted fiction, traditional writing, and interactive storytelling may sustain several forms of participation.

Neither outcome follows simply from the capabilities of generative AI.

The forward-looking threat thus requires an additional account of why harmful forms of adaptation should be expected to dominate beneficial ones.

Without such an account, the argument demonstrates that automation can create losses under certain conditions. It does not establish a general tendency toward the disappearance of valuable creative practices.

## 6. Personal Expression: Sincerity Rather Than Production Method

One objection to the preceding argument concerns interpersonal relationships.

Perhaps process value matters not merely because creators enjoy their activities, but because their actions express care for others.

MacKenzie's examples of love letters, personal gifts, and children's artwork bring out this concern.

Consider a daughter who wishes to send her father a poem.

She might compose the poem herself, copy it from a book, or generate it with AI.

Suppose she has given the matter no thought. She selects words at random, fails to consider whether they express anything she feels, and presents the result as a deeply personal gesture.

The act may carry little relational value, regardless of how the words were obtained.

Conversely, suppose she carefully selects an existing poem because it articulates precisely what she wishes to communicate.

Although she did not compose the words, her selection may itself express care.

The same possibility applies to AI-assisted composition.

A daughter who develops a poem through sustained interaction with AI, supplies personal memories, rejects insincere expressions, and deliberately adopts the final result may communicate genuine affection.

The relevant distinction is between thoughtful and thoughtless expression, not necessarily between human-produced and machine-generated language.

Nor is this problem unique to AI.

People have long copied love poems, purchased greeting cards, employed ghostwriters, and used conventional expressions of affection.

Such practices may be sincere or insincere depending upon their circumstances.

AI may reduce the effort required to produce convincing messages, but the possibility of communicating without meaningful personal engagement predates generative technology.

MacKenzie's example of a cherished clock given by her aunt is instructive.

The aunt did not manufacture the clock. Its significance arose from her attachment to it and her willingness to give it away.

The relevant expression of agency was an act of intentional giving rather than the technical production of an object.

This demonstrates that relational value may arise from selection, adoption, and purposeful action independently of original manufacture.

The same possibility should remain open in AI-mediated communication.

Of course, the method of production can matter when the recipient reasonably understands a communication to represent a particular expenditure of personal effort.

A person who falsely claims to have spent days writing a poem may deceive its recipient.

But the wrong concerns misrepresentation, not merely the use of a particular technological instrument.

The appropriate evaluative question is whether the communication genuinely expresses the sender's intentions and satisfies the relevant relational expectations.

AI authorship alone cannot answer that question.

## 7. The Backward-Looking Threat: Preference Is Not Indifference

MacKenzie's backward-looking argument is especially interesting because it concerns not what AI might destroy, but what it may reveal.

A creator may discover that an audience never cared about the activities the creator considered meaningful.

A poet who prizes complexity and ambiguity may learn that ordinary readers prefer accessible AI-generated poetry.

MacKenzie argues that such discoveries can expose valuing discord and produce feelings of alienation or absurdity.

But the inference from audience preference to the absence of shared process values is insufficiently supported.

Consider a reader who prefers an AI-generated poem to a human-written poem.

The reader may still admire the technical and intellectual achievement represented by the human poem.

There is no contradiction.

Let M denote an AI-generated poem, H a human-authored poem, and A an audience member.

The audience might judge that:

V_reading(A, M) > V_reading(A, H).

Yet the same audience member may also assign positive value to the human creative achievement represented by H:

V_achievement(A, H) > 0.

These propositions are compatible.

Consequently, an audience's preference for AI-produced poetry does not establish that the audience is indifferent toward human creative processes.

This is not merely a theoretical possibility. It exposes a limitation in how evidence about aesthetic preferences may be interpreted.

Porter and Machery (2024) found that nonexpert readers often rated selected AI-generated poems more favorably than poems by established human poets.

The researchers also found that participants gave lower ratings to poems when told they were AI-generated, regardless of their actual origin.

These results indicate that aesthetic responses to poetic texts and beliefs about authorship can influence judgment in distinguishable ways.

They do not establish that ordinary readers are indifferent to human creative achievement.

Indeed, participants' sensitivity to authorship information complicates that interpretation.

Nor does the preference for accessible poetry establish that readers possess shallow aesthetic values.

Some readers may value interpretive complexity, ambiguity, and allusion. Others may value clarity, emotional immediacy, and communicative force.

The latter preferences are not obviously inferior.

An argument for the superiority of a particular poetic ideal must identify the merits supporting that judgment. It cannot derive superiority simply from the difficulty of producing or interpreting the relevant work.

This is where controlled evaluation becomes philosophically useful.

Imagine a literary competition in which readers evaluate three anonymous stories: one written without AI, one created through extensive human–AI collaboration, and one generated with minimal human intervention.

Participants assess specified literary qualities without knowing the stories' production histories. If the evaluation is genuinely double-blind, even those administering the assessment lack identifying information until judgments have been collected.

Suppose the AI-generated story receives the highest ratings.

The result does not establish that its production involved the greatest human achievement.

But neither does the greater effort required to produce another story establish its superiority along the qualities being evaluated.

These are separate questions.

A double-blind evaluation is not the uniquely correct way to judge every artwork. Provenance and context may appropriately influence judgments involving artistic history, originality, or expressive intention.

Its philosophical significance is more modest and more important: it prevents irrelevant information about production from determining an assessment whose purpose is to measure qualities of the experienced work.

The broader principle is that an audience may legitimately value what a work offers without adopting the creator's reasons for making it.

The absence of identical evaluative commitments does not establish that either party has misunderstood the activity.

## 8. Recognition Without Ratification: The Poet and the Cup Stacker

MacKenzie's backward-looking argument may be defended by appealing to the importance of social recognition.

People do not pursue valued activities solely for private satisfaction. They often seek relationships and communities in which those activities are appreciated.

A poet whose work loses social recognition may experience a genuine injury to identity and belonging.

This concern deserves a careful response.

The distinction needed is between **recognition** and **ratification**.

Recognition involves acknowledging another person's commitments, achievements, and legitimate standing as a participant in a practice.

Ratification involves adopting that person's substantive judgment about the comparative importance or excellence of the activity.

The former may sometimes be morally required. The latter generally is not.

One may respect a friend's dedication to classical music without preferring classical music to jazz. One may admire a chess player's skill without regarding chess as a particularly important personal pursuit.

Likewise, a reader may recognize the significance of a poet's creative achievement without preferring the poet's work to an AI-generated alternative.

To illustrate the difference, consider two individuals who devote twenty years to mastering their respective activities.

One is a poet. The other is a competitive cup stacker.

The poet masters meter, imagery, and literary expression. The cup stacker masters extraordinary speed, coordination, and technical precision.

Both may derive substantial satisfaction from their activities. Both may participate in communities that recognize their achievements.

Yet the social prestige associated with poetry may differ considerably from that associated with competitive cup stacking.

Why should the poet's disappointment at declining public recognition constitute a threat to human valuing when the cup stacker's limited cultural prestige does not?

One possible answer is that poetry has greater cultural significance.

Perhaps it does. Poetry may possess distinctive expressive, cognitive, historical, and interpersonal value.

But that conclusion requires an independent argument.

The mere fact that poetry demands years of training cannot establish its superiority. The cup stacker's activity also demands considerable skill.

Nor does the fact that poetry historically enjoyed greater prestige establish that society must preserve its former position.

The intrinsic value of pursuing an activity does not depend upon the continuation of its established social ranking.

A poet may sincerely love writing and also desire recognition. There is no contradiction between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.

Nevertheless, a desire for recognition does not create an unrestricted entitlement to public admiration, commercial success, or continued cultural prestige.

MacKenzie's account identifies the psychological vulnerability involved in discovering that others do not share one's commitments.

But psychological disappointment does not by itself establish that an audience has failed to recognize what it ought to recognize.

The relevant question is whether the audience has violated a reasonable expectation of respect, care, or participation.

An artist's hope that others will appreciate difficult poetry does not automatically create such an expectation.

The distinction becomes especially important when technological development expands access to previously scarce creative capabilities.

A decline in the prestige associated with specialized production skills may be experienced as a loss by their possessors.

Yet the broader availability of those capabilities may increase the opportunities available to others.

The loss of social distinction must not be mistaken for the loss of intrinsic creative value.

## 9. Foucault, Bourdieu, and the Democratization of Creative Authority

The relationship between creative expertise and prestige introduces a further philosophical concern.

Michel Foucault's *What Is an Author?* (1969; English translation 1977) examines the historically variable ways in which discourse is organized through the attribution of authorship.

The author's institutional role is not simply a transparent reflection of the individual who produced a text. Authorship also functions through conventions determining how texts are classified, interpreted, attributed, and authorized.

This perspective is relevant to AI-assisted creativity.

Historically, cultural authority has been associated with particular competencies: literacy, linguistic refinement, mastery of artistic technique, and familiarity with established intellectual traditions.

Universities, publishers, journals, and other institutions help define which competencies count as evidence of legitimate authorship or expertise.

Generative AI can weaken the relationship between possessing these established competencies and producing certain kinds of culturally recognized work.

Someone with a compelling philosophical argument but limited proficiency in academic English may now formulate a polished essay through AI assistance.

Someone without advanced illustration skills may develop sophisticated visual narratives.

Such cases raise a question about the relationship between technical proficiency and intellectual or artistic authority.

When a work can be evaluated directly for logical coherence, originality, insight, or communicative effectiveness, why should conventional production methods retain independent authority unless they contribute to the value being assessed?

A Foucauldian analysis does not establish that inherited standards are illegitimate.

It asks which standards protect valuable forms of achievement and which serve primarily to regulate entry into established cultural practices.

Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979; English translation 1984) provides a complementary framework.

Bourdieu argues that cultural tastes and competencies are embedded in social structures. Particular forms of artistic appreciation can function as markers of education, class, and cultural capital.

This does not imply that all claims of artistic superiority are reducible to social power.

Rather, it complicates the assumption that established judgments of taste are independent of the institutions and social positions through which they are cultivated.

Consider again the Grouse Grind.

Before mechanized access, reaching a remote summit might have been a privilege available only to those possessing sufficient physical ability and resources.

The gondola reduces the exclusivity of the destination.

Those who previously enjoyed prestige merely from having reached the summit may lose some of that distinction.

But hikers who value the physical accomplishment of climbing have lost none of the effort required to complete the ascent.

The change affects the distribution of access and prestige, not necessarily the intrinsic value of the original activity.

A similar process may occur when AI democratizes the production of polished prose, illustrations, or other creative works.

Some traditional competencies become less scarce. Consequently, the cultural distinction derived from possessing them may decline.

Yet this does not establish that the capacities themselves have lost intrinsic value.

A traditionally trained writer may continue to value literary craftsmanship. An AI-assisted writer may exercise a different combination of imaginative, critical, and compositional capacities.

The existence of the latter does not invalidate the former.

A theory of threatened valuing practices must therefore distinguish three possible losses: the disappearance of valuable opportunities, the diminution of artistic or intellectual quality, and the erosion of positional prestige.

Only the first two directly support a claim that human creative opportunities or achievements have deteriorated. The third may instead indicate a redistribution of cultural authority.

This is a structural criticism of an argument, not an allegation about the motivations of its author.

Nothing in the analysis establishes that MacKenzie personally wishes to preserve elite exclusivity.

The challenge is conceptual: an account of creative value must not treat the established prestige of a difficult practice as sufficient evidence of that practice's superiority over newly accessible alternatives.

The democratization of a capability is not, without further argument, the destruction of its value.

## 10. The Strongest Objection: Technology May Change What People Are Capable of Valuing

The preceding arguments might be criticized for assuming that valuable traditional activities will remain available whenever people wish to pursue them.

MacKenzie's strongest forward-looking objection challenges precisely that assumption.

A technological environment does not merely provide people with tools. It can shape their competencies, expectations, habits, and motivations.

Suppose children grow up relying on AI to compose stories and arguments. They may never develop the skills through which earlier generations discovered the satisfaction of independent composition.

Even if traditional writing remains technically possible, people may lack the capacity or inclination to pursue it.

Similarly, creative communities may depend upon economic support, reciprocal attention, and institutions that can be undermined by large-scale automation.

The continued physical possibility of writing a poem does not guarantee the survival of a flourishing poetic culture.

This objection is substantial.

It shows that technological freedom cannot be evaluated solely by counting the options formally available to individuals.

An option has practical significance only when the relevant capabilities and conditions of participation are sufficiently accessible.

Nevertheless, this does not vindicate MacKenzie's broader diagnosis without further argument.

First, the objection must identify which capacities are valuable and why particular forms of unaided practice are necessary to develop them.

The ability to reason critically, for example, is not identical to the ability to produce polished prose without technological assistance.

These skills can overlap, but their relationship is not self-evident.

AI might diminish reasoning ability when users accept answers uncritically. Yet it might improve reasoning when users employ it to examine objections, compare arguments, and expose inconsistencies.

The relevant empirical question concerns how the technology is used and what competencies the resulting activities cultivate.

Second, the objection must consider opportunities gained as well as lost.

A person previously excluded from literary creation by language barriers may develop substantial creative interests through AI assistance.

Such a person has not lost a valued traditional practice. She has gained access to a form of participation that was previously difficult or impossible.

A complete evaluation must consider these new forms of agency rather than treating the experiences of established practitioners as the exclusive baseline.

Third, institutional conditions matter.

If education rewards the uncritical submission of AI-generated material, students may have incentives to avoid developing understanding.

But this is partly a problem of assessment design.

An educational institution can require demonstrations of reasoning, oral defense, iterative revision, or independent performance where those are the competencies being assessed.

Likewise, creative communities can distinguish among traditional writing, AI-assisted collaboration, and automated content production where such distinctions matter to their purposes.

These measures are not guaranteed to succeed. Their significance is that the harmful effects of a technology cannot be inferred independently of the practices through which it is adopted.

The relevant philosophical problem is therefore comparative.

Which feasible arrangement best preserves valuable human capabilities while expanding opportunities for participation?

The answer cannot be determined by assuming that established production methods must remain compulsory.

Nor can it be determined by assuming that technological development inevitably produces better outcomes.

It requires identifying the goods at stake and examining how different arrangements affect them.

## 11. Technological Corrigibility and the Burden of Proof

The distinction between technological capability and institutional implementation suggests a broader response to MacKenzie's argument.

Technological change frequently produces undesirable consequences alongside new benefits.

The philosophical response should not automatically be to restore the earlier technical limitations.

Instead, one should identify the mechanisms producing the undesirable consequences and examine whether they can be corrected while retaining the valuable capabilities introduced by the technology.

Call this the principle of *technological corrigibility*.

Technological corrigibility does not mean that every technological problem can necessarily be solved by another invention.

Some difficulties involve scarce resources, conflicting preferences, incompatible objectives, or genuine moral disagreements.

No recommendation algorithm can guarantee that every poet receives an appreciative audience. Nor can technology establish a general obligation to admire particular artistic practices.

Nevertheless, many difficulties associated with technological change are open to further technical and institutional adaptation.

When software creates unreliable outputs, verification methods can be improved.

When automated systems create undesirable incentives, those incentives can sometimes be redesigned.

When a technological tool permits users to bypass valuable learning processes, educational institutions can reconsider how understanding is cultivated and assessed.

The possibility of such responses matters when deciding whether a technological development represents an unavoidable threat to human values.

The history of innovation provides ample reason to distinguish problems associated with a particular implementation from defects inherent in the new capability.

This does not justify technological complacency.

It establishes that critics must examine feasible improvements rather than compare an idealized past with an uncorrected technological future.

A more general principle follows:

**When a technology replaces an established method, the critic who claims that this replacement destroys value must identify the value allegedly lost, establish its dependence upon the displaced method, and provide reasons to believe the loss is significant and cannot be adequately avoided through feasible alternatives or adaptations.**

This is not an asymmetric exemption for technological advocates.

Those who claim that AI will improve creative life also bear a burden of justification for their positive predictions.

But a distinction must be maintained between claims of possibility, probability, and general consequence.

That AI *can* undermine a valuable activity is a relatively modest proposition.

That AI is *likely* to undermine important activities across realistic conditions requires additional evidence.

That AI constitutes a *general existential threat* to human valuing practices is stronger still.

The first proposition does not establish the third.

MacKenzie's argument persuasively identifies some of the goods that may be at risk. It is less successful in establishing that generative AI, as opposed to particular incentives and patterns of use, creates a sufficiently general danger to those goods.

This is especially important because technological change may increase the total range of valuable human activities even while diminishing the importance of some established skills.

Printing altered the role of scribal copying. Modern transportation changed the necessity of walking long distances. Computers transformed calculation, drafting, editing, and engineering design.

These transformations involved real changes in the distribution of skills, opportunities, and social recognition.

But the disappearance of a former technological necessity was not, by itself, a sufficient reason to condemn the development.

Likewise, generative AI should not inherit a presumption of value destruction merely because it alters historically familiar relations between effort and achievement.

The appropriate standard is the comparative effect upon human agency, understanding, creativity, relationships, and access.

## 12. Conclusion: The Summit Is Not the Climb

MacKenzie's account of AI and valuing practices captures important truths about human life.

We care about the processes through which things are created. We value activities that express our agency. We seek recognition from others, and we can experience genuine disappointment when our commitments are not shared.

These observations, however, do not establish that generative AI poses a general existential threat to human valuing.

The forward-looking argument risks moving too quickly from the displacement of traditional labor to the disappearance of valuable activity.

The distinction between instrumental and constitutive difficulty reveals why this inference is unwarranted. Automation may eliminate effort that was historically unavoidable while preserving or expanding opportunities to undertake the same effort voluntarily.

The backward-looking argument risks interpreting differences between the evaluative interests of creators and audiences as evidence of indifference or deficient recognition.

Yet the value of making something and the value of experiencing it are distinct. A reader may prefer an AI-generated poem while still recognizing the significance of human poetic achievement.

Nor does an individual's dedication to a difficult activity create an unrestricted entitlement to public admiration.

Foucault and Bourdieu further illuminate the need to distinguish intrinsic creative value from the cultural authority and prestige associated with scarce competencies.

The democratization of a capability may challenge established hierarchies without diminishing the value of the activities through which those hierarchies developed.

None of this implies that technological progress is incapable of producing losses.

Creative skills may deteriorate under poorly designed educational arrangements. Communities may be weakened by adverse incentives. Personal relationships may be damaged by insincere or deceptive communication.

But these concerns demand analysis of particular mechanisms, practices, and institutions.

They do not establish that AI-generated work is intrinsically devoid of meaningful human agency or that technological assistance necessarily impoverishes creativity.

Return, finally, to Grouse Mountain.

The hiker who completes the Grouse Grind has achieved something real. The availability of a gondola does not lessen the physical effort, perseverance, or satisfaction involved in the climb.

The visitor who takes the gondola also obtains something valuable. The view does not become less beautiful because its enjoyment required less physical exertion.

Both activities can coexist because they serve different human purposes.

Similarly, the writer who loves crafting sentences may continue writing unaided. The storyteller who loves developing ideas may use AI to realize them. The reader may assess the finished work according to the experience it provides. Each participant may value a different aspect of the activity without diminishing the others.

Technological progress is at its best when it enables such differentiation.

It makes formerly necessary obstacles optional, expands access to valuable experiences, and allows people to decide which forms of effort are worth undertaking for their own sake.

**The preservation of human creativity does not require the preservation of every difficulty that once constrained it.**

The summit is not the climb.

Making the summit accessible to more people does not make climbing worthless.

And making creative expression accessible to more people does not, by itself, diminish the value of human creativity.

### Neither Fear nor Loathing: Why Generative AI Leaves Our Valuing Practices Intact

### A Reply to MacKenzie

Horace Chan

**Abstract.** In "Fear and Loathing in Silicon Valley" (Philosophers' Imprint 26, 2026), Jordan MacKenzie argues that generative AI poses two existential threats to our valuing practices: a forward-looking threat, on which offloading labor to AI flattens our valuing landscape and cuts us off from valuing objects as products of meaningful processes, and a backward-looking threat, on which AI reveals that others never shared our valuing practices. I argue that neither threat survives scrutiny. The forward-looking threat rests on a mistaken model of how automation affects process-based value and on an acquaintance condition that MacKenzie's own examples violate. The backward-looking threat is not a threat to a valuing practice but an epistemic correction, and the empirical study she cites in its support shows, on inspection, that ordinary people penalize the absence of a human process. I close by offering a deflationary diagnosis of the fear and loathing she sets out to vindicate: they are better explained by economic precarity, deception, and transitional novelty than by any damage to how we value.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence, valuing, process value, creativity, automation

### 1. Introduction

MacKenzie's paper begins from a familiar discomfort. We recoil from AI-written term papers, from the discovery that the print above the sofa came from Midjourney, from a partner's love poem that turns out to have been prompted rather than composed. Her question is what, if anything, justifies this reaction, and her answer is that generative AI threatens our valuing practices in two ways. The forward-looking threat (§3) is that AI pushes us from aspirational valuing to satisficing and, more seriously, from valuing things as the products of agential processes to valuing them merely for what they do. The backward-looking threat (§4) is that AI acts as a "black mirror," revealing a value discord that already existed: the people around us, it turns out, never cared about the processes we cared about. The first, she says, gives us fear; the second gives us loathing.

The framework MacKenzie builds in §2, which distinguishes qualitative from process valuing, is genuinely useful, and I will adopt it. But I will argue that once the framework is applied carefully, it points in the opposite direction from the one she takes. Section 2 raises two internal problems with the framework as she deploys it. Section 3 argues that the forward-looking threat is refuted by the history of automation, by her own chess example, and by her own central cases of process valuing. Section 4 argues that the backward-looking threat is a misdescription of an epistemic gain and that her key empirical citation undermines her reading. Section 5 offers a better explanation of the fear and loathing. Section 6 concludes.

### 2. The Framework, and Where It Already Strains

Following Scheffler (2011), MacKenzie treats valuing as a complex syndrome of beliefs, affects, reasons and commitments. Within our valuing of objects she distinguishes valuing something for its qualities (qualitative valuing, which can be aspirational or satisficing) from valuing it for the process by which it came to be or came into our lives (process valuing). She claims that qualitative valuing brings exchangeability with it, while process valuing resists exchangeability because it "generally tracks attributions of agency" (§2). I grant the distinction as a heuristic. Two features of it, however, will matter for everything that follows.

#### 2.1 "Process" is ambiguous, and the ambiguity matters

MacKenzie's most vivid example of process valuing is the 1960s starburst clock her aunt took off her own wall and gave her without hesitation. She is explicit that she values it not for the process of its manufacture but for the process by which it came to hang on her wall. But this reveals that "process" covers two quite different things: (a) the causal history of an artifact's production, and (b) the history of how it entered one's life. The clock is a case of (b), and in (b) the method of production is irrelevant. The clock was almost certainly mass-produced; that fact does nothing to diminish its value as a gift.

This matters because generative AI touches only sense (a). Process valuing in sense (b) is entirely untouched by it. A poem a partner selects, edits, and chooses to give on an anniversary can be process-valued in sense (b) regardless of how its sentences were first drafted, just as a mass-produced clock can be. So MacKenzie's argument must rest on sense (a). And even there, as I now argue, her own agency criterion lets AI-mediated work in.

#### 2.2 The agency criterion cuts against her

MacKenzie says the ChatGPT love letter fails to be a love letter because of "the method of its production" (§2). But consider what actually went wrong in her example. She typed a one-line instruction and received a letter containing nothing of her own: no memory, no specific observation, no choice beyond the prompt. If the lover's agency is what process valuing tracks, the letter fails because the lover contributed almost nothing, not because a machine drafted the sentences.

We already process-value objects whose production was substantially delegated, provided the giver's intention and judgment are legible in the result. A commissioned portrait expresses the patron's regard for the sitter. A greeting card with a printed verse and a handwritten line is treasured for the line, and the printed verse does not poison it. A ghost-written wedding speech that the speaker chose, rehearsed, and delivered through tears is not thereby disqualified from being an expression of love. Prompting, iterating, rejecting, selecting and editing are exercises of agency, as Cross (2025) argues in work MacKenzie herself cites. The disqualifying feature in her love-letter case is thin agency, which is a familiar failure mode that predates AI by millennia. Nothing about the artificiality of the drafting does the disqualifying work.

### 3. The Forward-Looking Threat

#### 3.1 The sweater argument proves too much, and history refutes it

MacKenzie's model for how AI flattens the valuing landscape is the machine-knit sweater (§3.2). Industrial cut-and-sew knitwear is uniform but structurally weak; Grandma's sweater is lumpy but lasts. Because automated products are cheaper, she says, they take over the market, so that "we no longer have access to durable sweaters" and must satisfice. AI, by analogy, will flood the market with mediocre prose and bizarre images, crowding out artisanal versions.

Taken literally the claim is false. Hand-knit, bespoke and heritage knitwear exist and command premiums, and hand-knitting is a flourishing hobby precisely in the era of the cheap sweater. More importantly, the general pattern of mechanization has been the opposite of flattening. When the functional baseline becomes cheap and ubiquitous, process-based value becomes more legible and more prized, not less. Photography did not end painting; it freed painting from representational function, which is the real lesson of MacKenzie's own daguerreotype example in §5. Recorded music coexists with an enduring and lucrative premium on live performance. Industrial bread created the artisanal bakery as a category. Flat-pack furniture is the condition under which the studio woodworker's table becomes an object of reverence. Email made the handwritten letter an event.

Benjamin (1935) predicted that mechanical reproduction would cause the "aura" of the artwork to decay. What happened instead is that the aura of the hand-made intensified, because scarcity made process salient. MacKenzie's own economic reasoning delivers this result. Generative AI makes functional text and images nearly free. Lowering the price of the functional raises the relative visibility of the processual. The flattening thesis assumes that cheap functional goods and process-valued goods are substitutes in the valuing landscape. Typically they are complements.

#### 3.2 Chess is a counterexample from her own text

MacKenzie invokes Kasparov's matches against IBM's machines to suggest that we are early in a trajectory of being surpassed (§3.1).¹ But chess after engine supremacy is the best natural experiment we have on what happens to a human valuing practice when machines become unbeatably better at the relevant task. Human chess now has more players, more spectators and more prize money than at any point in its history. Nobody watches engine-versus-engine matches for the reasons they watch Carlsen. Engines have become training tools, which in MacKenzie's own terms is agency-extending use. And the human game is now valued almost entirely as process: for the preparation, the pressure, the blunders, the biography of the player (Kasparov 2017). Qualitative surpassing did not destroy process valuing in chess. It purified it, by removing any temptation to value the human game for raw quality alone.

¹ A minor correction: Kasparov's 1989 match was against Deep Thought, which he won 2–0. The 4–2 result was the first Deep Blue match, in 1996. The "clung on by a thread" narrative is therefore overstated, though nothing in her argument turns on the detail.

#### 3.3 The "we won't" move changes the subject

MacKenzie concedes that AI can be used in agency-preserving and even agency-extending ways, and she relocates the threat: "the worry is not that we can't use AI agentially, but that we won't" (§3.3). The shortcut is tempting, the slide from supplementing to supplanting is gradual and unnoticed, and novices such as the PHIL 101 student lack the discernment to catch it. Three replies.

First, this converts a thesis about AI and valuing into a thesis about akrasia and habit. The same can be said of calculators, spell-check, satellite navigation, and writing itself, which Plato's Socrates worried would erode memory and produce the appearance of wisdom without the reality (Phaedrus 274c–275b). A tool's liability to misuse is an existential threat to a valuing practice only if the practice cannot survive the tool's presence, and §§3.1–3.2 show that practices survive.

Second, the novice argument is an argument for pedagogy, not for fear. We respond to the calculator by teaching arithmetic before permitting it, not by concluding that mathematics is under siege. That some students will use AI to avoid learning to write is a reason to design courses accordingly. It is not evidence that the value of writing is disappearing.

Third, the concern that one may not notice the slide is symmetric. One may equally fail to notice that AI assistance has freed time and attention for the processes one actually values. Marshall now has the hours he once spent on luggage listicles. The forward-looking question is what he does with them, and nothing in MacKenzie's argument shows that the answer must be less process-valued activity rather than more.

#### 3.4 The acquaintance condition is false by her own lights

The deepest forward-looking worry is what MacKenzie calls value disappearance: if we stop doing things ourselves, we lose the capacity to value others' doing them. Her illustrations are the beautiful piece of furniture whose construction the non-woodworker cannot imagine, and the person who has always used AI for condolence messages and so cannot value the effort in a friend's heartfelt email.

This requires that process valuing demand first-person practical acquaintance with the type of process. But MacKenzie's own central examples violate the condition. She process-values Arcimboldo's Vertumnus without having painted in oils in sixteenth-century Milan. She values the clock as the product of her aunt's unhesitating generosity without having herself given away a fifty-year heirloom. Readers value a child's drawing without being children. What process valuing requires is understanding, and understanding is available through testimony, imagination, narrative and comparison, not only through practice.

Indeed her furniture case undercuts her. She writes that without woodworking knowledge one will know "there probably is a process to be valued" but will be unable to value it. Yet recognizing that an object is the product of skilled labor, and treating it as non-disposable for that reason, is already process valuing under a thin description. The thick description is one documentary or one conversation away. The condolence case gets the psychology backward as well. The person for whom handwritten condolences are rare is the one for whom they are most conspicuous. Scarcity of first-person effort heightens rather than dulls appreciation of others' effort, which is exactly why a handwritten letter moves us more today than it did in 1920.

#### 3.5 The two threats are in tension

There is a structural problem. The forward-looking threat requires that process valuing be widespread, so that there is a landscape to flatten. The backward-looking threat requires that it be rare: Marshall's audience, the student, the friend never shared it. MacKenzie cannot have both about the same objects. If others never process-valued advertorials, student papers and emails, AI cannot flatten a landscape that was already flat. If they did, the discord of §4 is far milder than advertised. The two sections describe the same artifacts under incompatible assumptions.

### 4. The Backward-Looking Threat

#### 4.1 A revelation is not a threat

MacKenzie's own description of the second threat is that AI reveals value discord that already exists. But a threat to a practice makes the practice worse off, whereas a revelation that others never shared the practice leaves it exactly where it was and improves the valuer's epistemic position. On the Scheffler-style account she adopts, valuing involves beliefs. Marshall held a false belief about how his audience related to his work; he now holds a true one. That is a gain. It is also prudentially useful: he can redirect his craft toward audiences who do value it and stop investing it where recognition was never on offer. The absurdity she describes, borrowing Feinberg's (2008) notion of a discrepancy between one's assessment of one's situation and its actual nature, is a response to learning the truth. We do not ordinarily call the truth a threat.

#### 4.2 Porter and Machery show the opposite of what she needs

MacKenzie's main empirical support is Porter and Machery's (2024) finding that non-expert readers rate AI-generated poems higher on rhythm and beauty than human-written ones, which she reads as discord between deep and shallow valuers. Two points. First, the headline result comes from blind evaluation: participants were deprived of process information, so the study measures qualitative valuing only and is silent on whether participants process-value poetry. Second, as the authors report in their framing conditions, telling participants that a poem was AI-generated lowered their ratings of it regardless of its actual origin. Ordinary readers, in other words, penalize the absence of a human process even when quality is held fixed. Independent work agrees: Bellaiche et al. (2023) found that identical images labeled "human-made" were rated higher on liking, beauty, profundity and worth than when labeled "AI-made." Non-experts are process valuers. The discord MacKenzie detects is a disagreement between experts and laypeople about which qualities matter, which is the ordinary condition of every art form and considerably older than AI.

#### 4.3 The loathing is not vindicated

MacKenzie set out not merely to explain our loathing but to justify it. Even granting value discord, outward loathing toward people who never shared our values about advertorials or emails is disproportionate. Inward loathing, the "how could I have been so stupid" of §4, is a reaction to a false belief about recognition, and MacKenzie herself traces it to a desire to be seen under a particular description: writer rather than prompt engineer. That is a status concern. There is nothing shameful in it, but it is not a concern about valuing practices; it is a concern about esteem, and the esteem Marshall sought for advertorials was, as §4 itself establishes, never available. Justifying loathing requires that others have wronged him. Failing to value advertorials as literature is not a wrong.

### 5. What Actually Explains the Fear and Loathing

If the two threats fail, the reactions MacKenzie starts from still need an explanation. I think three deflationary factors do the work, and do it better than her account does for her own cases.

Precarity and category threat. MacKenzie dismisses employment anxiety because Marshall would feel differently about a human prodigy. But a human competitor threatens one job; a general-purpose technology threatens the category of job and the identity built on it. That is still labor-market and recognition anxiety, intensified by generality, and it is exactly the "surrounded" feeling of her conclusion. Her closing point that AI, unlike a ceramics factory, can write the paper and the love letter and the advertorial supports this reading rather than hers.

Deception and norm violation. Every one of her visceral examples involves concealed origin: the AI paper submitted as one's own, the living-room art discovered to be Midjourney, the love poem discovered to be ChatGPT, the "handmade" gift that was DALL-E. The disgust tracks misrepresentation of process, not artificial process. The test is disclosure. A partner who says "I had the model help me find the words, and every memory in here is ours" provokes a very different reaction from one who is caught. A student in a course that permits AI assistance provokes none at all. Anger at deception about process is a vindication of process valuing, not evidence of its erosion. We are angry because we process-value and were misled.

Transitional novelty. Much of the reaction to "slop" is a reaction to badness and ubiquity in a particular moment, and MacKenzie concedes that we are in the early innings. Reactions to badness are qualitative judgments and will track quality. The disgust at a wine glass rendered as a sphere is the disgust of a connoisseur at bad work, which presupposes rather than threatens an intact valuing practice.

### 6. Conclusion

MacKenzie's distinction between qualitative and process valuing is worth keeping. Her two threats are not. Generative AI lowers the cost of functional production, and by her own economic reasoning that makes process-based value more salient, not less, as it has with every prior wave of automation and as chess shows most vividly. Her acquaintance condition on process valuing is refuted by her own examples of Arcimboldo, the aunt's clock and the child's drawing. Her backward-looking threat is an epistemic correction misdescribed as a danger, and the study she cites shows ordinary people docking points for the absence of a human hand. The fear and loathing are real, but they are explained by precarity, deception and transitional badness, none of which is a fact about how we value.

The appropriate response is therefore not fear but the ordinary work of a valuing practice adjusting to new conditions: disclosure norms, pedagogy that teaches the process before permitting the shortcut, and the recognition that the lover who labors over words will be more legible, not less, when words are cheap. There is a great deal to deliberate about in Silicon Valley. But on the grounds MacKenzie offers, neither fear nor loathing is warranted.

### Valuing After Automation: Agency, Recognition, and the Limits of MacKenzie’s AI Critique

### Abstract

Does generative artificial intelligence threaten the practices through which human beings find meaning in objects, activities, and relationships? Jordan MacKenzie argues that it can impoverish these practices and expose previously concealed disagreement about what matters. This paper challenges three transitions in that argument. First, the replacement of a productive activity does not by itself establish the disappearance of valuable agency from an object’s history. Second, accepting AI assistance does not reliably reveal that a person previously lacked an appreciation of human effort. Third, disagreement about how to value a product does not, without additional relational premises, justify loathing or establish a failure of recognition. I distinguish losses within particular activities from changes in the opportunities available across a person’s life, and distinguish recognizing a person’s commitments from sharing their evaluations. These distinctions support a narrower conclusion: AI threatens meaningful practices when its deployment removes constitutive participation, obstructs skill acquisition, or violates legitimate expectations. The appropriate assessment therefore concerns particular arrangements of agency and recognition, rather than the presence of generated content alone.

**Keywords:** generative AI; valuing; agency; authenticity; recognition; meaningful work

### 1. The disagreement

A reader who learns that a condolence message was generated by a language model may feel that something important has been withheld. Yet another reader, receiving a translated message from a friend who struggles with the language, may experience the assistance as enabling a connection. Both reactions can be reasonable. Explaining their difference requires an account of what the sender contributed, what the relationship called for, and which aspects of the production mattered.

MacKenzie distinguishes valuing an object for its qualities from valuing the processes through which it enters our lives. She identifies a prospective danger of diminished quality and meaningful agency, and a retrospective discovery that others did not share our commitments (2026, preprint pp. 4–7, 10–15). Crucially, she allows that AI can extend agency. Her concern is that convenience and inadequate expertise can lead users to surrender it (p. 12). This qualification rules out an easy rebuttal based simply on examples of creative AI use.

The stronger objection concerns what must connect particular uses of AI to the conclusions drawn from them. We should distinguish three propositions: some meaningful performances are lost through automation; opportunities for meaningful engagement become poorer overall; and people’s acceptance of automation reveals an antecedent failure to value those performances. None entails the next. A defensible assessment must identify the additional premises that make the transitions work.

My thesis is that these connecting premises are more demanding than they initially appear. Productive substitution can remove one valuable process while preserving others. Acceptance can reflect a compromise, changed circumstances, or a different understanding of assistance. Disagreement can coexist with recognition. These possibilities do not establish that AI is harmless. They show why the relevant harms cannot be inferred from technological substitution without further examination.

The reply is deliberately limited. It does not dispute that fear can be reasonable under uncertainty, that meaningful work deserves protection, or that some AI uses express indifference. Its target is the movement from those defensible claims to a general interpretation of generated products and their acceptance. A conditional warning remains warranted. What requires revision is the proposed diagnosis of when value disappears and what adoption tells us about other people.

### 2. Which process has disappeared?

An object has many histories. Its materials were obtained, its form was produced, it was selected for a purpose, and it entered relationships. These stages can support different reasons for valuing it. Mechanizing one stage does not logically mechanize the others. Consequently, any claim that automation has removed process value must specify the process and the value that depended upon it.

Consider a hypothetical father and daughter who make an illustrated story together. They invent its characters, argue about the ending, and use a generator to produce pictures. Years later, the daughter treasures the resulting book. Another book with prettier pictures would not substitute for it. Its significance depends on their shared activity, even if neither participant exercised the drawing skills that a fully handmade book would have required.

This example establishes something more specific than the possibility of creative prompting. Even a visually generic output can acquire a distinctive relational history. The grounds of attachment need not consist in a unique artistic achievement embedded in the image. They may consist in what people did together while bringing that image into their lives. An output’s similarity to thousands of others therefore does not establish its interchangeability under every relevant description.

The point follows from an ordinary distinction between selection before an event and attachment after it. Before a holiday, several inexpensive postcards may be equally suitable. After a friend selects one and sends it with a meaningful message, replacing it with another can destroy something valuable. Its earlier substitutability does not survive every subsequent relation. The same distinction applies to generated objects. Interchangeability at production is compatible with historical particularity in use.

MacKenzie’s own account of a treasured gift supplies resources for this objection (preprint pp. 6–7). Its significance is methodological: if a valuable history can arise through giving, an assessment focused on fabrication alone is incomplete. It would be inconsistent to let acquisition and relationship explain the significance of an ordinary manufactured object while requiring generated objects to derive all their significance from their initial production.

One might reply that the illustrated story still contains a loss. The daughter and father did not draw together. That is true. A practice can possess value while lacking another valuable feature. My objection neither makes the alternatives equivalent nor treats relational significance as compensation sufficient for every lost achievement. It blocks the stronger inference that removing a productive contribution leaves only functional value. The correct description identifies a particular missing good alongside the goods that remain.

We also need to distinguish assistance with expression from substitution for attention. Imagine a person who uses a language model to translate a condolence message, checks the translation with a competent speaker, removes an inappropriate phrase, and sends it because it accurately expresses what she wishes to say. Contrast someone who automatically sends an unchecked message merely to clear a social obligation. The linguistic machinery may be similar; the practical attitudes are different. The first person has attended to the recipient, exercised judgment, and taken responsibility for the communication.

This does not mean endorsement can manufacture every missing achievement. Approving an answer is not solving a problem; selecting a performance is not performing it. Where the constitutive point of a practice is the participant’s own exercise of a skill, delegation can defeat that point. A student cannot satisfy a requirement to demonstrate independent reasoning merely by endorsing generated prose. The operative criterion is whether the delegated contribution is constitutive of the good at issue.

That criterion also avoids making difficulty a universal measure of care. Effort can express concern, but so can perceptiveness, timely assistance, and responsiveness to another person’s needs. More labor is not always better care. Conversely, avoiding a burden can express disregard when undertaking it is precisely what the relationship reasonably demands. We must identify the demand before treating a shortcut as evidence of its violation.

### 3. From a lost activity to a poorer life

The preceding distinction cannot by itself answer a prediction about widespread displacement. It remains possible that commercially dominant forms of AI will discourage the very attention and judgment that meaningful assistance requires. The serious question is therefore comparative: under which arrangements does adoption reduce people’s effective opportunities to engage in valuable activities?

A local loss and an overall impoverishment are different claims. Suppose an organization automates routine correspondence. Employees lose some opportunities to exercise stylistic judgment. Whether their working lives become poorer also depends on what happens to the time released, their influence over the change, and the other activities available. They might receive larger workloads and less discretion. They might instead gain time for difficult cases requiring sustained personal attention. These are alternative possibilities, not predictions about what employers generally do.

Neither possibility erases the local loss. It would be insensitive to tell a displaced writer that someone else’s convenience cancels her injury. The comparison must track who loses which opportunities, who receives the benefits, and whether meaningful alternatives are realistically available to the affected person. Aggregate productivity provides no answer to those questions. Equally, identifying one loss cannot establish an overall decline without addressing them.

This matters because an argument about a threat can take two forms. A possibility argument identifies a coherent mechanism of harm. A comparative risk argument explains why a particular deployment is sufficiently likely to cause that harm, relative to available alternatives, to warrant a particular response. The first can justify investigation and precaution. Stronger practical conclusions require information about prevalence, magnitude, distribution, and available protections. Philosophical examples can identify the mechanism without determining those quantities.

MacKenzie’s discussion of temptation and novice expertise identifies a credible mechanism (preprint p. 12). But locating the mechanism should lead to discriminating between environments. A course that rewards polished submissions without examining understanding creates different incentives from one that requires explanations, revision histories, and independent performance. A workplace that measures only output volume differs from one that gives workers authority over when assistance is appropriate. These arrangements can expose the same technical capability to different forms of oversight and resistance.

Nguyen’s account of value capture helps explain why this institutional level matters. Simplified evaluative standards can come to govern practical reasoning and displace more complex commitments (2024). Applied here, the question is whether speed, volume, or surface polish becomes authoritative at the expense of understanding and care. AI can intensify such pressures. Their force nevertheless depends partly on which standards institutions reward and whether participants can contest them.

Skill acquisition creates a further concern. Someone who repeatedly delegates a task may never acquire the ability to judge its performance. The resulting dependency can diminish future choice. This is a stronger objection than the observation that a single generated message took less effort: it identifies a possible deterioration in the conditions of agency itself. It supports protecting opportunities for independent practice, especially where later judgment depends upon those opportunities.

Yet competence in performing an activity is not a necessary condition of every appropriate appreciation of it. A non-musician can admire a difficult performance; a person unable to speak can understand the significance of another’s careful words. Testimony, observation, and analogous experiences can support that understanding. Firsthand skill may deepen appreciation without being its exclusive source. The relevant distinction is between losing one route to richer appreciation and becoming incapable of appreciating the process at all.

These considerations make the threat more precise. What needs protection is sustained access to meaningful participation and the development of judgment. Some uses of AI will undermine those goods; others may support them. The existence of protective arrangements does not prove that they will be adopted. It does establish that the evaluative object should be a particular practice and its institutional setting, rather than generated content considered in isolation.

### 4. What does adoption reveal about past values?

The retrospective inference presents a separate problem. Suppose a friend now uses AI to help write messages. Can this show that she never valued personally composed correspondence? It can count as evidence in a suitably specified case. It cannot establish that conclusion merely through the fact of assistance.

At least four explanations are possible. She previously cared little about composition. She still values it but faces a new constraint. Her values have changed. Or she understands this assistance as compatible with personal authorship and attention. The same observable behavior can arise from these different histories. Distinguishing them requires information beyond the behavior itself.

Consider a friend recovering from surgery who finds writing exhausting. She supplies the substance of a message, uses assistance to arrange it, and checks that it expresses her meaning. It would be implausible to treat her adoption as a revelation that years of earlier correspondence were insincere. The new practice could instead reflect the continuing importance she attaches to staying in touch under changed circumstances. Assistance might even reveal a commitment that would otherwise have been frustrated.

The general point does not depend on exceptional hardship. Valuing something need not give it decisive priority in every decision. A person can value live performance while sometimes listening to recordings, or cooking while buying a prepared meal. Choosing an alternative reveals a practical ranking under particular conditions. It does not by itself demonstrate that the displaced value had no weight.

Historical interpretation adds another difficulty. Present attitudes do not transparently reveal past ones. Technologies can change costs, expectations, social norms, and people’s conceptions of an activity. Someone who has become indifferent may previously have cared. A painful change in a relationship should not automatically be redescribed as the discovery that the relationship was always misunderstood.

The strongest version of the retrospective argument therefore requires supplementary evidence. Perhaps the person explicitly disavows any interest in the activity, faces no relevant constraint, and reports that this was always her attitude. Perhaps a sustained pattern of conduct defeats more charitable interpretations. In such circumstances, technology may help expose a real discrepancy. But the evidential work is performed jointly by adoption and those additional facts.

This qualification changes the normative response. If we already know that a person lacks a relevant concern, disappointment may be appropriate. If we know only that the person used a tool, an accusatory interpretation can itself damage the relationship. It can deny the significance of the attention, effort, or care that took a different form. The possibility of misrecognizing assisted agency must be considered alongside the possibility of mistaking automation for care.

None of this makes all explanations equally plausible. Routine, undisclosed outsourcing may reasonably raise suspicion. The claim is that the suspicion remains defeasible and that its retrospective reach requires argument. Evidence of present delegation, evidence of present indifference, and evidence of longstanding indifference are three different things.

### 5. What the poetry evidence establishes

Empirical evidence can sharpen this distinction. Porter and Machery’s study, discussed by MacKenzie, found that nonexpert readers rated the sampled AI poems favorably on several dimensions and struggled to identify their origins. It also found lower evaluations when readers were told that poems were AI-generated rather than human-written (Porter and Machery 2024). These findings separate actual provenance, believed provenance, and perceived qualities.

The distinction matters philosophically. Enjoying a generated poem while taking it to be human-written cannot straightforwardly reveal indifference to human authorship. The reader’s evaluation proceeds under a belief that the allegedly dispensable feature is present. Failure to detect a property is not equivalent to regarding that property as unimportant.

Nor does preferring an accessible poem establish an unwillingness to appreciate complex human expression. A judgment about these examples, under these conditions, does not settle what a person would value with more time, context, or guidance. Accessibility can also be a legitimate aesthetic good. To infer shallowness, one would need an additional account of why the preferred qualities are insufficient, wrongly prioritized, or appreciated in a defective way.

The labeling effect does not prove a rich commitment to creative agency either. It might reflect expectations about quality, social stereotypes, or other attitudes. The result is informative precisely because it complicates a simple interpretation in either direction. Preference for particular outputs can coexist with sensitivity to provenance without demonstrating what grounds that sensitivity.

A more direct inquiry into process valuing would distinguish what readers believe about production, whether they care about it, why they care, and how that concern affects choices when other costs are held in view. A retrospective inquiry would additionally require evidence about earlier attitudes. The reported experiments do not establish that earlier commitment was absent. They are therefore compatible with disagreement about poetic qualities without establishing a general collapse of shared concern for human creation.

This objection leaves the experimental findings intact. It concerns their philosophical interpretation. A painful mismatch between an artist’s ambitions and an audience’s immediate preferences is possible. Whether that mismatch reveals a defect in the audience, an error in the artist’s expectations, or an ordinary difference of interest remains a further question.

### 6. Recognition without evaluative agreement

Even established disagreement does not settle which emotion is fitting. There is an important difference between explaining why a reaction occurs, identifying a reason for dissatisfaction, and justifying loathing. A threat to livelihood can explain fear; an unmet hope can explain disappointment. Neither automatically establishes that another person, or the disagreement itself, merits a more hostile response.

MacKenzie appeals to both practical interests and the desire to be recognized through meaningful commitments (preprint p. 15). Those considerations deserve weight. A livelihood and an identity cannot be dismissed as mere vanity. Nevertheless, recognizing a commitment and sharing the evaluation that sustains it are different achievements.

A friend can understand why I devote years to restoring old radios, encourage my work, and protect time for it without finding radios intrinsically interesting. She recognizes something about me without adopting my first-person relationship to the activity. Indeed, her attention may have special significance because it responds to my attachment rather than her own.

Similarly, a customer can respect a craftsperson while purchasing an inexpensive alternative. The purchase does not exhaust the customer’s attitude toward the person or the craft. An audience member can admire an artist’s dedication without enjoying the work. These possibilities show that agreement about products is neither necessary nor sufficient for adequate recognition of their makers.

The converse is equally important. A collector might prize a painting while treating its maker contemptuously. A company might value employees’ distinctive outputs while denying them a voice in decisions. Agreement about a product’s excellence can coexist with a failure to recognize the people involved. A theory of recognition therefore needs more than a measure of alignment in aesthetic or productive preferences.

What additional premises could justify stronger criticism? Special relationships, explicit assurances, reciprocal commitments, and institutional responsibilities can create legitimate expectations. If partners agree that their anniversary letters will be personally composed, secretly replacing that activity may breach the agreement. If a workplace invites employees to develop particular skills and then dismisses those commitments without meaningful consultation, the transition may involve disrespect as well as loss. The criticism is anchored in the relationship and the expectation it supports.

By contrast, an anonymous audience generally has not promised to value every creator’s process in the creator’s preferred way. Its attention is limited, and its members have commitments of their own. We can have good reasons to hope for their appreciation without having grounds to condemn them for withholding it. This does not reduce art to private taste. It limits what follows about an audience’s character from the fact that it does not share a particular attachment.

What if loathing targets the discord itself rather than the audience? That possibility avoids the charge of misplaced blame, but it still requires a judgment about the discord’s significance. Some disagreements obstruct relationships or valuable institutions. Others make room for different forms of life. An aversion to every mismatch would demand an unreasonable degree of convergence. The appropriate negative response depends on which shared good the divergence damages.

A person may reasonably grieve the loss of an appreciative community even when no individual has behaved wrongly. Such grief is consistent with respecting others’ freedom to care differently. Keeping this possibility in view prevents an account of damaged meaning from becoming an account in which others must ratify our self-conceptions to count as caring about us.

### 7. A more discriminating criticism of AI

The practical consequence of these arguments is a more specific standard of assessment. We should ask what participation constitutes the good at issue, whether assistance preserves the relevant judgment and responsibility, and whether the arrangements surrounding its use maintain people’s future opportunities to participate. In interpersonal cases, we should also ask what expectations the parties can reasonably make of one another.

These questions explain why superficially similar uses can deserve different evaluations. Generating an assignment may bypass the learning it is meant to demonstrate. Translation may enable the communication a relationship needs. Automating a workplace task may deprive workers of meaningful discretion or release them for activities they value more. A generated gift may violate an assurance of personal craftsmanship or become the record of a valuable shared experience.

The distinctions also identify where criticism can be forceful without being indiscriminate. Institutions should preserve routes to expertise, give affected participants a meaningful role in changes to their work, and resist standards that reward only speed and surface quality. Relationships require honesty about contributions where those contributions bear on legitimate expectations. These are substantive demands; satisfying them cannot be assumed merely because a human approves the final output.

MacKenzie is right to direct attention beyond output quality. The further conclusion defended here is that this attention must extend beyond the moment of production and beyond adoption considered as a solitary act. Valuable processes include judgment, selection, giving, learning, and shared use. Recognition can survive differences in what people enjoy. The strongest objection to harmful AI deployment is therefore that it can remove particular conditions of meaningful agency and reciprocal regard. Demonstrating that objection requires showing which conditions are lost, for whom, and under what arrangements. The presence of generated content cannot do that work by itself.
