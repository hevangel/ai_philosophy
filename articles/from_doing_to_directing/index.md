# From Doing to Directing: Process, Results, and the Bootstrapping Problem in AI-Native Education

## Executive Summary

Generative AI creates a problem for education that is more fundamental than plagiarism or academic integrity. If AI-assisted workers can eventually produce work faster than equally skilled manual workers, then an educational system organized around unaided production risks training students for a workflow that no longer exists. Yet a simple response—“let students use AI from day one”—creates a second problem: **how does a person become capable of judging AI-produced work if that person never acquired the underlying competence that earlier generations gained by doing the work themselves?**

Software engineering is the best early laboratory for this question because AI adoption there is already extensive and comparatively measurable. Google's 2025 DORA research, based on nearly 5,000 technology professionals and more than 100 hours of qualitative data, found AI use at work had reached 90%; more than 80% of respondents believed it increased productivity. Yet DORA simultaneously found that greater AI adoption remained associated with lower delivery stability unless organizations had strong testing, version control, feedback loops, and other control systems. Stack Overflow's 2025 survey similarly found that 84% of respondents were using or planning to use AI tools, while more developers distrusted AI accuracy than trusted it. By 2026, the pattern had sharpened into **conditional trust**: 48% said they trusted AI when its output was easily verifiable, but only 6.6% trusted it for important decisions.

The emerging lesson from software is therefore not simply **“use AI.”** It is:

> **Automate production aggressively where appropriate, but make specification, verification, feedback, provenance, and accountability stronger as production becomes cheaper.**

This is reinforced by company experiments but also qualified by controlled research. OpenAI reports an internal software project in which every line of code, tests, continuous-integration configuration, documentation, and tooling was generated by Codex, with the team estimating roughly a tenfold reduction in development time; its summary of the operating model is essentially “humans steer, agents execute.” Yet an independent METR randomized trial of experienced open-source developers found the opposite in its particular setting: early-2025 AI tools made 16 experienced developers completing 246 real tasks **19% slower**, despite the developers believing AI had accelerated them. Thus the premise that AI necessarily makes every worker faster is not empirically established as of October 2026. What *is* established is that AI has become embedded enough in professional workflows that excluding it from education altogether increasingly creates an authenticity problem.

The learning evidence is equally mixed and, importantly, points toward **design rather than prohibition**. In a 2025 *PNAS* field experiment involving nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students, unrestricted GPT-4 raised assisted practice performance substantially but produced a 17% reduction relative to controls when students later took an unassisted examination. A guardrailed tutor that used teacher-designed hints largely eliminated that learning penalty. By contrast, a 2025 randomized college-physics study found that a carefully designed AI tutor produced substantially greater learning than an active-learning class, with an estimated effect of 0.73–1.3 standard deviations while students typically spent less time on task. A 2026 Anthropic randomized experiment is especially relevant to the user's question: among 52 mostly junior software engineers learning an unfamiliar Python library, AI-assisted participants scored 50% on a subsequent mastery quiz versus 67% for hand-coding participants, with the largest gap appearing in debugging—the very skill required to detect erroneous AI code. But participants who used AI for explanation and conceptual inquiry performed better than those who simply delegated production.

These findings suggest a defensible philosophy of education:

> **Education should not preserve manual production merely because previous generations learned that way. Its purpose should be to produce autonomous people who can create value, with or without AI, while understanding enough to specify what should be produced, detect important failures, verify evidence, explain and defend judgments, and accept responsibility for outcomes. Manual work remains justified wherever it is necessary to produce or test those capacities, but it should be a means rather than an unquestioned end.**

This paper calls that model **verification-first AI-native education**. It differs both from traditional *manual-first* education and from unrestricted *AI-first* education. Students should use AI early because AI operation, orchestration, and judgment are themselves emerging competencies. But AI should initially be designed to preserve cognitive participation: asking for predictions, explanations, counterexamples, error diagnoses, citations, tests, and defenses rather than silently replacing them. Selected no-AI exercises should remain—not because handwriting, manual coding, or unaided essay production is inherently superior, but because temporary removal of AI is currently one of the best diagnostic tools for determining whether a learner possesses a mental model independent of the machine.

The central unresolved question is the **bootstrapping problem**. Almost all current software professionals capable of supervising AI acquired substantial competence before highly capable generative AI existed. Even Anthropic's 2026 skill-formation experiment studied participants who had already used Python at least weekly for more than a year. Consequently, industry experience cannot yet tell us whether a child who starts programming, philosophy, engineering, or law with powerful AI from day one can eventually become as good a verifier as today's experts. That generation has barely had time to exist. This is not an argument for banning AI; it is an argument for treating the question as an empirical problem and running longitudinal experiments rather than turning inherited educational practice into dogma.

## Thesis and Philosophical Framework

The conventional framing of the educational AI debate—*process versus results*—is too crude. Process does not have one fixed value. Its importance changes according to the relationship between the person doing the work and the person evaluating it.

The theory developed here begins with three relational categories.

| Relationship | Why process matters | Why results matter | Appropriate default |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Self** | The process is part of one's own experience, identity, enjoyment, struggle, learning, and self-development. | One normally also wants the activity to accomplish something. | Individual may rationally value both process and result. |
| **Independent other / stranger** | Ordinarily less important because the recipient does not experience the producer's effort; however, process still matters when it affects rights, fraud, safety, provenance, exploitation, or trust. | The recipient primarily encounters the value, quality, reliability, or usefulness delivered. | Judge primarily by value plus ethical legitimacy and accountability. |
| **Supervised learner**—child, student, apprentice, junior employee | Process matters because the supervisor has a developmental responsibility: today's method changes tomorrow's competence. | Immediate results matter, but they are partly evidence about development rather than the sole objective. | Optimize the process for eventual autonomous competence. |

This framework explains why it can be perfectly consistent for a person to enjoy writing an essay unaided, not care whether an independent author's essay was drafted with AI, and nevertheless restrict a student's AI use on a particular exercise. The evaluator occupies three different relationships.

But the third category contains a temporal feature that is crucial for educational philosophy. **Supervision is supposed to end.** The purpose of raising a child is not to preserve dependence on the parent. The purpose of teaching is not to make the student permanently imitate the teacher. The purpose of apprenticeship is not to perpetually preserve the apprentice's inefficient methods. Education succeeds partly when its special process constraints become unnecessary.

This leads to a **principle of transitional supervision**:

> A teacher is justified in controlling a learner's process to the extent that doing so contributes to capabilities the learner will need once that control is removed.

A process requirement therefore needs justification. “I learned this way” is historically descriptive, not educationally sufficient.

The framework becomes clearer if “process value” is separated into four kinds.

**Intrinsic or experiential process value** belongs primarily to the participant. Someone may value writing, proving, programming, painting, praying, or deriving equations by hand because doing so is meaningful. AI should not make such experiences illegitimate.

**Developmental process value** exists when doing something is a means of acquiring competence. The learner works through a proof not because society urgently needs another handwritten proof, but because attempting proofs may develop mathematical judgment.

**Constitutive process value** exists when the process is part of what the activity *is*. A scientific result that cannot be traced to defensible methods is epistemically deficient even if the conclusion happens to be correct. Certain legal procedures cannot simply be replaced by an algorithmically correct outcome because procedural rights are themselves part of justice. Some literary, artistic, and theological educational activities may similarly make authorship, reflection, interpretation, or spiritual formation part of the intended good.

**Assurance process value** arises because recipients need evidence that results are safe and trustworthy. Software tests, laboratory replication, legal citation checking, engineering review, and source verification matter even to strangers who have no interest in how hard the producer worked. Software-industry behavior illustrates this distinction: Stack Overflow's 2026 survey found 93% of respondents considered source attribution at least somewhat important when deciding whether to trust AI answers, including 79% who considered it important or very important.

This qualification is important. The statement *“for strangers, I care about the result, not their process”* is often sensible as a consumer principle, but it cannot mean that any hidden process is morally irrelevant. A bridge that works today but was never structurally checked, a legal brief containing fabricated precedents, or a scientific result produced through falsified data cannot be made acceptable simply by looking impressive. The relevant distinction is not **process versus results** but **process as effort** versus **process as assurance, rights, or constitutive integrity**.

### The evaluator question

The hardest question is the one raised directly in the preceding discussion:

> **If you do not know how to produce something yourself, can you really evaluate it?**

The answer is **sometimes yes, but not automatically**.

Production and verification are different cognitive tasks. One can sometimes verify more than one can generate. A person may be unable to devise a sophisticated mathematical proof yet follow and check one. A software engineer may not remember a library's exact syntax yet understand its architecture well enough to identify an unsafe design. A scientific reviewer need not personally have discovered an experiment to evaluate its methods. In software especially, executable tests, static analysis, compilers, specifications, type systems, benchmarks, and reproducible execution allow portions of verification to be **externalized**.

But there is a limit. Verification requires knowing *what could be wrong*. Without a domain model, a novice may verify superficial features while missing conceptual failures. The Microsoft Research/CHI 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers found that greater confidence in GenAI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort, while greater confidence in one's own ability was associated with more; participants described AI-era critical thinking as shifting toward verification, integration, and stewardship. This does not prove that AI causes cognitive decline—the study was self-report and correlational—but it identifies precisely the new educational task: if generation is delegated, the human must become better, not worse, at stewardship.

Anthropic's 2026 coding experiment makes the potential circularity concrete. AI can generate code; humans therefore need debugging skill to oversee it. But in that experiment, the largest learning deficit among AI-assisted participants occurred in debugging. The technology whose outputs require oversight can, when badly used during training, reduce acquisition of the skill needed to provide that oversight.

That is the **bootstrapping problem**:

> **How does an AI-first novice acquire the expertise necessary to supervise a system that is performing the very activities through which previous experts acquired their expertise?**

Current industry success does not answer it, because today's senior engineers are an unusual transitional generation: they developed domain expertise in a largely pre-generative-AI environment and subsequently added AI to an existing mental model.

This point should prevent two opposite errors. It is too early to conclude that AI-first education cannot work. It is equally too early to conclude that because experienced engineers can effectively supervise AI, novices can skip the experiences that created those engineers.

## Evidence from Education and Software Engineering

The evidence accumulated from 2024 through 2026 does not support either a blanket prohibition or unconditional AI adoption. It supports a more specific proposition: **AI's effect depends heavily on task design, learner behavior, prior knowledge, and the availability of verification structures.**

### What educational experiments actually show

The Bastani et al. *PNAS* field experiment is unusually important because it separated **AI-assisted performance** from **learning retained after AI was removed**. Nearly 1,000 students used either a standard GPT-4-like interface, a more constrained tutor using teacher-generated hints, or a control condition. Unrestricted GPT access increased practice grades by 48%, yet those students subsequently scored 17% lower than the control group on an unassisted examination. The guardrailed tutor generated much larger assisted gains while its students' later unassisted performance was statistically indistinguishable from control.

That result demonstrates why educational evaluation cannot simply measure the quality of AI-assisted homework. **Performance and learning are different dependent variables.** A system can raise the former while lowering the latter.

Kestin et al.'s 2025 *Scientific Reports* randomized crossover experiment demonstrates the opposite side of the picture. College physics students taught with a research-informed AI tutor learned substantially more than students in an in-person active-learning lesson. The estimated treatment effect after controlling for prior knowledge and other factors was 0.63 standard deviations by linear regression and approximately 0.73–1.3 standard deviations after accounting for ceiling effects. The median AI-tutor student spent 49 minutes on the material compared with an assumed 60 minutes of instructional time in class.

These studies are not contradictory. One gave students a tool capable of supplying solutions and observed harmful dependency; the other intentionally designed AI around educational practices. The implication is that **“AI or no AI” is the wrong independent variable**. The interaction design matters.

Broader evidence likewise shows positive average effects alongside heterogeneity. A 2026 meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* synthesized 35 studies involving 4,193 participants and reported a moderately positive overall effect of ChatGPT on learning outcomes, while finding significant variation by instructional mode, subject, and intervention duration. A 2025–26 systematic review of generative AI in higher education similarly characterized the empirical literature as mixed rather than uniformly beneficial or harmful.

Large observational evidence should be interpreted even more cautiously. OECD's PISA 2025 results report that students using AI for particular schoolwork activities such as drafting, summarizing, and research scored about 20 science points below non-users on average, while weekly general-purpose AI users performed similarly to non-users and students combining general AI use with school opportunities to acquire AI literacy tended to score slightly higher. OECD explicitly describes the relationship between AI use and performance as complex; these associations do not establish causality.

The most directly relevant emerging evidence is Anthropic's January 2026 coding-skill study. Its 52 participants were **not AI-first novices**: they were mostly junior engineers who had been using Python at least weekly for over a year. They were randomized while learning the unfamiliar Trio library. The AI group subsequently averaged 50% on a mastery test compared with 67% in the hand-coding group; the AI condition's speed advantage was small and statistically insignificant. Crucially, qualitative analysis suggested that *how* AI was used mattered: wholesale delegation and AI-led debugging clustered with low mastery, while conceptual inquiry and generation followed by active comprehension clustered with better outcomes. Anthropic explicitly notes the small sample, immediate assessment, and unresolved longitudinal effects.

That experiment is therefore evidence **for the bootstrapping concern**, but it is not evidence that an intentionally designed AI-first curriculum would fail.

### What software engineering has learned first

Software engineering offers a useful model because it has relatively strong external verification mechanisms and unusually rapid AI adoption.

DORA's 2025 research found AI use among software-development professionals at 90%, with a median of approximately two hours of daily interaction reported in Google's summary. DORA found positive relationships between AI adoption and delivery throughput/product performance but a continuing negative relationship with delivery stability. Its interpretation is systemic: accelerated production exposes weaknesses downstream unless teams strengthen automated testing, version control, modularity, and feedback loops. DORA's current metrics accordingly evaluate both **throughput** and **instability**, rather than treating speed as success by itself.

This is an important educational analogy. If AI lets students generate ten essays, programs, designs, or analyses in the time previously needed for one, education should not simply celebrate output volume. It needs the equivalent of software's tests, reviews, incident detection, and rollback mechanisms.

The Stack Overflow surveys show the same emerging professional norm. In 2025, 84% of respondents were using or planning to use AI in development, and 51% of professional developers reported daily use, yet 46% actively distrusted AI accuracy versus 33% who trusted it. In 2026, 48% reported trusting AI where the output could be readily verified, while only 6.6% trusted it for important decisions; among developers with one to five years of experience, trust for important decisions was only 4%. The 2026 survey was fielded from June 23 through August 5 and contained 30,903 responses marked valid, although its recruitment through Stack Overflow properties and opted-in community channels means it should not be interpreted as a representative census of all developers.

Developers are also not responding to AI by abandoning traditional knowledge entirely. In Stack Overflow's 2026 survey, 79% reported focusing on AI programming skills, but 52% were also learning coding/programming skills and 59% still used technical documentation for learning.

Company case studies suggest where the workflow may be heading. OpenAI reports an internal project in which Codex generated every line of code as well as tests, continuous-integration configuration, documentation, observability, and tooling; the team estimated it completed the project in roughly one-tenth the hand-coding time. OpenAI's 2026 internal research-organization analysis likewise reports increased coding-agent use and more experiments, while noting that agent success still requires considerable human steering as tasks grow more complex; more than half of successful tasks estimated at four to eight hours involved at least one human intervention. OpenAI also explicitly cautions that increased experiment volume is difficult to interpret causally because compute availability increased simultaneously.

These are informative observations, not independent controlled evidence. METR's randomized study provides a valuable counterweight: with 16 developers, 246 real tasks, and mature repositories the developers knew for an average of five years, early-2025 tools increased completion time by 19%, contrary both to participant expectations and to expert forecasts.

The responsible conclusion in 2026 is therefore not *“manual work cannot compete with AI in every task.”* Rather:

> **AI is becoming a normal part of professional production, sometimes producing very large gains, sometimes little benefit, and sometimes measurable slowdown. Competitiveness increasingly depends not merely on possessing AI but on knowing when to delegate, how to specify, how to verify, and how to integrate AI into an effective system of work.**

## The Bootstrapping Problem and Educational Models

The core design question can now be stated more precisely.

Traditional professional education implicitly followed this sequence:

**produce manually → encounter errors → develop internal models → acquire judgment → supervise others/tools**

An AI-first environment is tempted to reverse it:

**ask AI to produce → inspect output → somehow acquire judgment**

The word *somehow* is the problem.

The old system may contain substantial inefficiency. Students often spend time memorizing syntax, formatting, boilerplate, calculations, or prose-production conventions that machines can now handle well. But some of that apparently inefficient struggle may have been the mechanism through which students acquired error models.

Anthropic's coding experiment offers a plausible example. Participants without AI encountered more syntax and Trio-concept errors and had to resolve them; the researchers hypothesize that resolving those errors contributed to their stronger debugging performance. This does not establish that every learner must reproduce traditional struggle. It suggests that curricula need a replacement mechanism for the learning previously generated by mistakes.

The philosophical mistake would be to confuse **historical mechanism** with **necessary mechanism**.

Perhaps manually making and correcting 100 mistakes is necessary to become a good verifier.

But perhaps an AI-native curriculum could expose a learner to 1,000 deliberately constructed mistakes, force diagnosis and correction, provide instantaneous adaptive feedback, and produce superior verification expertise without requiring the learner to originate every artifact.

That alternative has not yet been adequately tested.

### Three educational models

| Dimension | Manual-first | Unrestricted AI-first | Verification-first AI-native hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starting assumption** | Foundational production ability must precede tool use. | Professional reality is AI-mediated, so students should work that way immediately. | AI should appear early, but interaction must be designed around comprehension and verification. |
| **Typical first-year task** | Write/solve/code from scratch without GenAI. | Prompt AI for a working artifact and refine it. | Predict → prompt → inspect → test → explain → revise → defend. |
| **Main strength** | Builds an independent baseline and exposes learners to errors directly. | Maximizes authenticity, AI fluency, iteration speed, and tool familiarity. | Attempts to acquire both AI fluency and independent judgment. |
| **Main weakness** | May spend scarce instructional time on production activities that have become economically cheap or obsolete. | Can create false competence: high-quality artifacts without corresponding understanding. | More complex to design and assess; evidence base is still immature. |
| **Role of manual work** | Dominant during early education. | Minimal or absent. | Targeted diagnostic exercise used where it builds or tests a needed mental model. |
| **Role of AI** | Delayed reward/tool after fundamentals. | Default producer from day one. | Tutor, generator, adversary, critic, simulator, and production agent from day one. |
| **Assessment risk** | Measures abilities students may rarely exercise professionally. | Product quality can conceal learner incompetence. | Requires dual assessment of artifact quality and independent competence. |
| **Current evidential status** | Historically established route by which today's experts were trained, but not designed for current AI workflows. | Insufficient longitudinal evidence for true novices. | Most consistent with current mixed evidence, but also requires direct longitudinal testing. |

The strongest current option is therefore not “manual first and AI later,” nor “AI does everything immediately.” It is an **AI-native hybrid in which verification is taught from the beginning**.

This differs importantly from merely allowing ChatGPT in a conventional class. AI-native pedagogy changes the learning objective.

Instead of asking:

> Can the student produce this artifact without AI?

the principal professional question becomes:

> Can the student reliably cause a good artifact to exist, know why it is good, detect when it is bad, identify evidence supporting it, repair failures, defend consequential decisions, and recognize when AI should not be trusted?

A second, diagnostic question remains:

> What must this learner still be able to do when the AI is removed in order to perform those responsibilities?

That is where selective manual work remains justified.

### The verifiability gradient

Software should not be generalized mechanically to every field. Software has properties that make aggressive AI delegation unusually feasible: programs can often be executed; tests can be automated; output can be compared with specifications; changes can be versioned and rolled back; failures can generate telemetry.

The feasibility of AI-first education therefore depends partly on a field's **verifiability gradient**.

At one end are tasks for which errors are cheaply and objectively detectable: compilation, unit tests, numerical checks, formal proofs under proof assistants, and some engineering simulations.

At the other end are tasks whose quality depends heavily on context, interpretation, contested premises, tacit judgment, human meaning, or normative reasoning: pastoral advice, literary voice, philosophical originality, legal strategy, historical interpretation.

The more external verification a discipline permits, the less human competence needs to duplicate production exactly. The less externalizable verification is, the more dangerous it becomes to train people as mere AI operators without deep domain understanding.

This gives a general rule:

> **The easier it is to verify an output independently of knowing how to produce it, the stronger the case for early production automation. The harder verification is, the stronger the case for preserving substantial foundational domain formation.**

### From software factory to knowledge factory

The software industry's strongest transferable insight is not code generation. It is the architecture surrounding generation.

DORA's evidence emphasizes small batches, rapid feedback, testing, stability, and strong underlying systems rather than tool adoption by itself. OpenAI's internal agentic workflows similarly place humans in steering and evaluation roles rather than measuring success by manually typed code.

A generalized **AI factory** for intellectual work therefore looks like this:

1. Human defines purpose, audience, and constraints.
2. Specification and acceptance criteria are written.
3. AI decomposes the work and proposes a plan.
4. AI generates the artifact or its components.
5. Automated checks run: tests, retrieval, citations, consistency.
6. If machine checks fail, return to step 3.
7. If machine checks pass, a human verifies the semantics and domain content.
8. Can the verifier explain and defend the critical claims? If not, escalate: reconstruction, a second model, source review, or expert review, then return to step 2.
9. If yes, release, submit, publish, or deploy.
10. Feed outcomes, errors, incidents, and objections back into the specification.

The educational point is subtle. **Students should not merely be trained to stand at step 4 and “prompt better.”** They need to learn steps 1, 2, 5, 7, 8, and 10. As generation becomes easier, those stages constitute more of the human comparative advantage.

Anthropic's 2026 AI Fluency Index illustrates why this must be explicit. In 9,830 Claude conversations, iterative refinement was common, but fact checking and challenging AI reasoning were much rarer; when AI generated artifacts, users were less likely to question the reasoning or notice missing context. AI fluency therefore cannot be reduced to prompt fluency.

## Curriculum, Assessment, and Discipline Transfer

An AI-native curriculum should separate several competencies that traditional education often bundled together under the single phrase “knows how to do the work.”

| Competency | Meaning | Importance in an AI-dominant environment | Suitable assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Generative competence** | Ability to create an artifact from scratch. | Still useful, but the amount required may decline for routinized production. | Short unaided tasks; reconstruction of critical components. |
| **Conceptual competence** | Understanding principles, structures, causal relationships, assumptions, and domain vocabulary. | Essential because it supplies the mental model against which AI output is judged. | Oral explanation, concept mapping, novel transfer questions. |
| **Specification competence** | Defining the actual problem, constraints, acceptance criteria, and desired value. | Becomes more important as production gets cheaper. | Write a specification/rubric before AI use; identify ambiguous requirements. |
| **Orchestration competence** | Decomposing work, selecting tools/models, providing context, iterating, and coordinating agents. | New core professional competency. | Timed AI-assisted project with full interaction record. |
| **Verification competence** | Detecting errors, unsupported claims, unsafe assumptions, and boundary failures. | Potentially the central bottleneck of AI-native work. | Seeded-error diagnosis, adversarial examples, test construction. |
| **Provenance competence** | Knowing where claims, data, quotations, precedents, or design assumptions came from. | Critical when plausible generated material can be fabricated or decontextualized. | Source tracing and citation audit. Stack Overflow respondents increasingly demand attribution for AI answers. |
| **Calibration competence** | Knowing when one's own or AI confidence exceeds actual knowledge. | Necessary to decide when to escalate. | Confidence-rated answers scored for calibration as well as correctness. |
| **Accountability competence** | Ability to explain and take responsibility for consequential choices. | Cannot simply be delegated with generation. | Oral defense, incident review, professional-signoff simulation. |
| **Recovery competence** | Ability to intervene when AI fails or is unavailable. | Particularly important in high-stakes or brittle systems. | Restricted-tool incident simulations. |

This distinction answers the question *“Do students have to know how to do it in order to evaluate it?”* more precisely.

They may not need **full generative fluency** at the same level as a pre-AI professional. But they almost certainly need **conceptual, diagnostic, and verification fluency**. Whether those can be taught without substantial manual generation is the central empirical question.

### A proposed curriculum

A verification-first AI-native program would introduce AI **from day one**, but not as an answer machine.

Early work would alternate among four modes.

**AI-as-tutor:** the system may ask questions, provide hints, generate examples, explain concepts, and adapt difficulty, but it should not always reveal finished solutions. This direction is consistent with the difference between unrestricted and guardrailed tutoring observed by Bastani et al., and with the step-by-step learning model described by OpenAI's Study Mode.

**AI-as-generator:** students ask the system to produce sophisticated artifacts earlier than they otherwise could, letting beginners inspect expert-like outputs and work on larger problems.

**AI-as-fallible colleague:** instructors deliberately provide generated artifacts containing subtle mistakes. Students must find them, classify them, fix them, and design tests that would have caught them.

**AI-as-adversary:** the model produces objections, edge cases, alternative interpretations, hostile cross-examination, counterexamples, or conflicting evidence. The student learns to defend rather than merely generate.

Manual work remains, but in **small, strategically chosen doses**. A programmer may have to implement an algorithm unaided, not because professional life will forbid coding agents, but because the teacher needs evidence that the student understands control flow, data structures, state, and debugging. A philosophy student may have to construct a short argument without AI, not because every future paper must be written manually, but because the instructor needs to know whether the student can recognize validity and formulate reasons independently.

In this model, a no-AI task is analogous to a stress test, not a lifestyle prescription.

### Assessment should become dual-channel

Traditional take-home essays and assignments increasingly conflate two questions:

1. *How good is this product?*
2. *How capable is this student?*

AI breaks the assumption that the first reliably measures the second.

Assessment should therefore use two channels.

The **production channel** measures the quality of what a learner can accomplish using the realistic tools of the profession: correctness, usefulness, originality where relevant, efficiency, robustness, source quality, and responsiveness to requirements.

The **competence channel** measures whether the learner understands and can govern the production system: oral defense, unseen transfer, error diagnosis, source authentication, critical reconstruction, counterexample generation, uncertainty calibration, and selected no-AI tasks.

This is not a return to universal closed-book examinations. It is a recognition that an AI-generated artifact has weak evidential value about the human unless the institution separately measures the human.

### Transfer across disciplines

| Discipline | AI can increasingly produce | Human verification must emphasize | Useful diagnostic checkpoint | How much is process constitutive? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Philosophy** | Argument drafts, objections, literature summaries, thought experiments, prose. | Validity, premise plausibility, hidden assumptions, counterexamples, source fidelity, originality of intellectual position. | Oral defense against a novel objection; repair a subtly invalid argument without AI. | Moderate to high when the educational aim is formation of philosophical judgment rather than publication alone. |
| **Theology** | Exegesis drafts, homily structures, historical summaries, doctrinal comparisons. | Scriptural/source fidelity, doctrinal tradition, pastoral context, theological coherence, sensitivity to community. | Defend interpretation from primary texts; respond to a pastoral case orally. | Potentially high where spiritual discernment or pastoral authorship is itself part of vocation. |
| **Law** | Research summaries, drafts, issue spotting, contractual language. | Authentic precedent, jurisdiction, procedural posture, factual fit, adverse authority, professional duties. | Source audit plus live response to a modified hypothetical. | High in procedural/accountability dimensions; the lawyer remains answerable for professional judgment. |
| **Engineering and software** | Code, designs, calculations, test generation, documentation. | Requirements, invariants, safety margins, architecture, testing, debugging, failure modes. | Seeded defect or incident response; explain why tests establish—or fail to establish—safety. | Lower for typing/code production; high for safety, verification, and signoff. DORA's results strongly support surrounding verification systems. |
| **Literature and writing** | Draft prose, stylistic transformations, analysis, editing. | Close reading, textual evidence, voice, interpretive judgment, intentionality. | Live close reading; revise an AI interpretation using the text. | Often high where developing one's own voice is an explicit educational objective. |
| **Science** | Literature synthesis, code, hypotheses, protocols, analyses, drafts. | Data provenance, reproducibility, statistics, causal inference, methodological validity, replication. | Diagnose a flawed analysis or experimental design; reproduce a result from data. | Very high for methodological integrity even where production is automated. OpenAI's own research workflow still retains humans for priority-setting and judgment. |

The software analogy is therefore most useful as a **structural** analogy, not a literal one. Other disciplines should copy software's separation of generation and verification, its acceptance tests, small-batch feedback, provenance, monitoring, and post-failure learning. They should not assume that prose, law, theology, or philosophy can be “unit tested” as objectively as executable software.

## Ethics, Equity, and Institutional Policy

An AI-native philosophy of education cannot be based solely on productivity. It changes the distribution of opportunity, authority, responsibility, and dependence.

**Equity.** AI may democratize access to tutoring, explanation, editing, translation, programming assistance, and intellectual production. But an educational institution that makes advanced AI central to assessment while leaving students to purchase unequal access creates a new class divide. Globally, ITU estimated in 2025 that 2.2 billion people remained offline, disproportionately in low- and middle-income countries, while affordability and quality gaps persisted even where coverage existed. OECD's PISA 2025 findings similarly report that school opportunities to develop AI literacy were more common among socioeconomically advantaged students. UNESCO therefore frames AI education explicitly around inclusion, equity, human agency, and avoiding widened technological divides.

An institution that requires AI should consequently provide a baseline institutional tool, accessible alternatives, accessibility support, and training rather than treating premium model access as a private student responsibility.

**Accountability.** Delegating an act is not the same as delegating responsibility for it. A student who submits an AI-assisted argument should be able to stand behind its claims. An engineer approving generated code should understand the evidence for its safety. A lawyer cannot convert a fabricated citation into a defensible submission by saying that a model generated it. This suggests an educational norm of **ownership rather than authorship purity**: the central question becomes whether the person legitimately takes responsibility for what is submitted, including verification appropriate to its stakes.

**Competence and dependency.** Education should avoid producing graduates who are excellent only while one proprietary model behaves as expected. At the same time, demanding full pre-AI competence in every automated subtask would waste instructional time. The objective is **graceful degradation**: a graduate need not reproduce an entire AI system's output manually, but should recognize failure, continue critical functions, and know when to stop or escalate.

**Verification must scale with stakes.** A spelling suggestion requires almost no governance; a bridge design, medical interpretation, legal filing, or high-consequence scientific claim requires much more. Software's evolving conditional-trust norm reflects this intuitively: Stack Overflow's 2026 respondents were much more willing to trust outputs they could easily verify than important decisions. Educational AI policies should teach that distinction rather than establishing one rule for all AI use.

**Privacy and provenance.** AI-native assignments may produce detailed interaction logs useful for assessment, but those logs can contain personal data, intellectual exploration, unpublished research, or sensitive client-like scenarios. Institutions should therefore collect only what is necessary for pedagogical purposes and distinguish learning analytics from surveillance.

**Regulatory literacy is becoming part of professional literacy.** In the European Union, the AI Act entered into force on August 1, 2024, became generally applicable on August 2, 2026 subject to staged exceptions, and its AI-literacy obligations began applying on February 2, 2025. Certain high-risk education-related requirements have later application dates. Even outside the EU, this illustrates why professional education increasingly has to include not only “how to prompt” but also when AI use carries governance obligations.

### Institutional policy

The appropriate institutional response is not a universal green light or red light. Policies should distinguish **learning objectives**.

| Institutional policy question | Recommended principle |
|---|---|
| Should students be allowed to use AI? | Default toward permitted, disclosed use where AI reflects authentic professional practice; restrict it only where an assignment deliberately measures an unaided competency. |
| Should every course require AI? | No. Require AI where it contributes to the course's competencies; preserve AI-free spaces where process is intrinsically or constitutively important. |
| What should disclosure accomplish? | Enable accountability and provenance, not ritual confession. Disclosure should identify consequential AI roles and verification performed. |
| How should assessment change? | Stop treating polished take-home artifacts alone as strong evidence of individual competence. Add oral defense, error diagnosis, unseen transfer, provenance checks, and selected restricted-tool assessments. |
| What should faculty learn? | Tool capabilities and failure modes, task redesign, verification design, privacy, source checking, and methods for distinguishing product performance from student learning. UNESCO's framework similarly emphasizes AI competency rather than mere tool access. |
| How should access be handled? | Provide institutionally supported baseline access rather than allowing ability to pay for stronger models to become an unacknowledged assessment variable. |
| What should institutions measure? | Learning retention, transfer, verification ability, calibration, productivity, equity, and failure rates—not simply adoption counts or assignment grades. |
| How often should policy be revised? | Regularly, because model capabilities and professional workflows are changing faster than conventional curriculum cycles. DORA's 2024–25 shift itself shows rapid changes in observed relationships between AI and software outcomes. |

This direction is already consistent with institutional movement away from denial. UNESCO's 2025 survey of 400 respondents affiliated with UNESCO Chairs and UNITWIN Networks across 90 countries found that nearly two-thirds reported their institutions already had or were developing AI guidance, and nine in ten respondents said they personally used AI professionally. The sample is specialized and not globally representative, but it demonstrates that AI governance has become an institutional rather than hypothetical question.

The strongest policy principle is therefore:

> **Do not regulate AI according to nostalgia for a previous production process. Regulate it according to the capability an assignment is intended to develop or certify.**

If the capability is “write coherent prose without assistance,” AI should be restricted.

If the capability is “produce a publishable policy analysis using contemporary research tools,” forbidding AI may make the assessment less authentic.

If the capability is “evaluate arguments,” the student should perhaps receive both human- and AI-generated arguments and be judged principally on diagnosis.

The assignment's tool rule should follow from the competency, not the other way around.

## Research Agenda and Conclusion

The most important conclusion of this review is not that manual-first education has been disproved. It is almost the opposite: we have not yet run the experiment that would allow us to know whether it is necessary.

Most current evidence evaluates one of three things:

- experienced workers adding AI to skills they already possess;
- students using AI inside conventional curricula;
- short interventions measuring immediate performance or near-term learning.

What we need to know is different:

> Can a generation educated with powerful AI from the beginning become as capable of independent verification, conceptual reasoning, error detection, and professional accountability as a generation that first learned to produce the work manually?

The current evidence base cannot answer that. Anthropic's 2026 study comes unusually close, but its participants already had at least a year of regular Python experience, and the outcome was measured immediately after a short unfamiliar-library task. The researchers themselves identify long-term skill formation as unresolved. Existing industry surveys are even less capable of answering it because their respondents overwhelmingly come from the transitional generation whose foundational skills predate today's agentic systems. Stack Overflow's adoption data describe current practice, not a randomized educational pathway.

### Proposed experiment: AI-first versus manual-first education

A serious test should be longitudinal, randomized, multi-site, and competency-specific, ideally replicated across several disciplines.

| Design element | Proposal |
|---|---|
| Participants | Genuine beginners with minimal prior substantive training in the target discipline. Prior AI and domain experience measured before randomization. |
| Disciplines | Begin with programming because outputs are highly testable, then replicate in philosophy/writing, engineering, and a high-stakes interpretive field such as law. |
| Manual-first arm | Initial foundational period prohibits generative AI for core production; AI is introduced after predefined competencies are reached. |
| AI-first arm | AI available from the first lesson with normal production delegation permitted; students receive instruction in tool use and source verification. |
| Verification-first hybrid arm | AI available from day one, but pedagogy requires prediction, explanations, testing, error diagnosis, provenance, and selected unaided reconstruction. AI is deliberately configured or instructed to scaffold rather than automatically answer some tasks. |
| Instructional control | Same curricular concepts, total instructional time, learning objectives, and assessment schedule. Instructor effects modeled or balanced across groups. |
| Primary competence outcome | Ability to identify and correct plausible, domain-relevant errors in AI-produced work without receiving the answer from another AI. |
| Other outcomes | Conceptual knowledge; AI-assisted artifact quality; time to completion; unaided transfer; delayed retention; source verification; debugging/error localization; oral defense; confidence calibration; ability to formulate acceptance tests; ability to recover after AI removal. |
| Delayed tests | Assess immediately, then again after meaningful delays such as the following term and later program stages. The central issue is durable expertise, not next-day recall. |
| Adversarial tests | Give students polished AI outputs containing seeded subtle errors, invented evidence, misleading assumptions, or locally correct but globally defective solutions. |
| Professional simulation | Give all groups the same frontier AI and realistic time constraint after foundational training; measure quality × speed × verification reliability rather than speed alone. |
| Analysis | Preregister primary outcomes and hypotheses; use intention-to-treat analysis and models accounting for site/instructor; separately test superiority in AI-assisted professional performance and non-inferiority in independent verification. The non-inferiority margin should be set before seeing results. |
| Model drift | Record exact models and settings. Where practical, maintain a frozen-model benchmark alongside a current-model condition so improvement in AI capability is not mistaken for improvement in pedagogy. |
| Ethics | No group should be permanently denied professionally relevant tools. After the experimentally necessary comparison period, all participants receive AI training, while delayed outcomes continue to be measured. |

The critical test would not be whether the AI-first students produce better work during training. Based on the existing literature, they very well might.

The critical test is whether, after several years, they can detect a convincing machine-generated mistake that they have never seen before.

If AI-first students equal manual-first students on that measure while becoming substantially better AI orchestrators, the traditional case for prolonged manual-first education becomes weak.

If they produce better artifacts but systematically fail to detect subtle errors, then manual or otherwise effortful foundational practice retains an instrumental justification.

If the verification-first group preserves understanding while outperforming both groups in AI-assisted production, the debate between “traditional fundamentals” and “AI from day one” will have been exposed as a false dichotomy.

## Actionable recommendations

For educators, the strongest position supported by evidence available through October 2026 is not to wait for AI to disappear, not to surrender education to AI, and not to preserve manual methods merely because today's experts happened to learn through them.

Students should begin learning with AI early enough that AI operation does not become an afterthought. UNESCO already treats AI understanding, application, creation, ethics, and critical judgment as educational competencies in their own right. But educators should deliberately prevent production competence from being mistaken for learner competence. The evidence from mathematics shows that unrestricted AI assistance can increase visible performance while decreasing unassisted learning; the college-physics evidence shows that carefully constructed AI tutoring can instead produce large learning gains.

Manual practice should therefore become selective rather than ceremonial. Keep it wherever removing AI reveals a mental model the learner will need for verification, recovery, or professional responsibility. Remove it where it merely reproduces a mechanical burden that no longer contributes meaningful competence.

Assessment should shift aggressively toward error detection, explanation, oral defense, provenance, novel transfer, and calibration. Students should routinely be handed excellent-looking but defective AI work and asked to find what is wrong. They should learn that the hardest professional skill is increasingly not generating the first answer but knowing when the answer should not be trusted.

Institutions should provide equitable access to AI where they expect students to use it, train teachers in AI-era assessment, protect student privacy, and distinguish high- from low-stakes uses. UNESCO's equity concerns, ITU's continuing connectivity divide, and OECD's evidence of unequal access to AI-literacy opportunities make a purely “bring your own AI” educational model difficult to defend.

And researchers should treat the bootstrapping problem as one of the most important unanswered questions in contemporary education.

The philosophical conclusion can therefore be expressed in a compact form:

> Process matters differently depending on the relationship. For oneself, it may be intrinsically valuable. For independent recipients, output value usually dominates, subject to ethics, provenance, and safety. For learners under one's supervision, process matters because it shapes the person who will eventually operate without that supervision. But because the purpose of education is ultimately autonomy, no educational process deserves permanent preservation merely because it once produced competent adults.

AI changes the economic value of production. It does not eliminate the need for competence. It changes where competence has to reside.

The central task of AI-era education is therefore neither to preserve human labor nor to maximize machine output. It is to produce people who can enter an increasingly automated world and still answer four questions:

- What are we trying to accomplish?
- How will we know whether the result is actually good?
- What could be wrong even when it looks right?
- Who is prepared to take responsibility for the answer?

Software engineering's early experience suggests that this is where the human role is moving. DORA finds that AI amplifies the quality of the system surrounding it; Stack Overflow developers trust AI most when they can verify it; OpenAI's agentic workflows still require human steering; and Anthropic's skill-formation experiment warns that careless delegation can impair the very debugging ability needed for oversight.

The appropriate educational response is therefore not manual-first forever and not automation without formation.

It is AI from early on, understanding throughout, verification by design, and independence as the end goal.
