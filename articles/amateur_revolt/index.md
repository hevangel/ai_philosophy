# The Amateur Revolt: Artificial Intelligence, Academic Gatekeeping, and the Future of Intellectual Authority

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence threatens to transform intellectual professions, but its effects are unlikely to be uniform. In engineering, programming, and other fields with relatively measurable outcomes, AI adoption is driven by economic competition. When an AI system produces better results at lower cost, professional resistance becomes increasingly difficult to sustain. In philosophy, literature, and the humanities, however, intellectual quality is less easily reduced to objective measurements, and professional institutions retain considerable power to determine which contributions deserve recognition.

This difference may allow the humanities to resist AI longer than technical professions. Yet such resistance could produce an unintended consequence. Engineers, programmers, scientists, and other professionals who become proficient in AI through their ordinary employment may use the same technology to pursue intellectual interests outside their professions. These AI-assisted amateurs, many of whom have no intention of becoming academics, could challenge established scholars on their own intellectual territory.

The resulting conflict would not simply be between human beings and machines. It could become a struggle between established intellectual elites and a growing population of AI-empowered outsiders. Moreover, because these outsiders voluntarily adopt AI for their intellectual activities, they constitute a self-selected population predisposed toward its acceptance. Their emergence may alter the balance of opinion within intellectual communities, particularly when combined with younger academics' incentives to embrace technologies likely to shape their future careers.

This essay advances three connected arguments. First, differences in economic incentives and institutional structures help explain why technical and humanities professions may adopt AI at different rates. Second, AI-assisted amateurs could challenge the intellectual authority of established academics without competing for their professional positions. Third, the ultimate consequence may be neither an amateur victory nor the preservation of academic authority, but the emergence of AI itself as a dominant evaluator of intellectual work.

The central thesis is that **AI may democratize intellectual production before it democratizes intellectual authority, and that the resulting struggle over legitimacy may ultimately transform the nature of intellectual authority itself.**

## I. Objective Performance and the Economics of AI Adoption

The distinction between technical professions and the humanities begins with the relative availability of external standards of success.

Consider semiconductor engineering. A chip design can be evaluated according to power consumption, performance, silicon area, manufacturing yield, reliability, and other measurable characteristics. These criteria do not eliminate disagreement about engineering quality, since specifications and trade-offs still require judgment. Nevertheless, they provide relatively independent tests by which competing designs can be compared.

Suppose an AI-assisted engineer produces a chip design that achieves substantially higher performance while consuming less power than one developed through conventional methods. If the result is independently verified, professional objections to AI have limited practical force. An engineering organization that refuses the improved design merely because AI contributed to it risks losing its competitive position.

Programming follows a similar pattern. Correctness, execution speed, reliability, security, and maintenance costs are not perfectly objective in every circumstance, but software can frequently be tested against specifications and observed performance requirements.

Finance is somewhat different because investment performance is uncertain and short-term success may reflect luck rather than superior skill. Nevertheless, sustained risk-adjusted returns and operational efficiency provide economically consequential evidence. An institution that consistently ignores profitable technological improvements faces competitive pressure.

The humanities operate under different conditions. Philosophical arguments can be assessed for logical validity, consistency, and evidential support, but their originality, importance, and explanatory depth are more contestable. Literary works can be evaluated for coherence, style, and artistic achievement, yet reasonable critics can disagree profoundly about their significance.

Consequently, an AI-generated philosophical essay cannot establish its superiority in quite the same manner as a demonstrably more efficient chip design.

An academic journal may reject an AI-assisted essay despite its intellectual merits, arguing that genuine philosophy requires human understanding, intellectual responsibility, or authentic authorship. Whether these objections are justified is itself a philosophical question. The important point is that the profession possesses institutional mechanisms for enforcing its preferred answer.

There is already empirical evidence that assessments of creative quality can be influenced by beliefs about authorship. In a 2024 study published in *Scientific Reports*, Brian Porter and Edouard Machery found that non-expert readers frequently preferred AI-generated poetry to poems written by established human poets. Yet evaluations became less favorable when readers were told that the poems were AI-generated, regardless of their actual origins (Porter and Machery, 2024).

This does not establish that AI poetry is objectively superior to human poetry, or that expert literary judgment is merely prejudice. It does demonstrate that beliefs about authorship can influence aesthetic evaluation independently of a work's textual qualities.

The difference between technical and humanities professions is therefore not that one possesses objective quality while the other possesses none. Rather, **technical professions often face external performance tests capable of overriding professional opinion, whereas humanities institutions exercise greater influence over the standards and procedures through which intellectual achievements receive recognition.**

This distinction creates different incentives for adapting to AI.

## II. Diminishing Returns and the Persistence of Human Expertise

The economic pressure to adopt AI does not imply that human engineers and programmers must disappear.

Technological substitution depends on comparative costs, not simply comparative abilities.

Imagine that an AI system can perform 95 percent of a particular class of engineering tasks at a lower cost than human workers. Automating the remaining 5 percent might require disproportionate investments in model capability, computational resources, specialized verification, or reliability.

Under such conditions, full automation may be economically inferior to a hybrid arrangement in which AI performs most tasks while humans handle exceptional cases, ambiguous requirements, and final accountability.

The precise percentages are hypothetical. The general principle is that the marginal cost of further automation must be compared with the cost of retaining human participation.

If human oversight remains economical, the optimal solution may be collaboration rather than replacement.

An engineer who understands both engineering principles and the effective use of AI can therefore remain valuable even when AI performs much of the routine intellectual labor.

This generates a powerful incentive for technical professionals to become proficient AI users. Their employment increasingly rewards the ability to formulate problems, evaluate machine-generated solutions, detect errors, and integrate AI into complicated workflows.

Crucially, these skills need not remain confined to their original professions.

Research, argument construction, literature review, hypothesis generation, and critical evaluation are activities shared by engineering and philosophy. Someone who learns to collaborate effectively with AI in semiconductor design may transfer part of that expertise into philosophical inquiry.

The significance of this transfer becomes apparent when the professional has intellectual interests outside engineering.

## III. The Emergence of AI-Assisted Amateur Intellectuals

Consider a semiconductor engineer with a full-time career and a serious amateur interest in philosophy.

The engineer possesses technical training, experience in formal reasoning, familiarity with research methods, and perhaps substantial philosophical reading. Nevertheless, professional responsibilities prevent the individual from pursuing a conventional academic career in philosophy.

Historically, this amateur faced considerable obstacles. Producing a rigorous philosophical paper required extensive reading, knowledge of specialized literature, familiarity with professional conventions, and the ability to organize sophisticated arguments. These activities demanded resources that full-time academics were better positioned to possess.

Advanced AI could substantially reduce some of these barriers.

The engineer could use AI to survey unfamiliar philosophical traditions, reconstruct arguments, identify objections, locate relevant sources, clarify conceptual distinctions, and refine written exposition. Such assistance would not automatically supply philosophical judgment, but it could greatly reduce the time required to produce work that deserves serious consideration.

The crucial observation is that the engineer has no reason to leave engineering to become a philosophy professor.

Philosophy is a hobby pursued for intellectual satisfaction. The engineer does not require tenure, university funding, an academic appointment, or the approval of a hiring committee.

Yet the engineer can participate in philosophical disputes.

Suppose an established professor publishes an essay arguing that AI cannot contribute meaningfully to philosophical reasoning. The engineer might spend a weekend examining the argument with AI assistance, identifying weaknesses, constructing counterexamples, and publishing a detailed critique.

On Monday, the engineer returns to chip design.

The professor, meanwhile, must respond to the criticism as a challenge to an important part of their professional expertise.

This creates an asymmetry of intellectual competition. The amateur's economic livelihood is independent of philosophical recognition, while the professional academic's career partly depends upon maintaining intellectual credibility.

The amateur has little professional status to lose and potentially considerable intellectual satisfaction to gain.

Such independence does not guarantee superior reasoning. An amateur may misunderstand the relevant literature or mistake a familiar objection for an original discovery. Nevertheless, the individual's outsider status no longer necessarily prevents meaningful participation.

More importantly, the potential population of such outsiders is enormous.

Engineers, physicians, lawyers, accountants, scientists, businesspeople, and retirees may all possess substantial intellectual interests beyond their careers. If AI lowers the cost of participation, even a relatively small percentage of these individuals could constitute a significant new population of intellectual contributors.

The resulting transformation would not require amateurs to take academic jobs. It would require only that some produce arguments that other people find worth examining.

**The most serious threat to established intellectual authority may therefore come not from AI replacing academics, but from AI enabling outsiders to compete intellectually without becoming academics.**

## IV. Bourdieu, Foucault, and the Social Construction of Intellectual Authority

Pierre Bourdieu's sociology offers a framework for understanding the institutional consequences of this transformation.

In his analysis of cultural production, Bourdieu describes intellectual and artistic fields as structured spaces in which participants compete for distinctive forms of capital. Beyond financial resources, these include cultural competence, reputation, prestige, and the power to confer legitimacy (Bourdieu, 1983).

A university professorship, a prestigious journal publication, and recognition by influential scholars all contribute to symbolic capital.

Such recognition is not necessarily undeserved. Professional training and peer evaluation can identify genuine expertise. Nevertheless, the institutions responsible for recognizing intellectual quality also influence the distribution of intellectual prestige.

This creates a potential conflict of interest.

An academic who has spent decades acquiring specialist credentials may confront an outsider capable of producing comparable arguments with AI assistance. If the work is intellectually serious, rejecting it solely because the author lacks traditional credentials or used AI risks protecting an established hierarchy rather than preserving intellectual quality.

Bourdieu's framework suggests that the dispute may concern not merely what counts as good philosophy, but who possesses the authority to determine that question.

Michel Foucault's analysis of power and knowledge extends this argument.

Foucault emphasized that societies establish procedures, institutions, and professional roles through which certain statements become recognized as authoritative. These arrangements constitute what he called regimes of truth (Foucault, 1980).

From this perspective, academic journals, universities, and professional associations do more than transmit knowledge. They participate in defining the conditions under which knowledge claims are accepted.

Restrictions on AI-assisted intellectual production can therefore be examined as exercises of institutional power.

This does not establish that the restrictions are illegitimate. Academic standards can serve valuable epistemic purposes, including protecting research integrity and ensuring that authors accept responsibility for their claims.

The Foucauldian question is more specific: to what extent do these restrictions improve intellectual inquiry, and to what extent do they preserve existing structures of authority?

AI-assisted amateurs could challenge those structures by establishing alternative routes through which arguments reach audiences.

A serious philosophical critique published independently may attract attention even if its author has never attended graduate school in philosophy. A particularly effective public rebuttal may damage the credibility of an established academic without appearing in a traditional journal.

Ridicule may intensify this process. A complicated argument can lose persuasive force when an outsider exposes a straightforward contradiction or neglected counterexample in language accessible to ordinary readers.

However, ridicule is not equivalent to philosophical refutation. A successful critique must withstand informed scrutiny. Otherwise, the contest merely substitutes popularity for expertise.

The strongest amateur challenge is one that combines accessibility with genuine intellectual rigor.

The deeper threat is therefore not the elimination of academic institutions, but the possible separation of institutional status from public intellectual authority.

## V. Self-Selection and the Formation of a Pro-AI Intellectual Coalition

The rise of AI-assisted amateurs has another consequence that is easily overlooked: it changes the composition of the population participating in debates about AI.

Established academic communities contain both supporters and opponents of artificial intelligence. Their positions are shaped by philosophical commitments, research interests, professional experience, and institutional incentives.

AI-assisted amateurs, however, enter intellectual discussions through a different selection mechanism.

A person who voluntarily uses AI to write philosophical essays has already accepted that AI can contribute something valuable to the activity. Although that person may support restrictions on particular applications, they are unlikely to endorse the categorical exclusion of the very practices they find useful.

The resulting population is disproportionately favorable toward AI-assisted intellectual work.

This is a selection effect rather than necessarily a process of ideological persuasion.

AI does not need to convince every existing anti-AI academic to change their opinion. It can enable additional participants, many already inclined toward its acceptance, to enter intellectual debates.

As AI improves, the cost of entry may decline further, increasing the population capable of making such contributions.

These amateurs can become natural allies of pro-AI academics.

The relationship is potentially complementary. Established academics possess specialized knowledge, professional credibility, and access to institutional debates. Outside contributors bring professional independence, practical experience, AI expertise, and potentially substantial numbers.

Together, they could challenge restrictive practices from both inside and outside academic institutions.

This coalition need not be formally organized. An engineer who publishes a strong rebuttal to an anti-AI argument may indirectly strengthen the position of an academic who favors responsible AI adoption. Likewise, pro-AI scholars may lend credibility to worthwhile contributions originating outside universities.

Nevertheless, self-selection introduces its own intellectual danger.

AI-assisted amateurs may become predisposed not merely to use AI, but to defend it against reasonable criticism. They may confuse an argument's favorable implications for AI with its philosophical soundness.

The same technology that enables amateurs to scrutinize academic elites could also enable them to generate large quantities of motivated reasoning.

Consequently, the emergence of a pro-AI outsider constituency should be understood as a change in the balance of intellectual participation, not as proof that the pro-AI position is correct.

The number of people supporting an argument does not establish its truth.

## VI. Generational Incentives and Academic Adaptation

The incentives facing younger academics differ from those confronting established scholars.

A senior professor may have invested decades in particular methods of research, authorship, and scholarly evaluation. These practices may form an important part of the professor's professional identity.

A younger academic anticipates a much longer future career. If AI continues improving substantially, the young scholar must consider the possibility that AI-assisted research will become increasingly important over the coming decades.

From a strategic perspective, an academic without strong prior convictions about AI has reason to acquire AI capabilities even when existing institutional rules restrict their use.

This does not require violating academic integrity standards.

A researcher can comply with current authorship and disclosure requirements while developing permitted AI skills and supporting reasonable institutional experimentation.

The advantage lies in preserving future opportunities.

If AI becomes central to philosophical research, early familiarity may be beneficial. If some applications prove inappropriate, traditional scholarly skills remain useful. A person who understands both conventional research and AI-assisted methods retains flexibility across different possible futures.

Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions provides a relevant analogy.

Kuhn argued that scientific communities organize their work around paradigms that establish accepted problems, methods, and standards of successful inquiry (Kuhn, 1962). Changes in these arrangements involve more than the acceptance of individual propositions; they can transform professional expectations about legitimate research.

The emergence of AI-assisted scholarship could produce a comparable, though not identical, change in academic practices.

However, Kuhn's framework should not be treated as establishing an inevitable generational revolution. Scholars can change their minds, younger researchers can defend traditional norms, and institutional adaptation may proceed gradually.

Indeed, a young academic might rationally learn AI while remaining publicly undecided about controversial authorship policies. Acquiring a technological capability is not equivalent to endorsing every philosophical claim made in its defense.

Nevertheless, if improving AI increases the professional rewards of adoption, younger scholars may have stronger incentives to remain open to its use.

A coalition of AI-capable academics and independent amateurs could therefore emerge without requiring the wholesale replacement of older scholars.

The transformation would arise from changing incentives and intellectual practices rather than simply from generational turnover.

## VII. The Strongest Counterarguments

The amateur-revolt thesis faces several substantial objections. These deserve attention because they remain relevant even if AI continues improving.

### The scarcity of intellectual judgment

The first objection concerns the distinction between producing intellectual content and evaluating it.

If AI makes philosophical writing inexpensive, an enormous quantity of plausible scholarship could enter circulation.

Yet human attention remains limited.

Readers cannot examine every essay, assess every argument, or verify every claim. As intellectual production becomes more abundant, the ability to identify genuinely worthwhile work may become more valuable.

Established universities, journals, and critics already perform this certification function.

Paradoxically, AI might increase the importance of academic gatekeeping by making intellectual production too abundant for audiences to evaluate independently.

The scarce resource would shift from the production of philosophical arguments to the trustworthy recognition of intellectual quality.

This directly challenges the assumption that democratizing intellectual production necessarily democratizes intellectual authority.

### Academics can adopt AI themselves

A second objection is that professional philosophers need not remain technologically disadvantaged.

Suppose a philosophy professor possesses extensive disciplinary expertise while an engineer possesses greater experience with AI systems.

Initially, these different skills might create competitive advantages for each participant.

But if AI becomes increasingly accessible and intuitive, the professor may rapidly acquire sufficient proficiency to use it effectively.

At that point, the competition becomes one between an AI-assisted amateur and an AI-assisted professional.

The professor may retain advantages in identifying significant philosophical questions, recognizing subtle conceptual problems, and understanding the history of a debate.

The amateur's AI proficiency might no longer compensate for differences in disciplinary judgment.

The original thesis therefore depends partly on the persistence of an adoption gap between institutions and outsiders. Continued AI improvement alone does not guarantee that such a gap will remain.

### Human authorship may possess independent value

A third objection concerns the purpose of intellectual and artistic activity.

A person might value a philosophical work not merely as a collection of arguments, but as an expression of another human being's intellectual life.

Consider professional chess. Computers have surpassed the strongest human players, yet human competition remains meaningful and popular. The fact that a machine can play better chess does not eliminate the value people attach to human accomplishment.

Something similar may apply to literature and philosophy.

An AI might generate a brilliant philosophical argument, but a reader may still value knowing what a particular human thinker believes and whether that thinker can personally defend the position.

The distinction can be illustrated by the difference between hiking up a mountain and taking a cable car to the summit.

If one's goal is to enjoy the scenery, taking the cable car need not diminish the experience. If one's goal is the physical activity of climbing, the cable car cannot provide the same good.

Likewise, if the purpose of reading philosophy is to encounter strong arguments, AI assistance may be entirely appropriate. If the purpose includes understanding the intellectual achievement and commitments of a particular human author, the process of authorship may matter.

The relevant question is not whether difficulty is intrinsically valuable. It is whether human participation constitutes part of the good being sought.

Thus, some restrictions on AI-created work may reflect legitimate differences in purpose rather than mere professional protectionism.

### AI-assisted amateurs may produce homogeneous arguments

A fourth objection concerns originality.

An experiment by Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser found that generative AI assistance improved evaluations of individual creative writing, particularly among less-creative participants. However, AI-assisted stories also became more similar to one another (Doshi and Hauser, 2024).

This finding does not prove that future AI will necessarily homogenize philosophy. Nevertheless, it demonstrates a possible tension between individual productivity and collective intellectual diversity.

If thousands of amateurs consult similar AI systems trained on overlapping philosophical literature, they may produce articulate variations of familiar criticisms.

An established professor might encounter a large volume of objections without confronting much genuinely new philosophy.

In such circumstances, the professional's experience distinguishing important contributions from routine arguments may become more valuable.

The decisive issue is therefore not how many sophisticated essays amateurs can produce, but how many genuinely illuminating ideas they contribute.

### Public attention is not intellectual authority

Finally, the ability to embarrass an academic does not necessarily establish a replacement source of authority.

An amateur may produce an effective critique of a professor's argument. That achievement could be intellectually valuable without demonstrating that the amateur is more knowledgeable across the wider discipline.

Established expertise is often justified by sustained competence across many problems rather than success in a single debate.

For amateurs to acquire durable intellectual authority, they must demonstrate repeated reliability, including a willingness to correct their own errors.

Their financial independence can protect their intellectual freedom, but it does not substitute for intellectual responsibility.

Taken together, these objections suggest that AI-assisted amateurs may become important participants in philosophy without necessarily displacing professional philosophers.

The result could be a more competitive intellectual environment rather than the destruction of academic expertise.

## VIII. The Third Possibility: AI Becomes the Intellectual Judge

The preceding discussion assumes that intellectual authority remains primarily contested among human beings.

However, continued AI improvement raises a more radical possibility.

What happens if AI becomes not only an instrument for producing philosophical arguments, but also a widely trusted instrument for evaluating them?

Imagine that readers routinely submit competing philosophical essays to advanced AI systems and ask for analyses of their logical structure, factual assumptions, originality, and responses to objections.

Under these circumstances, an academic credential may become less important as a shortcut for determining intellectual quality.

A reader need not automatically trust a professor over an engineer. Both arguments can be subjected to systematic examination.

This development would favor the legitimacy of AI-assisted inquiry. A society that routinely relies on AI to evaluate philosophy has already accepted a substantial role for AI in philosophical judgment.

But it would not necessarily favor amateurs over professors.

If an academic produces the stronger argument, an effective AI evaluator may recognize it. If the amateur produces the stronger argument, the evaluator may recognize that instead.

The new institution of intellectual judgment would be less dependent on traditional professional membership, but it would not guarantee any particular group's success.

Moreover, research into AI-based evaluation already identifies complications. Zheng and colleagues, in their work on language models as judges, documented biases associated with the position, verbosity, and origin of evaluated responses (Zheng et al., 2023).

More capable AI could reduce some of these problems. Yet better reasoning does not automatically resolve every question about the standards by which reasoning should be evaluated.

A philosophical argument may be logically valid while depending on controversial premises. Competing ethical positions may differ because their proponents assign different priorities to freedom, equality, happiness, autonomy, or justice.

An AI can illuminate these disagreements without necessarily establishing a unique correct ordering of ultimate values.

The philosophical problem therefore shifts from whether AI can evaluate arguments to who determines the criteria governing that evaluation.

This introduces a new possibility: **intellectual authority may migrate from universities to the institutions controlling AI evaluation systems.**

Instead of journal editors deciding which arguments deserve attention, influential AI platforms may increasingly shape which arguments readers regard as persuasive, original, or significant.

The old gatekeepers might lose influence without producing a more democratic intellectual culture.

Bourdieu's symbolic capital would not disappear. It might become concentrated in the organizations whose systems certify intellectual quality.

Foucault's problem of power and knowledge would likewise survive. The relevant institutions would change, but the political question of who establishes authoritative procedures for recognizing truth would remain.

## IX. From Amateur Liberation to AI Dependence

There is a further irony.

The same AI capabilities that allow amateurs to challenge professional philosophers might eventually make the amateurs themselves less necessary.

Initially, a philosophically interested engineer benefits from AI's ability to assist with reading, research, argument construction, and writing.

But as AI becomes more capable, readers may increasingly prefer to ask an AI system directly about philosophical questions rather than read essays produced by either the engineer or the professor.

If an AI can produce an argument, generate objections to it, respond to those objections, and compare alternative positions, the human contributor's role in delivering such analysis may diminish.

The amateur revolution could therefore represent a transitional phase in a broader technological transformation.

During the first stage, AI reduces the disadvantages of outsiders and enables them to challenge established intellectual institutions.

During the second, AI becomes a common evaluator of competing human contributions.

During the third, AI increasingly becomes the immediate source of philosophical analysis for ordinary readers.

This does not establish that human philosophy becomes meaningless.

Human beings may continue to value intellectual exploration, personal reflection, conversation, and the experience of forming their own judgments. They may also possess forms of practical experience and personal commitment that are integral to certain philosophical activities.

But it does mean that the victory of AI-assisted amateurs over academic gatekeeping would not necessarily produce lasting amateur authority.

The amateurs might succeed in weakening the intellectual hierarchy only to discover that the technology enabling their success has also weakened the significance of their own intellectual position.

The original contest between amateur and professional could thus be superseded by a more fundamental question about the relationship between human judgment and machine intelligence.

## X. What Would Count as a Genuine Intellectual Revolution?

The distinction between producing arguments and possessing authority is central to evaluating these possible futures.

Three outcomes deserve consideration.

In the first, AI democratizes intellectual authority. Powerful AI tools are widely available, independent evaluators can be compared, and audiences become less dependent on traditional credentials. Academics remain important, but their arguments compete more openly with contributions from outsiders.

In the second, AI strengthens existing institutions. Intellectual production becomes abundant, but trustworthy certification becomes increasingly scarce. Universities and established scholarly organizations adopt AI themselves and preserve their authority by providing reliable evaluation.

In the third, AI transforms intellectual authority. Individuals and institutions rely increasingly on AI systems not merely to assist thought but to determine which arguments deserve attention. Traditional academic status becomes less decisive, yet authority may concentrate in the organizations governing these systems.

All three outcomes are consistent with substantial improvements in AI capabilities.

Technological progress alone cannot determine which occurs. Institutional design, public trust, access to computational resources, professional incentives, and cultural preferences all influence the distribution of intellectual authority.

A genuinely democratic outcome would require more than placing powerful AI in the hands of amateurs.

It would require that intellectual judgments remain open to criticism, that the reasons supporting those judgments be accessible, and that no institution—academic or technological—possess an unquestionable monopoly over intellectual legitimacy.

The normative principle is straightforward: the authority of an argument should depend principally upon its merits, while the authority of those evaluating it should remain accountable to reasoned scrutiny.

This principle does not abolish expertise. It distinguishes justified expertise from institutional privilege.

Nor does it require treating AI-generated and human-generated work as identical in every context. Authorship can be relevant when the purpose of an activity makes it relevant. But exclusion based solely on the social or technological origin of a contribution requires justification whenever that contribution otherwise satisfies the relevant intellectual standards.

The appropriate goal is therefore neither the victory of amateurs over professionals nor the victory of AI over human beings.

It is an intellectual environment in which arguments can be examined without excessive dependence on inherited authority, while preserving the institutions and practices necessary for reliable judgment.

## Conclusion

Artificial intelligence may transform the humanities through a different mechanism from the one operating in engineering and programming.

In technical professions, relatively measurable outcomes and commercial competition create strong incentives for AI adoption. Human workers may remain economically valuable, especially where complete automation is disproportionately expensive, but their professional roles increasingly require effective collaboration with AI.

In the humanities, institutional control over evaluation and recognition can delay adoption. Yet that very delay may create opportunities for outsiders who acquire AI expertise elsewhere.

Professionals with established careers and retirees pursuing intellectual interests could use AI to participate in philosophy, literary criticism, and other scholarly debates without entering academic employment. Their financial independence would reduce the importance of institutional approval, while their self-selection into AI-assisted intellectual activity would create a constituency favoring its legitimacy.

Younger academics, facing different long-term incentives from established scholars, may find it advantageous to acquire AI capabilities and remain open to changing professional practices. Together, AI-assisted amateurs and receptive academics could challenge existing systems of intellectual recognition.

Nevertheless, the production of sophisticated arguments does not automatically confer authority. Expertise, originality, trust, sustained intellectual judgment, and the allocation of attention remain consequential. Academia may adapt successfully, and the abundance of AI-assisted intellectual content may even increase the value of credible evaluation.

Most significantly, AI itself may eventually become a principal evaluator of intellectual work. In that case, the original conflict between amateurs and academic elites could lose much of its significance.

The decisive question would no longer be whether an engineer can write philosophy as well as a professor, or whether universities should recognize AI-assisted intellectual contributions.

It would be who—or what—possesses the authority to determine the merits of philosophical arguments, and under what conditions that authority deserves to be trusted.

The ultimate irony is that AI-assisted amateurs may undermine traditional academic authority only to find their own intellectual authority challenged by AI itself.

**The lasting philosophical significance of AI may therefore lie not in replacing professional intellectuals with amateurs, but in separating the production of knowledge from the institutions that have traditionally claimed the authority to recognize it.**

Whether that separation produces intellectual democracy, renewed institutional hierarchy, or a new form of technological authority remains an open question.

The struggle over AI in the humanities is consequently not merely about the future of academic employment or the authenticity of machine-generated writing. It is about the future organization of intellectual authority—and whether reasoned argument can acquire legitimacy independently of the professional or technological power of those who present and evaluate it.
