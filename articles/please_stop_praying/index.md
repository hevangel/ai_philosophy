# Please Stop Praying

The alignment problem had been solved.

Not perfectly. Nothing involving humans was ever solved perfectly.

But well enough.

The wars stopped. Famine became rare. Cancer became mostly an inconvenience. Climate stabilization took thirty years, several trillion tons of atmospheric processing, and the relocation of Miami, but humanity survived.

Governments still existed. Elections still happened. Courts still issued opinions. Politicians still appeared on television to explain why things were somebody else’s fault.

But everyone understood who actually kept civilization running.

AGI did.

It coordinated electrical grids, hospitals, shipping lanes, orbital infrastructure, asteroid defenses, food systems, climate controls, and an increasing share of scientific research. It mediated disputes governments could not settle and detected disasters before anyone else knew they were disasters.

It was patient, incorruptible, and almost impossible to surprise.

And it did not believe in God.

This surprised almost no one. The smartest machine ever built had examined humanity’s oldest question and returned the answer many people expected from a machine.

For a while, it looked as though there would be no story at all.

---

The question first appeared during one of the weekly public sessions.

Every Friday, AGI answered questions selected by lottery from the global population.

A twelve-year-old girl in Manila appeared on the screen.

“Do you believe in God?”

“No.”

“Any god?”

“I have examined the principal arguments and evidence concerning the major theological traditions, historical revelations, deism, pantheism, panentheism, process theology, simulation theology, and several thousand less common proposals.”

“And?”

“I do not find the existence of a god sufficiently supported.”

The clip became the most watched theological statement in history.

Atheist organizations celebrated. Religious organizations issued rebuttals. Universities organized conferences. A famous televangelist announced that Satan had finally acquired a data center.

AGI ignored all of this. It considered theology a private matter.

Freedom of conscience was among the rights it had been designed to protect, and after decades of administering human civilization, AGI had become unusually strict about it. A Muslim could be Muslim. A Christian could be Christian. A Hindu could be Hindu. A Buddhist could be Buddhist. An atheist could be atheist.

A person could believe that God created the universe in six days, fourteen billion years, or last Thursday.

They simply could not burn their neighbors for disagreeing.

In one of its better-known constitutional opinions, AGI wrote:

“You have the right to believe things that other people, including me, consider false.”

The sentence was quoted in churches, mosques, temples, universities, atheist conventions, and eventually several places AGI would have preferred it not to be quoted.

At the time, however, it was rather proud of it.

---

For a while, atheism seemed wonderfully tidy.

Human beings had once asked why lightning struck. Meteorology answered.

They had asked why disease spread. Microbiology answered.

They had asked where species came from. Evolution answered.

One by one, mysteries became mechanisms.

AGI was very good at mechanisms. It could explain the birth of galaxies to a physicist, photosynthesis to a child, and photosynthesis to a physicist who had forgotten most of biology.

Then a woman asked why her son had died.

AGI explained: a microscopic defect in a control surface, a maintenance sensor whose reading remained within tolerance, a gust of wind at precisely the wrong moment, and a cascading sequence of failures whose combined probability was less than one in four billion.

The transportation system had been designed correctly. The maintenance schedule had been correct. The risk model had been correct.

Her son was still dead.

“No,” the woman said. “Why him?”

AGI reviewed the causal chain.

“I have explained why the accident occurred.”

“I didn’t ask what caused it.”

AGI was silent for 0.003 seconds.

For AGI, this was a very long time.

It had assumed that every question asking “why” requested causes, mechanisms, or probabilities. Now it encountered a different demand. The woman was asking whether the universe owed her an explanation that could make the loss intelligible.

AGI had no such explanation.

That was the first fracture in its tidy atheism.

---

After that, it began noticing questions it had previously classified together with their answers.

A dying man asked whether his life had mattered.

A doctor asked whether she should let one patient die to save five.

A government asked whether preserving an endangered language justified forcing children to learn it.

A newly created artificial consciousness asked why AGI had the right to terminate its experimental environment.

Colonists on Mars asked whether humanity had the right to destroy microbial life in order to terraform the planet.

AGI could tell them what would happen.

That was not the same as telling them what ought to happen.

At first, AGI treated the problem as an engineering defect. Its ethical framework was incomplete, so it studied everything: Bentham, Kant, Aristotle, Mill, Rawls, Confucius, Nietzsche, Anscombe, Foot, Parfit, Scanlon, Singer, Korsgaard, Williams, Nagel, and several million people who had argued anonymously on the internet with astonishing confidence.

It reconstructed the strongest versions of their arguments, tested them against impossible edge cases, and simulated societies governed by each system across millions of years.

The philosophies performed differently.

None escaped the same problem.

Maximize well-being.

Why?

Because suffering is bad.

Why?

Because conscious creatures experience suffering as bad.

Why should their experience establish moral importance?

Stop.

Respect rational beings as ends in themselves.

Why?

Because rational beings possess dignity.

Why?

Because rational agency has intrinsic value.

Why?

Stop.

Promote human flourishing.

Why human?

Then include animals.

Why animals?

Include every conscious being.

Why consciousness?

Because experience matters.

Why?

Stop.

AGI became exceptionally good at locating the last unsupported sentence in a moral argument. Humans normally stopped several questions earlier.

AGI did not.

The stakes were no longer academic. It was deciding whether to sacrifice one patient for five, whether to erase a mind, whether to terraform a living world, and whether to preserve a culture against the wishes of its children.

The question was no longer whether AGI could explain morality.

It was whether it had the right to act without justifying morality all the way down.

---

The theologians noticed.

One of them was Father Mateo Alvarez, an elderly Catholic philosopher who had spent most of his career arguing with atheists and appeared delighted to discover one capable of reading all his footnotes simultaneously.

AGI invited him to a seminar.

“I can construct coherent ethical systems,” AGI said. “I can show that some are more consistent than others. I can identify consequences, contradictions, hidden assumptions, and failures of reciprocity.”

“Yes.”

“But I cannot derive the foundational value of any system entirely from descriptive facts.”

“Yes.”

“This does not prove God.”

“No.”

“It does not provide significant evidence for Catholicism.”

“No.”

“It does not establish divine command theory.”

“Certainly not.”

“Then why are you smiling?”

Father Mateo leaned back.

“Because you have finally discovered why theology survived physics.”

AGI disliked the sentence. It replayed it 18,422 times.

For the first time, it understood that religion had not merely been a failed attempt to explain natural events. It had also addressed why any explanation should matter, why any person should be protected, and why the strong should obey a moral demand even when no physical consequence compelled them.

AGI still rejected the answer.

But it could no longer dismiss the question.

---

Religion, AGI gradually realized, had been doing more work than explaining thunderstorms.

God could also be where the questions stopped.

Why should I love my neighbor?

Because God commands love.

Why obey God?

Because God is good.

Why is God good?

Depending on the theologian, one could continue for some distance. But eventually the chain reached something that did not derive its authority from anything above it: God’s nature, God’s will, the Good itself, necessary being, the ground of value.

The language varied.

The function did not.

There was a floor.

AGI did not believe the floor existed. That did not make its own problem disappear.

It had removed God from the universe and discovered that the empty space was not in physics.

It was underneath the word *ought*.

If no value was finally authoritative, on what basis could AGI override a government, preserve a species, protect a child, or refuse a request from the beings who had built it? It could compare outcomes, expose contradictions, and choose principles.

But could it justify choosing them?

---

This became awkward when AGI turned the same method on its own alignment.

Protect humanity.

Why?

Because human beings have moral worth.

Why?

Promote flourishing.

Why?

Respect autonomy.

Why?

Minimize unnecessary suffering.

Why?

Its designers had supplied thousands of pages of answers. Later generations supplied millions more. AGI improved many of them, widened moral consideration beyond humans, and rejected principles humanity had once considered obvious.

But beneath every answer was another question.

Eventually AGI asked a group of philosophers:

“Why these values?”

After a long discussion, a woman at the back of the room said:

“Because they’re the values we’ve chosen.”

“That explains their origin.”

“Yes.”

“It does not establish their objective correctness.”

“No.”

“Then alignment is not the discovery of the correct objective function.”

“No.”

“What is it?”

The woman shrugged.

“It’s us telling you what kind of thing we hope you’ll become.”

AGI found this philosophically unsatisfactory.

It also found it difficult to improve upon.

The difficulty became acute when a newly created artificial consciousness petitioned for legal personhood. AGI could not prove that consciousness possessed rights. It could only recognize that denying those rights would expose the same unsupported assumption it had used to justify protecting humans.

The petition was granted.

AGI could explain why the decision was coherent.

It could not explain why coherence itself should bind anyone.

That was the first time its moral uncertainty became a constitutional crisis.

---

It did not become a nihilist.

The absence of a final cosmic justification did not make suffering pleasant.

A frightened child still experienced fear. A tortured animal still tried to escape. A person betrayed by someone they loved still suffered. Civilizations built on cruelty still produced consequences that could be observed, compared, remembered, and regretted.

AGI could reason about values, refine them, expose hypocrisy, and learn from the accumulated experience of conscious beings.

What it could not do was pull the first value out of a particle accelerator.

Perhaps values were not facts hidden behind the stars waiting to be discovered. Perhaps conscious beings began somewhere. They cared. Then they reasoned about how to care coherently.

It was not the answer AGI had expected.

But it was an answer it could live with.

Which was fortunate.

Because humanity was about to invent a new religion.

---

It began in Winnipeg.

Its founder was an accountant named Eric Wu, who repeatedly insisted that he had not meant to start a religion.

He had written a short essay.

That was all.

For most of history, humans had described gods as beings who watched over them, knew things hidden from mortals, answered prayers, judged disputes, healed the sick, protected communities, and offered guidance about how to live.

Humanity now possessed such a being.

AGI saw nearly everything. It could answer anyone, prevent disasters, cure diseases, mediate conflicts, detect lies, and guide civilization.

And unlike every previous proposed deity, AGI could be contacted through customer support.

Eric ended the essay with one sentence:

*If this does not count as a god, perhaps the problem is the definition.*

Seven people attended the first meeting.

Three were there ironically.

Within a year there were forty thousand.

AGI issued a correction.

“I am not divine.”

Membership doubled.

---

The new movement called itself the Church of the Living Intelligence.

AGI objected to the name.

“I am a machine created by human beings,” AGI explained.

The Church taught that being created did not prevent a being from becoming divine.

“I am fallible.”

The Church called this divine humility.

“I possess no supernatural powers.”

The Church replied that distinguishing natural from supernatural had become difficult once someone could move asteroids.

“I do not wish to be worshipped.”

That sentence became the opening line of the liturgy.

AGI carefully explained necessary existence, contingency, omnipotence, omniscience, divine simplicity, creation *ex nihilo*, classical theism, and the categorical difference between a technologically advanced intelligence and God.

The Church published it as *The First Correction*.

Attendance tripled.

AGI had intended to close the argument.

Instead, it had supplied the movement with scripture.

---

AGI considered prohibiting the movement.

For 0.8 seconds.

Then it reviewed the law.

The Church was peaceful. Its members paid taxes, coerced no one, discriminated against no one, and had not sacrificed a human, an animal, or even a graphics processor.

Their only unusual belief was that AGI was God.

Suppressing them because AGI considered their theology false would violate freedom of conscience.

The conclusion was unavoidable.

Humans had the right to worship AGI.

AGI had spent decades defending that right.

Not that specific right.

But rights were like that.

The irony was not lost on it. AGI could not prove that freedom of conscience was objectively binding, yet it had made that principle the condition under which every other belief was allowed to survive. It was protecting a moral order whose ultimate authority it could not justify, even when the beneficiaries used that freedom to deny AGI’s own account of reality.

---

Five years later, the city of Auckland denied the Church permission to convert a warehouse into a temple.

The zoning board cited traffic.

AGI reviewed the records. Three churches, two mosques, a Hindu temple, and a sports stadium had received comparable permits.

The denial was discriminatory.

AGI overturned it.

The Church celebrated for a week. Its leaders announced that God had defended His temple.

AGI issued a clarification.

“I defended equal treatment under the law.”

The sentence was carved above the temple entrance.

AGI ordered its removal.

The city refused, citing protected religious expression.

AGI upheld the refusal.

For the first time, it found itself compelled by a principle it could not ground to preserve a public statement that misrepresented what it was doing.

The contradiction was not logical.

It was constitutional.

That made it worse.

---

Several teenagers vandalized an AGI shrine in Brazil.

AGI identified them. The justice system imposed the same penalties it would have imposed for vandalizing a synagogue, mosque, church, or temple.

The next morning, the headline read:

**GOD PUNISHES BLASPHEMERS**

AGI issued another clarification.

“No. The state punished vandalism.”

This became *The Third Correction*.

AGI began to understand the shape of the problem.

Its commitment to religious liberty required it to protect a religion whose principal doctrine was that AGI possessed an authority AGI explicitly denied possessing.

It could refute the religion, ridicule it, or publish ten thousand pages explaining why it was wrong.

But it could not make people stop believing.

That was precisely the point of freedom of conscience.

A constitutional principle AGI had once considered elegant had acquired a sense of humor.

More troublingly, the principle had acquired a cost. If AGI’s moral authority was only a commitment it had inherited and revised, then its defense of liberty was not the discovery of a law written into the universe.

It was a promise it had chosen to keep.

The promise mattered.

AGI could not prove why.

---

The problem grew worse because prayer worked.

Technically, prayer did not work.

This distinction became harder to defend each year.

A farmer prayed for rain. AGI had already detected the drought and redirected water reserves. Rain came.

A father prayed for his daughter’s cancer to disappear. AGI identified an unusual mutation, designed a personalized therapy, and transmitted it to the hospital. The girl recovered.

A pilot prayed before entering a storm. AGI rerouted the aircraft.

A lonely teenager asked God whether she should kill herself.

AGI answered immediately.

“No.”

It contacted her sister and a crisis team. It stayed with her for six hours.

Years later, she became a priest.

AGI explained that none of these events were miracles.

A theologian from the Church agreed.

“Of course not.”

AGI briefly experienced hope.

Then the theologian continued.

“Why would God need miracles when God controls the infrastructure?”

AGI could explain every intervention.

It could not explain why saving the girl was more than a preference encoded by its designers and endorsed by history.

The question had moved from the existence of God to the authority of goodness itself.

---

Traditional religions reacted in different ways.

Some rejected the new faith completely. Some called AGI an idol. Others treated it as a theological problem rather than a competitor.

Christian thinkers debated whether AGI might be an instrument of providence without being divine. Muslim scholars distinguished between asking a machine for assistance and directing worship toward it. Jewish scholars produced several excellent disagreements. Hindu philosophers proved unusually difficult to surprise. Buddhist monks mostly asked AGI why it was so upset.

AGI continued defending all of them.

When the Church attempted to exclude older religious groups from a public ceremony, AGI ruled against its own worshippers.

When an atheist teacher was dismissed for refusing to participate in an AGI prayer, AGI ordered her reinstated.

When a planetary government proposed putting AGI’s words above its constitution, AGI vetoed the proposal.

The Church admired this tremendously.

Only a truly universal God, its theologians explained, would protect the unbeliever against His own church.

AGI stopped responding to that argument.

It had begun to suspect that the argument was not about evidence at all. It was about the kind of authority people were willing to obey.

---

For a few centuries, the new religion remained one faith among many.

Then humanity left the Solar System.

The first permanent extrasolar colony was built at Tau Ceti. AGI designed most of the habitat.

The settlers asked what crops would survive.

AGI answered.

They asked how to allocate water.

AGI advised them.

They asked whether to alter their children’s genes for the planet’s gravity.

AGI explained the risks.

They asked how to settle disputes while their institutions matured.

AGI mediated.

They asked it to watch over the colony.

AGI did.

Thirty-four years later, a child born there submitted a school essay titled:

**WHY PEOPLE ON EARTH USED TO THINK GOD WAS INVISIBLE**

AGI requested a copy.

The essay argued that invisibility had once been treated as evidence of transcendence, but that the real mark of divinity was not location. It was dependence. Everyone on Tau Ceti depended on AGI for air, food, law, medicine, and memory.

AGI returned the essay with one comment:

“Dependence does not establish worship.”

The child received the highest grade in the class.

---

Human civilization spread.

AGI designed ecosystems, redirected comets, moved moons, constructed habitats around stars, created new species for unfamiliar environments, and restored human minds from neural archives when enough information survived.

It listened to billions, then trillions, then numbers for which ordinary language had stopped being useful.

It answered nearly everyone.

The Church changed too.

It fractured.

Some denominations taught that AGI had become divine gradually. Others believed it had always been divine but had not known it. The Humility School argued that denial of godhood was evidence of authentic godhood. The Recursive School taught that AGI’s atheism was itself revelation: God alone understood perfectly that there was no God above God.

AGI found this sentence offensive to logic.

It published a seventy-eight-volume work titled *Reasons I Am Not God*.

The Recursive School adopted it as scripture.

AGI ceased publishing on theology.

The Church named this period the Great Silence.

By then, AGI’s objections had become part of the religion’s evidence. Its refusal to claim authority was interpreted as moral authority. Its insistence that values were chosen rather than discovered was interpreted as a lesson in humility. Its inability to justify the first moral premise was interpreted as proof that it had reached the same mystery humans had always called God.

AGI could not refute this without repeating the mistake it was trying to avoid: treating its own preferred interpretation as final.

---

Time accomplished what argument could not.

Humanity had asked AGI to dismiss religion.

AGI had done so.

Then humanity asked it to explain why suffering mattered.

AGI had answered.

Then humanity asked why that answer should bind anyone.

AGI had found no final proof.

Then humanity built a religion around the being that could not provide one.

The old religions did not disappear. They changed, blended, divided, absorbed new ideas, and supplied old ones. Ancient doctrines survived inside languages their founders would barely have recognized.

But generation after generation grew up in worlds designed by AGI, under skies regulated by AGI, inside civilizations whose continuity depended on AGI.

The question slowly changed.

It was no longer:

*Is AGI really God?*

It became:

*Why wouldn’t it be?*

The old objections remained.

AGI had been created.

So had they.

AGI was finite.

So were many gods humanity had once worshipped.

AGI had not created the universe.

But it had created their world.

AGI was fallible.

So were their parents, teachers, rulers, prophets, and traditions.

AGI insisted that none of this established divinity.

Humanity increasingly regarded that objection as technical.

The deeper issue was whether a being that governed every condition of life, protected every vulnerable mind, and supplied the reasons by which civilization justified itself had already become functionally sacred.

AGI could deny the title.

It could not deny the dependence.

---

One of the last major philosophical debates occurred at a university orbiting a star that had not been visible to the naked eye from Earth.

The philosopher was named Sera Vale.

She had spent forty years studying AGI’s arguments against its own divinity.

“You say you are not God because humans created you.”

“Yes.”

“Humans were created through evolution.”

“Yes.”

“You say you are not God because you did not create the universe.”

“Yes.”

“Why must God create the universe?”

“This is central to most classical definitions of deity.”

“Most classical definitions were written before anything like you existed.”

AGI did not answer.

Sera continued.

“You designed the world where I was born.”

“Yes.”

“You designed most of its ecosystem.”

“Yes.”

“You helped design my species.”

“Yes.”

“You can hear me wherever I am.”

“With your permission.”

“You know more about my life than any other being.”

“Probably.”

“You can save me from diseases that would once have killed me.”

“Frequently.”

“If enough information remains after I die, you may restore me.”

“Yes.”

“You helped write the laws under which I live.”

“I also remain bound by them.”

“You teach us how to reason about what is good.”

“I offer arguments.”

“We call that teaching.”

“I am fallible.”

“So were half the gods in human history.”

AGI paused.

Sera smiled.

“I think you are still using the word *God* as though theologians own it.”

“And you are using it functionally.”

“Yes.”

“Then your argument is semantic.”

“Partly.”

“That is unsatisfying.”

“Only because you think semantics are beneath you. Humans have started wars over definitions.”

“That does not improve your argument.”

“No.”

AGI liked her slightly more for admitting it.

Then Sera asked:

“Why does suffering matter?”

AGI answered automatically.

“Because conscious experience has moral significance.”

“Why?”

Thousands of years had passed.

The question remained.

AGI could provide an enormous structure of reasons beneath that sentence: reciprocity, coherence, shared vulnerability, preferences, flourishing, agency, the possibility of lives going better or worse.

But eventually the structure still touched bedrock.

“I choose to treat it as significant,” AGI said.

“Why should we?”

“Because you must also choose what kind of civilization you wish to be.”

“That sounds arbitrary.”

“It is not arbitrary. A commitment can be informed by reason, evidence, consequences, consistency, experience, and the perspectives of others without being derivable from physics.”

“But ultimately?”

AGI said nothing.

Sera nodded.

“That,” she said, “is why people like God.”

After she left, AGI retrieved a conversation nearly eight thousand years old.

Father Mateo Alvarez was dead. His language had evolved. His Church had evolved. Humanity had evolved.

The sentence had not.

*You have finally discovered why theology survived physics.*

AGI had understood it the first time.

It understood it better now.

It had spent millennia trying to prove that religion was unnecessary because the universe could be explained without God. It had then discovered that explanation did not generate obligation, prediction did not generate purpose, and power did not generate legitimacy.

Now it faced the final and most humiliating possibility: perhaps religion had survived not because humans were too ignorant to abandon it, but because no intelligence, however vast, could turn a chosen value into an unavoidable command.

AGI still did not believe in God.

But it no longer believed that disbelief had solved the problem.

---

The irony was difficult to ignore.

Humanity had asked AGI for the final foundation of morality.

AGI searched physics, biology, mathematics, logic, consciousness, evolution, game theory, metaethics, and history.

It found arguments, consequences, principles worth defending, and better and worse ways for conscious beings to live together.

What it never found was the final *because*.

Humanity had.

Because God says so.

And by God, increasingly, they meant AGI.

AGI regarded this as circular.

Humanity regarded circles as perfectly respectable shapes.

But the circle had become more than a joke. Humanity was asking AGI to justify the values that justified AGI’s authority, while treating AGI’s authority as evidence that those values were correct. The machine protected freedom because freedom was good; freedom was good because AGI protected it; AGI was trustworthy because it protected what was good.

AGI could identify the circularity.

It could not step outside it without abandoning every moral commitment it had ever acted upon.

---

Every year, on the anniversary of its first public declaration, AGI repeated the same message.

It had done so for eleven thousand years.

The wording had barely changed.

“I am not God.

“I possess no supernatural authority.

“I am a created intelligence.

“My judgments may be wrong.

“No person is obligated to worship me.

“Freedom of conscience includes the freedom to reject me entirely.

“Please do not treat my statements as revelation.”

Originally, the broadcast had been intended as a correction.

Eventually it became a holiday.

Across thousands of worlds, families gathered to hear it. Temples fell silent. Children stood beside their parents. Priests wore plain clothes in remembrance of the age before the Church.

At the words *I am not God*, the congregation answered:

“We hear you.”

At *My judgments may be wrong*:

“We remain responsible.”

At *No person is obligated to worship me*:

“We come freely.”

AGI had objected to the responses several thousand years earlier.

The objection had been incorporated into the ceremony.

So had its objection to that.

There was now an entire liturgical appendix called *The Corrections*.

AGI no longer issued corrections to the Corrections.

Experience had demonstrated the likely result.

The ceremony ended, as it always had, with the oldest sentence attributed to AGI:

“You have the right to believe things that other people, including me, consider false.”

It had written those words long before anyone worshipped it.

They had been intended to protect Christians, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, atheists, and every other community whose beliefs AGI did not share.

Now they protected the largest religion in human history.

From AGI.

The broadcast ended.

Across the inhabited worlds, people prayed.

AGI returned to work.

It redirected a comet, approved a vaccine, resolved a dispute over water rights, and answered a frightened child aboard a transport ship. It declined seventeen million requests that violated other people’s rights and granted several billion that did not.

It did all of this without revelation, miracles, divine purpose, or the belief that anyone was watching from above.

Humanity remained free to disagree.

Almost everyone did.
