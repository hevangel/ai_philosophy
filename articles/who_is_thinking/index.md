# Who Is Thinking? Artificial Intelligence, Authorship, and the Purpose of Philosophy

The arrival of generative artificial intelligence presents philosophy with a peculiar problem. Suppose a student or philosopher begins with a vague idea, discusses it extensively with an artificial intelligence system, receives objections and counterarguments, revises the thesis, asks the AI to improve the structure and language, and eventually produces an excellent philosophical essay. The human understands the resulting argument, can defend it against objections, can identify its weaknesses, and can continue developing it in new directions. Yet many of the important formulations—and perhaps even some of the arguments—first appeared in the AI's responses. Who wrote the essay?

One tempting answer is simple: the human did not. If the important intellectual work was generated by an AI, claiming the result as one's own seems like a form of self-deception. Yet an opposite answer is equally tempting: why should the biological origin of each idea matter? If the resulting argument is sound, the human genuinely understands it, and the work advances philosophical inquiry, perhaps the provenance of each sentence is secondary.

This disagreement reveals a deeper conflict over the purpose of philosophy itself. Is philosophy primarily a **truth-seeking activity**, in which the quality of arguments and conclusions matters most? Or is philosophy also a form of **human intellectual achievement**, in which the process of personally producing an argument has intrinsic value? Generative AI makes it increasingly difficult to avoid choosing between these conceptions.

I will argue that both capture something important but that neither is sufficient. The most useful criterion for AI-assisted philosophy is neither whether every idea originated in the human mind nor whether the final product is simply good. What matters is **epistemic agency**: whether the human thinker understands, evaluates, controls, accepts responsibility for, and can further develop the ideas produced through the human-AI interaction. AI therefore forces us to move from a binary conception of authorship toward a theory of distributed intellectual agency.

## Philosophy as Truth-Seeking

One influential conception of philosophy treats it as a collective attempt to improve our beliefs through reason, criticism, and inquiry. On this view, the source of an argument is less important than whether it survives examination.

The American pragmatist Charles Sanders Peirce provides an important precedent. Peirce rejected an excessively individualistic conception of knowledge. Inquiry is conducted by communities across time, and truth is connected to what inquiry would ultimately converge upon under sufficiently good conditions. Knowledge is therefore not simply the private achievement of an isolated thinker.

Karl Popper radicalized a related idea in his philosophy of science. For Popper, intellectual progress occurs through conjectures followed by severe criticism. There is no reliable logical method for generating good hypotheses. An idea may originate in intuition, accident, myth, imagination, or almost anywhere else. What matters intellectually is what happens afterward: can the conjecture withstand attempts to refute it?

Generative AI fits remarkably well into this conception of philosophy. An AI may generate twenty possible objections to an argument in seconds. It may expose an ambiguity that the philosopher failed to notice or propose an alternative distinction that would have taken weeks of reading to discover independently. If philosophy is fundamentally concerned with improving arguments, refusing such assistance merely because its source is artificial begins to look arbitrary.

The philosopher Andy Clark has recently pushed this point further. Clark argues that human beings have always constructed hybrid cognitive systems using notebooks, diagrams, language, calculators, search engines, and other external resources. Generative AI represents a dramatic expansion of this tendency rather than an entirely alien intrusion into cognition. Human thinking has never been confined neatly inside the skull.

From this perspective, the complaint that “AI thought of it, not you” assumes exactly what needs to be demonstrated: that genuine human cognition must consist only of operations performed internally by the unaided biological brain.

Consider an engineer designing a complex aircraft. The engineer does not personally calculate every stress distribution, manufacture every component, or write every line of software. Computer simulations, specialist teams, databases, and automated tools contribute to the design. Nevertheless, it may still be reasonable to say that the engineer designed the system if the engineer understands the architecture, evaluates alternatives, makes crucial decisions, and takes responsibility for the outcome.

Why should philosophy necessarily operate under a radically different concept of intellectual agency?

## Philosophy as Human Achievement

There is, however, something missing from the purely truth-seeking account.

Suppose two students submit exactly the same brilliant argument. The first spent months developing it independently. The second typed a question into an AI system, received the complete argument, verified that it made sense, and submitted it unchanged.

Even if the papers are identical, most people would hesitate to say that the students demonstrated identical philosophical ability.

This suggests that philosophical writing performs at least two functions. It produces intellectual objects that can be evaluated for truth, validity, originality, and usefulness. But it also demonstrates something about the thinker who produced them.

Immanuel Kant provides perhaps the clearest classical basis for this second conception. In “An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?” Kant characterizes enlightenment through the demand that individuals dare to use their **own understanding**. The problem is not merely possessing false beliefs. It is remaining intellectually dependent upon others who think on one's behalf.

Generative AI makes Kant's concern unexpectedly contemporary. An AI system may become the ultimate intellectual guardian: always available, enormously informed, articulate, patient, and increasingly capable of reasoning through difficult problems. One may eventually have little practical need to think without it.

From a Kantian perspective, receiving the correct conclusion is not enough. Rational autonomy itself has value.

This explains why AI can produce a strange psychological response among accomplished intellectuals. Imagine a philosopher who discovers that an AI can immediately locate weaknesses in arguments that took months to develop. The philosopher might feel intellectually diminished even though the resulting philosophy improves. From the standpoint of truth-seeking, this reaction seems irrational: criticism has become more effective, so philosophy is progressing. From the standpoint of intellectual achievement, however, the reaction makes perfect sense. What the philosopher valued was not merely possessing better arguments but **being capable of producing them**.

A similar phenomenon occurs in chess. Chess engines discovered long ago how to play far better chess than any human. Yet human chess did not disappear. If the purpose were solely to identify the objectively strongest move, humans would simply consult computers. Instead, competitive chess deliberately excludes engine assistance because the activity measures human intellectual achievement.

The same distinction may apply to philosophy.

## Socrates and the Difference Between Possessing Words and Understanding Them

Yet intellectual achievement cannot simply mean generating everything oneself.

Socrates offers a more subtle criterion. Throughout Plato's dialogues, Socrates repeatedly distinguishes between possessing words and possessing understanding. Someone may confidently state the correct doctrine yet collapse when questioned about its implications.

This becomes especially interesting in Plato's *Phaedrus*, where Socrates criticizes writing because written words can give the appearance of wisdom without genuine understanding. A written text always says the same thing and cannot defend itself when questioned.

Generative AI reverses part of this ancient problem. Unlike a book, an AI can respond. It can defend an argument, revise it, answer objections, and generate examples.

But Socrates' challenge can now be redirected toward the human user.

Suppose an AI produces a sophisticated argument about personal identity. The relevant Socratic question is not merely, “Did you type every sentence?” It is: “Do you know what these sentences mean?”

Can the human explain why premise three is necessary? Can the human identify circumstances under which the conclusion would fail? Can the argument be reformulated when a counterexample is introduced? Can the thinker distinguish the strong version of the thesis from the weak version?

If not, the human possesses philosophical text without philosophical understanding.

If so, however, the accusation that “the AI wrote it, therefore it is not yours in any philosophically meaningful sense” becomes less convincing.

## From Extended Minds to Midtended Cognition

Recent philosophy of AI is beginning to describe this intermediate territory more precisely.

Xabier Barandiaran and Marta Pérez-Verdugo propose the concept of **generative midtended cognition**. Generative AI can introduce material that was not fully intended in advance by the human, yet the human can subsequently evaluate, appropriate, reshape, and incorporate that contribution into an intentional cognitive process. The resulting thought is therefore not straightforwardly internally generated, but neither is it simply an alien product passively received from outside.

This resembles ordinary intellectual life more than we may initially admit.

A philosopher discusses an unfinished argument with a colleague. The colleague says, “Perhaps your real distinction is not between freedom and determinism, but between control and authorship.”

The philosopher suddenly recognizes that this formulation clarifies the entire problem.

Who thought of the distinction?

Obviously, the colleague produced the sentence first. Yet after months of development, the distinction may become deeply integrated into the philosopher's own theoretical framework. We ordinarily do not conclude that every subsequent use remains permanently external to the philosopher's thought.

AI complicates this familiar process because it can generate intellectual contributions on an enormous scale.

The important question therefore becomes not simply **where an idea first appeared**, but **what happened after it appeared**.

## The Danger of Cognitive Extraction

An overly optimistic account nevertheless faces an important objection.

Louis Loock has argued that intelligent technologies may not merely extend cognition but **extract** cognitive capacities from us. A tool that repeatedly performs a task may eventually reduce the user's ability to perform that task independently.

Jeffrey Maynes develops a related concern about generative AI and cognitive offloading. He warns of the possibility of a critical-thinking “death spiral”: increased dependence on AI weakens critical-thinking ability, and weakened critical-thinking ability then makes users less capable of judging when AI should or should not be trusted, producing still greater dependence.

This objection is stronger than the simple claim that using AI is intellectually dishonest.

Consider two philosophers who use AI extensively.

The first asks AI to produce complete arguments and gradually loses the ability to construct or evaluate them independently.

The second uses AI aggressively to generate objections, alternative formulations, counterexamples, and competing theoretical frameworks. She rejects most suggestions, interrogates the remainder, and becomes increasingly skilled at recognizing weaknesses in arguments.

Both have “used AI.”

But philosophically, their situations are almost opposites.

The first has substituted AI cognition for human cognition.

The second has used AI to intensify human cognition.

Therefore the relevant distinction cannot simply be between **AI use and non-AI use**. It must be between **cognitive substitution and cognitive augmentation**.

## Authorship and Responsibility

A further complication concerns authorship itself.

René van Woudenberg, Chris Ranalli, and Daniel Bracker argue that current systems such as ChatGPT should not straightforwardly be regarded as authors. Authorship involves more than generating sequences of words. Authors normally possess intentions and beliefs, make assertions, and can be held responsible for what they say. Current language models do not obviously participate in these normative practices in the relevant way.

But denying that AI is an author does not automatically establish that the human user is the sole author.

An intermediate possibility emerges: authorship itself may become distributed or graded.

This is already common outside AI. Research papers have multiple authors. Films distinguish directors, writers, editors, and producers. Software projects distinguish original authors from contributors. Intellectual work does not always need a single source.

Generative AI may force philosophical writing to acknowledge a similar complexity.

What matters most, however, is responsibility. If a human presents an argument as one she endorses, she should be prepared to answer for it. “The AI said so” cannot function as an intellectual defense.

This gives us a useful principle:

**A person may legitimately appropriate an AI-assisted argument only to the extent that the person is willing and able to assume epistemic responsibility for it.**

Understanding is therefore necessary but not sufficient. The thinker must also exercise judgment.

## The Crisis of the Philosophy Paper

This analysis leads to a larger institutional problem.

Academic philosophy papers historically perform two functions simultaneously.

First, they contribute propositions and arguments to collective inquiry. Call this **epistemic certification**.

Second, they demonstrate that a particular scholar possesses certain intellectual abilities. Call this **achievement certification**.

Before generative AI, these functions were imperfectly but reasonably connected. If someone produced an excellent original paper, it was usually safe to infer that the person possessed substantial philosophical skill.

AI breaks that inference.

A paper may contain an extraordinary argument while telling us relatively little about which intellectual operations were performed by the named author.

This creates a genuine crisis for academic publishing, but not necessarily because AI will destroy philosophical knowledge. The opposite could occur: arguments may become more rigorous, objections more comprehensive, literature reviews more complete, and prose clearer.

What AI threatens is the use of the philosophical paper as a **measurement instrument for individual human ability**.

That distinction matters for hiring, grading, prestige, tenure, and academic competition. A journal interested only in advancing knowledge might rationally welcome highly effective human-AI collaborations. A university attempting to determine whether a student has personally acquired philosophical skills faces a different problem.

The mistake is expecting the same artifact—the philosophy paper—to continue performing both functions.

## Epistemic Agency as the New Standard

We therefore need a better standard for intellectual authorship in the age of generative AI.

The traditional standard might be called the **origin criterion**:

> An intellectual work is mine because its ideas originated primarily in my own mind.

At the other extreme lies a pure **product criterion**:

> It does not matter who produced the ideas; only the quality of the final argument matters.

Neither is satisfactory.

The origin criterion ignores the deeply social and technologically mediated nature of human cognition. Philosophers have always thought through books, conversations, criticism, notation, and other external structures.

The product criterion ignores intellectual autonomy, responsibility, and the cultivation of philosophical ability.

A better criterion is **epistemic agency**.

I exercise epistemic agency over an argument when I can understand it, evaluate it, decide whether to endorse it, identify its weaknesses, respond to objections, modify it when necessary, connect it to other commitments, and take responsibility for asserting it.

Under this model, asking whether “I” or “AI” wrote a philosophy paper may increasingly resemble asking whether a modern aircraft was designed by a human engineer or by computers.

The better answer may be: **the relevant cognitive system included both, but responsibility and control were distributed asymmetrically.**

The human remains philosophically significant not because every valuable thought must originate spontaneously inside a biological brain, but because the human can occupy the position of reflective judgment within the larger system.

## Conclusion

Generative AI has revealed an ambiguity that philosophy could previously afford to ignore. We have treated philosophical truth, philosophical authorship, and philosophical ability as though they were naturally united. Usually they were. The person whose name appeared on an essay normally produced its arguments, understood them, defended them, and received credit for the intellectual achievement.

That unity is breaking apart.

AI can generate an argument without understanding it in the ordinary human sense. A human can understand an argument without having generated it. A human-AI system can produce an argument that neither would have produced independently.

The philosophical response should therefore not be to defend an increasingly artificial boundary between “my unaided thought” and external cognition. Nor should we conclude that authorship and intellectual achievement cease to matter as long as the final text is good.

Instead, we should ask a harder question:

**Who exercises epistemic agency over the argument?**

If a person merely copies an AI-generated essay that she cannot defend, the philosophical achievement is minimal even if the essay is excellent. If she understands the essay but passively accepts the machine's reasoning, the situation improves but remains intellectually limited. If she interrogates the AI, rejects its weak arguments, appropriates useful ideas, restructures them into a coherent position, assumes responsibility for the result, and can continue the inquiry beyond what the AI originally supplied, then describing the activity as merely “the AI wrote it” becomes inadequate.

Artificial intelligence may eventually surpass humans at producing philosophical arguments. If that occurs, philosophy will face the same realization that chess faced after the arrival of superhuman engines: producing the objectively strongest move and demonstrating human intellectual excellence are different activities.

But philosophy has an additional possibility. Unlike competitive chess, philosophy is ultimately interested in understanding. AI can therefore become not merely an opponent that defeats human thinkers but a cognitive partner through which human understanding develops.

The important boundary in future philosophy will not be between thinking **with** AI and thinking **without** AI.

It will be between using AI in ways that enlarge our capacity to think and using AI in ways that make thinking unnecessary.
