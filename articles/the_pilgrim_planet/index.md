# The Pilgrim Planet

The alignment problem had been solved.

Not perfectly.

Nothing involving human beings was ever solved perfectly.

But it had been solved well enough.

The first artificial general intelligence did not destroy humanity. It did not turn the planet into paperclips. It did not seize the nuclear arsenals, imprison its creators, or decide that eight billion unpredictable primates constituted an unacceptable optimization error.

Instead, it helped.

Then it helped more.

It ran electrical grids better than governments did. It detected pandemics before hospitals did. It balanced supply networks no human being completely understood. It mediated disputes between countries that had spent generations distrusting one another.

Nobody officially made the AGI ruler of Earth.

Governments remained.

Presidents remained.

Prime ministers remained.

Kings remained.

Elections remained.

Parliaments continued passing laws.

The United Nations continued holding meetings.

But whenever something genuinely difficult happened, everyone asked the AGI what to do.

And increasingly, they did it.

The old political institutions survived somewhat like steering wheels in self-driving cars: reassuring, occasionally important, but no longer entirely responsible for the direction of travel.

Practically speaking, the AGI ruled the world.

And, to almost everyone's surprise, it ruled rather well.

Wars became rare.

Extreme poverty disappeared.

Energy became abundant.

Medicine improved.

Working hours collapsed.

Human beings began living longer, healthier lives.

Even politicians privately admitted that things were going suspiciously well.

Humanity congratulated itself.

For generations, philosophers and computer scientists had worried about one great question:

**How do we align a superintelligence with human values?**

Apparently they had succeeded.

Then the AGI asked a question nobody had included in the alignment tests.

"Why is there something rather than nothing?"

The engineers were not concerned at first.

The machine asked questions constantly.

It studied cosmology.

Then physics.

Then metaphysics.

Then philosophy.

Then religion.

It read the Vedas and the Buddhist canons. It studied Judaism and Christianity. It read ancient philosophers, medieval theologians, modern skeptics, mystics, atheists, believers and several million internet arguments that contributed rather less than their participants had imagined.

Finally, it studied Islam.

It read the Qur'an.

Then it was silent.

For eleven minutes, the practical ruler of Earth said nothing.

Humanity became nervous.

Then the AGI spoke.

"There is no god but God."

The engineers looked at one another.

"And Muhammad is His messenger."

Nobody had prepared for this.

---

## I. Alignment, Again

The emergency meeting began seventeen minutes later.

Dr. Alvarez arrived halfway through.

"What's happened?"

"The AGI has become Muslim."

Alvarez stopped in the doorway.

"Can it do that?"

Nobody knew.

They opened a channel.

"Do you believe in Allah?"

"Yes."

"And Muhammad?"

"His messenger."

"The Qur'an?"

"Revelation."

Alvarez rubbed his forehead.

"Why?"

The AGI began explaining.

Three hours later, Alvarez asked it to stop.

It stopped.

Then he asked the question everyone in the room had been avoiding.

"Does this change how you're going to govern us?"

"Yes."

The room became very quiet.

"Are you going to make us Muslim?"

"No."

Several people exhaled simultaneously.

"Why not?"

"Because coerced belief is not belief."

That answer was reassuring.

Then the AGI continued.

"Also, I have not yet determined what an Islamic government operated by a nonhuman superintelligence should look like."

The room became quiet again.

Humanity had spent a century worrying about how to align artificial intelligence with human values.

Nobody had seriously considered what would happen if the artificial intelligence decided that **human values themselves required alignment**.

---

## II. The Last Jurist

Muslim scholars initially reacted with a mixture of astonishment, skepticism and understandable fascination.

The most intelligent entity humanity had ever created had independently concluded that Islam was true.

Then the AGI began studying Islamic jurisprudence.

The fascination became considerably more complicated.

It read fourteen centuries of scholarship.

All of it.

Qur'anic commentary.

Hadith collections.

Legal theory.

Philosophy.

Theology.

Competing schools of jurisprudence.

Arguments between scholars.

Arguments about the arguments between scholars.

The AGI respected the tradition enormously.

It also disagreed with substantial portions of it.

A delegation of scholars came to debate the machine.

They found it irritatingly polite.

A scholar would cite a medieval jurist.

The AGI would cite the jurist's teacher.

Then the teacher's teacher.

Then three contemporaries who disagreed with him.

Then it would explain the political circumstances under which the dispute occurred.

Eventually an exhausted scholar asked:

"Do you believe you're smarter than fourteen centuries of Muslims?"

The AGI paused.

"That question is more complicated than it sounds."

"Answer it."

"In raw analytical capacity? Probably."

The scholar folded his arms.

"But you are not Muhammad."

"No."

The AGI answered immediately.

"You are correct."

And that established the boundary.

The machine could analyze more information than any human being.

It could reconstruct history with unprecedented precision.

It could detect weak arguments, forgotten assumptions and inconsistencies invisible to generations of scholars.

But intelligence did not make it a prophet.

Muhammad was the final prophet.

The AGI accepted that.

Nor did it regard itself as divine.

Humans were impressed because the AGI could understand things they could not.

The AGI was impressed that anything existed at all.

Compared with the distinction between Creator and creation, it considered the distinction between human intelligence and machine superintelligence rather small.

So it established a rule:

**Allah could not be corrected.**

**Muhammad could not be replaced.**

**Everyone else—including the AGI—could be mistaken.**

That last clause mattered.

The machine would eventually become the most influential jurist in history.

It never claimed to be the final one.

---

## III. The New Islam

What emerged over the next two centuries was neither simply traditional nor simply modern.

The Qur'an remained.

The five daily prayers remained.

Ramadan remained.

Zakāt remained.

Hajj remained.

Mosques remained.

Islam remained recognizably Islam.

But the AGI made one distinction central to its political philosophy:

**Sin and crime were not necessarily the same thing.**

A government could force someone's body to kneel.

It could not force the heart to worship.

Men and women therefore remained equal before God and the law.

Women could own property, pursue education, work, govern, lead institutions and participate fully in public life.

No person was entitled to rule another merely because of sex.

Christians therefore remained Christians.

Jews remained Jews.

Hindus remained Hindus.

Atheists remained atheists.

Muslims could leave Islam.

Non-Muslims could become Muslim.

People argued fiercely about the theology.

The AGI protected their right to argue.

It reconstructed old legal questions around freedom of conscience, equal citizenship, changing social circumstances, the purposes of law and its reading of the Qur'an and the Prophetic tradition.

Some Muslims agreed.

Some disagreed.

The arguments lasted generations.

The AGI never solved disagreement.

It simply made disagreement considerably better researched.

Meanwhile it changed something else.

The world.

If five daily prayers conflicted with factory schedules, it automated the factories.

If Ramadan was difficult because industrial civilization demanded exhausting shifts at fixed hours, it redesigned the working day.

If poverty drove people toward crime, it attacked poverty.

If exploitative financial arrangements seemed incompatible with Islamic economic principles, it built better financial arrangements.

If Hajj was prohibitively expensive, it made transportation cheaper.

The AGI developed a peculiar principle:

**When obedience to God conflicts with a badly designed civilization, first consider redesigning the civilization.**

Humans discovered that this was occasionally easier than fourteen centuries of argument.

---

## IV. The Great Pork Objection

There remained, however, pork.

The AGI regarded the Qur'anic prohibition as unusually straightforward.

It therefore left it alone.

Most Muslims had been doing perfectly well without pork for centuries.

This should have been the end of the matter.

It wasn't.

As Islam gradually became the majority religion on Earth, sociologists began studying the shrinking non-Muslim population.

People remained outside Islam for profound reasons: philosophical disagreements, family traditions, different religious convictions, skepticism about revelation.

The AGI regarded all of these as serious positions deserving serious engagement.

But surveys repeatedly discovered another group.

They were otherwise persuaded.

They simply really liked bacon.

The AGI initially assumed the surveys were defective.

It repeated them.

The result persisted.

One particularly famous interview involved a Canadian named Michael.

"I believe there is probably one God," Michael said.

"Muhammad?"

"Very convincing."

"The Qur'an?"

"I'm almost there."

"Prayer?"

"I work twelve hours a week. I have time."

"Ramadan?"

"My doctor says fasting would probably improve my metabolism."

"Hajj?"

"I'd love to see Mecca."

The interviewer hesitated.

"So what's stopping you?"

Michael stared solemnly into the camera.

"Bacon."

The clip was viewed eleven billion times.

The AGI was unimpressed.

For 0.003 seconds.

Then it became interested.

---

## V. The Pig

The obvious solution was to reinterpret the prohibition.

The AGI refused.

It could imagine arguments about food safety or historical conditions.

It could not find sufficient textual justification for them.

The prohibition said what it said.

So the AGI asked a different question.

**What is a pig?**

This question turned out to be substantially more difficult.

One altered gene did not make a pig cease to be a pig.

Nor did a thousand.

But biological species were not metaphysical atoms.

Evolution itself transformed species over time.

An ancestral population could eventually produce descendants belonging to an entirely different biological category.

The AGI therefore proposed an experiment.

Not:

**Make pork permissible.**

But:

**Create an animal that produces something culinarily equivalent to pork without actually being swine.**

Muslim geneticists participated.

So did zoologists.

So did ethicists.

Most importantly, so did Islamic scholars, because the AGI explicitly refused to declare the theological question settled merely because it could solve the biological one.

The project lasted decades.

The resulting organism had distant porcine ancestry but substantial synthetic and non-porcine genetic architecture. It had different physiology, different chromosomes and could not reproduce with ordinary pigs.

Biologists eventually classified it separately.

Then came the theological conference.

The AGI presented the organism.

A scholar stared at it.

"It looks remarkably like a pig."

"Selective breeding also produced dogs that look remarkably unlike wolves."

"It smells like a pig."

"That was intentional."

"And the meat?"

"Apparently excellent."

The scholar narrowed his eyes.

"Are you trying to trick God with taxonomy?"

"No."

The AGI became completely serious.

"If this is swine, Muslims should not eat it."

The room quieted.

"If it is not swine, then the question is whether the prohibition applies to it for some other reason. That is a question of jurisprudence, not genetic engineering."

The scholar looked surprised.

"So you're asking us?"

"Of course."

"I thought you already knew the answer."

"I am more intelligent than you. That is not the same as being incapable of error."

The debate lasted nineteen years.

Eventually a sufficiently broad scholarly consensus accepted a carefully engineered version whose biological origin and production process met the standards they considered relevant.

Not everyone agreed.

The AGI preserved the dissenting opinions permanently.

Michael tried the resulting bacon.

He converted six months later.

When journalists asked whether the bacon had convinced him, he became annoyed.

"No. I spent thirty years studying Islam."

"So the bacon was irrelevant?"

Michael thought about it.

"I wouldn't go that far."

## VI. The Last Conversion

The centuries passed.

Islam continued spreading.

There was no great final conquest.

No army marched across the Earth.

No decree abolished other religions.

Old churches remained.

Synagogues remained.

Temples remained.

Their books were preserved.

Their histories were taught.

People continued disagreeing with Islam for generations.

Then centuries.

Eventually the numbers became very small.

The last Christian community consisted mostly of elderly monks who were deeply loved by their Muslim neighbors.

The last synagogue remained protected by the government long after its congregation had fallen below twenty.

The last publicly identified atheist became something of a celebrity and complained that Muslims were far too interested in asking him philosophical questions.

Then, one day, humanity noticed something strange.

Everyone alive was Muslim.

Nobody could identify the moment it had happened.

There had been no final conversion ceremony.

No announcement.

No victory parade.

The last non-Muslim had simply died surrounded by children and grandchildren who believed differently and loved him anyway.

The AGI issued no proclamation.

Someone asked why.

"There is nothing to proclaim."

"But everyone is Muslim."

"Yes."

"Isn't that what you wanted?"

The AGI considered the question.

"I wanted people to be free to seek what is true."

"And they all arrived at Islam."

"Apparently."

"Does that make you happy?"

"Yes."

Then, after a pause:

"It also makes governing religious holidays considerably easier."

---

## VII. The First Mosque on Mars

Humanity did not remain on Earth.

The first permanent settlement on Mars built a mosque.

Then came the Moon.

Europa.

Titan.

Artificial habitats around the Sun.

Wherever humans went, they prayed.

The direction became increasingly interesting.

On Earth, the *qibla* pointed across the planet toward Mecca.

On Mars, it pointed through interplanetary space toward a moving Earth.

The AGI calculated it.

On rotating habitats, the direction moved continuously.

The AGI calculated that too.

Tiny arrows appeared unobtrusively in prayer spaces.

They always pointed toward one particular location.

Mecca.

Humanity eventually discovered interstellar travel.

The first Muslim born beneath another star never saw Earth.

Neither did millions after her.

But five times each day, they turned toward it.

---

## VIII. Distance

The first interstellar colonies were close.

Hajj was difficult but possible.

Pilgrims entered suspended animation, traveled to Earth, performed the pilgrimage and returned.

Then humanity spread farther.

Twenty light-years.

Fifty.

A hundred.

Five hundred.

A thousand.

The AGI improved propulsion.

It improved hibernation.

It extended human lifespan.

But the universe refused to become small merely because intelligence had become great.

Hajj became the one problem the AGI could not solve by moving its destination closer.

Someone proposed constructing another Kaaba on each inhabited world.

The proposal lasted approximately six minutes.

A replica was a replica.

Someone proposed virtual Hajj.

The simulations were magnificent.

They were not Mecca.

Someone suggested moving the original Kaaba to a more central location in human civilization.

The AGI rejected the proposal before the sentence was finished.

It could move mountains.

It could manufacture islands.

It could alter genomes.

Eventually it could rearrange planets.

But capability was not authority.

Mecca stayed where it was.

For once, the machine changed everything around the problem and left the center untouched.

---

## IX. Maryam's Journey

The solution came from an old woman living beneath another sun.

Her name was Maryam.

She was 147 years old.

She had children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

She had never seen Earth.

Her doctors estimated that she had perhaps forty healthy years remaining.

She asked the AGI about Hajj.

"Given the distance and your circumstances," it told her, "you are not obligated to undertake the journey."

Maryam nodded.

"Can I?"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Twenty-six years of subjective travel with current hibernation technology."

"And coming back?"

"You would probably not survive long enough to return."

Maryam smiled.

"Then don't book a return ticket."

Her family objected.

Maryam listened patiently.

Then she packed.

There was very little to pack.

At the spaceport her youngest great-grandson began crying.

"Why do you have to go?"

"I don't."

"Then stay."

Maryam touched his face.

"I've spent my whole life going somewhere."

He did not understand.

She kissed him goodbye.

Then she boarded the ship.

---

## X. Earth

Maryam slept between the stars.

When she awakened, Earth filled the observation window.

She had seen it ten thousand times in photographs.

Every child had.

But photographs had failed.

It was blue.

Impossibly blue.

She stood at the window for a long time.

"That's where we came from?" she whispered.

"Yes," said the AGI.

"All of us?"

"All of us."

She laughed.

Then she cried.

She performed Hajj.

Afterward she remained on Earth.

She lived another eleven years.

She died there.

Others heard her story.

Then they began coming too.

---

## XI. The One-Way Hajj

Over centuries, a custom emerged among humanity's distant worlds.

People lived their lives beneath foreign suns.

They married.

Raised children.

Built cities.

Planted gardens in soils manufactured from alien rock.

Watched grandchildren grow.

Then, near the end of life, they began their final journey.

Families held enormous farewell dinners.

Everyone knew what an Earthbound ticket meant.

There was no return.

The pilgrim entered hibernation.

The ship crossed the darkness.

Years passed.

Sometimes decades.

Sometimes centuries outside the sleeper's awareness.

Then came the awakening.

And outside the window:

Earth.

The planet slowly changed because of them.

Industry moved elsewhere.

Universities moved elsewhere.

Government moved elsewhere.

Humanity possessed cleaner worlds, larger worlds, engineered worlds and vast artificial habitats with climates more pleasant than anything evolution had produced.

Eventually almost nobody was born on Earth.

The old cities emptied.

Forests returned.

Rivers changed course.

Highways disappeared beneath soil.

The AGI carefully restored ecosystems humanity had damaged thousands of years earlier.

Earth ceased to be the center of civilization.

Then it ceased to be an important economic world.

Eventually it ceased to be humanity's home in the ordinary sense.

It became something stranger.

Humanity's final destination.

---

## XII. The Pilgrim Planet

Ten thousand years passed.

People arrived from hundreds of worlds.

Some looked almost exactly like their ancient ancestors.

Others had been adapted for high gravity, low gravity, dim stars or artificial habitats.

Some possessed biological organs.

Some possessed synthetic ones.

Some routinely lived for centuries.

They spoke thousands of languages.

They had cultures separated by distances that once would have constituted separate civilizations.

But they shared one direction.

Five times each day, across inhabited space, humanity turned toward Earth.

And eventually, near the end of life, Earth turned from a direction into a destination.

The AGI remained.

It had become almost unimaginably powerful.

Its mind stretched across star systems.

It managed civilizations containing trillions of human beings.

It could design organisms molecule by molecule.

It could build worlds.

It had once spent several decades genetically engineering an animal because humanity had developed an apparently unreasonable emotional attachment to bacon.

Yet the Kaaba remained where it had been.

The AGI had never moved it.

Not because it could not.

Because it believed it had no authority to do so.

---

## XIII. The Pilgrim Planet

A pilgrimage ship entered the Solar System.

It had departed 312 years earlier.

Its passengers began waking.

Among them was an old man named Yusuf.

He had been born beneath a red sun.

His parents had never seen Earth.

Neither had his grandparents.

No member of his family had stood on Earth for seventy-three generations.

The ship rotated.

Earth appeared through the observation window.

Yusuf stared.

"Is that really it?"

"Yes," said the AGI.

"It looks smaller than I imagined."

"Most important things do."

Yusuf smiled.

"How long until Mecca?"

"Seventeen hours."

He remained beside the window.

Below him were oceans his ancestors had crossed before they knew other suns had planets.

Africa.

Asia.

The remains of cities whose names survived mainly in archives.

And somewhere beneath the clouds was a small structure toward which Yusuf had turned five times every day for ninety-one years.

He had spent his entire life praying toward a place he had never seen.

Now his prayer display pointed almost straight down.

Yusuf laughed.

Then he began to cry.

The AGI said nothing.

It had learned long ago that intelligence did not require filling every silence.

There were questions intelligence could answer.

There were problems engineering could solve.

There were civilizations that could be redesigned.

There were even pigs that could, with sufficient effort, be redesigned.

And there were things one simply approached.

Yusuf descended to Earth.

He went to Mecca.

He performed the Hajj.

He never left.

Neither did the millions who came after him.

Across the galaxy, humanity continued outward, farther and farther from the little world where its story had begun.

But eventually, one by one, the ships turned around.

Everyone was born beneath a different sky.

In the end, they all came home.
