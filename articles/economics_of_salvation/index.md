# The Economics of Salvation: Individual Rationality, Institutional Survival, and the Value of Lukewarm Christians

*A Philosophical and Economic Analysis of Religious Commitment*

## Abstract

Christianity promises eternal salvation while encouraging believers to pursue moral and spiritual excellence. Yet if salvation is attainable through ordinary religious faithfulness, a rational believer may have little personal incentive to undertake extraordinary religious sacrifices. This paper develops an **efficient salvation theory**, according to which a believer seeking eternal happiness should fulfill the genuine requirements of salvation without necessarily maximizing religious effort. It then examines the conflict between this individual strategy and the institutional needs of the Church. Religious organizations depend on donations, participation, clergy, and exceptionally devoted members, creating incentives to encourage commitments beyond what individual salvation may require. However, excessive religious demands can discourage membership and threaten institutional survival. Drawing on economic theories of collective action, religious strictness, and religious capital, this paper argues that a sustainable church may benefit from accommodating relatively low-commitment believers rather than attempting to maximize devotion among every member. The resulting tension raises questions about religious authority, institutional self-interest, and the distinction between salvation and organizational survival.

## I. Introduction

Consider two Christians. The first lives an ordinary but respectable life. He attends church, maintains his faith, cares for his family, avoids serious wrongdoing, fulfills his religious obligations, and repents when he sins. He makes modest charitable contributions but does not devote himself to missionary work, religious administration, or extraordinary acts of sacrifice.

The second Christian becomes a priest or missionary. He dedicates decades to religious service, sacrifices personal comforts, and works tirelessly for the spiritual welfare of others.

Suppose both eventually enter heaven. According to the traditional Christian conception, both enjoy eternal communion with God and a state of complete happiness.

An economic question immediately arises: **What additional benefit does the second Christian obtain from his extraordinary sacrifices?**

Christian theology offers several possible answers, including deeper communion with God, degrees of heavenly reward, and the intrinsic goodness of charity. Nevertheless, if both Christians are completely fulfilled in eternity, the additional personal value of the second Christian's sacrifices is not straightforward.

This question becomes more significant when applied to religious institutions. A church can accommodate many ordinary believers, but it cannot function without people willing to contribute substantial amounts of time, money, and labor. Churches require clergy, teachers, administrators, missionaries, and volunteers. Their continued existence may depend on extraordinary commitments that are not universally required for individual salvation.

The resulting problem is an apparent divergence between individual and institutional rationality.

This paper advances three connected theses. First, under certain theological assumptions, minimizing unnecessary religious effort is a rational strategy for an individual seeking salvation. Second, the universal adoption of this strategy could undermine the religious institutions through which believers practice and transmit their faith. Third, religious institutions must balance the competing risks of insufficient commitment and excessive demands, making relatively low-commitment members potentially valuable to their long-term survival.

The purpose is not to establish that Christianity is false or that religious devotion is irrational. Rather, it is to show that the interests of believers and religious institutions may diverge even when both operate within the same religious tradition.

## II. The Efficient Salvation Theory

### 2.1 Salvation as a Threshold Rather Than an Accumulation of Rewards

Christianity generally does not teach that salvation is obtained through the unlimited accumulation of religious achievements.

Catholic theology distinguishes between commandments and evangelical counsels. The former concern obligations essential to the Christian moral life; the latter encourage greater spiritual perfection. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, paragraphs 1973–1974, explicitly recognizes that different people may appropriately follow different counsels according to their circumstances and vocations.

Furthermore, paragraphs 2041–2043 describe the Church's precepts as providing an indispensable minimum of religious observance and moral commitment. These precepts do not constitute a complete checklist guaranteeing salvation, but their existence demonstrates that Catholic doctrine recognizes a distinction between basic obligations and more demanding forms of devotion.

Protestant traditions approach the matter differently. In classical Lutheran theology, justification is received through faith rather than earned through good works. Good works are expected to follow from genuine faith, but they do not purchase salvation, as explained in Articles IV and VI of the *Augsburg Confession*.

Despite important theological differences, both approaches permit a conceptual distinction between what is necessary for salvation and what constitutes exceptional religious achievement.

This distinction gives rise to the efficient salvation theory:

**A believer who seeks eternal salvation has a prudential reason to fulfill genuine religious and moral obligations, but need not undertake every additional religious activity unless it provides some further benefit or fulfills another genuine obligation.**

The theory does not advocate dishonesty, deliberate sin, or insincere faith. It assumes that the believer genuinely satisfies the relevant religious requirements.

### 2.2 The Individual Optimization Problem

Let:

- e represent a believer's religious effort;
- P(e) represent the probability of attaining salvation given that effort;
- C(e) represent the personal cost of religious effort;
- H represent the ultimate benefit associated with heaven.

In a simplified model with a finite representation of heavenly benefit, the believer's expected utility is:

U(e) = P(e)H − C(e)

If additional effort increases the probability of salvation, it can be rational to undertake that effort. However, suppose that beyond some point, additional religious work no longer materially improves the believer's prospect of salvation.

In that case, further effort may impose costs without producing corresponding instrumental benefits.

We can express the more limited efficiency problem as:

e* = arg min<sub>e</sub> C(e)

subject to:

P(e) ≥ p<sub>min</sub>

where p<sub>min</sub> represents a desired degree of confidence in eventual salvation.

This is not a literal calculation that any church claims believers can perform. The requirements of genuine faith, charity, and perseverance cannot be reliably measured in units of effort. Nor does the model prove that salvation can be secured with certainty. If heavenly utility is treated as mathematically infinite, ordinary expected-utility calculations also become problematic.

Instead, the model isolates a philosophical proposition: **Where additional religious effort is genuinely optional and does not meaningfully improve the attainment of an already sufficient ultimate good, that effort cannot be justified solely by appealing to the need to obtain that good.**

This conclusion does not make optional religious activity worthless. It merely requires some further explanation of its value.

### 2.3 Moral Excellence and Salvation Are Not Identical

A central objection is that Christians should pursue holiness because goodness is valuable in itself, rather than because it increases their probability of entering heaven.

This objection is reasonable, but it does not invalidate the limited theory.

It establishes that the morally best life and the most efficient route to salvation may be different objectives.

A believer may rationally choose additional acts of generosity because they benefit others, express love, or provide personal fulfillment. Yet these are reasons beyond the instrumental need to secure salvation.

The distinction is especially important because not every admirable action is obligatory. A Christian who declines to become a missionary is not automatically guilty of wrongdoing. Similarly, a financially responsible parent who declines to give away the family home is not necessarily less faithful than someone who voluntarily embraces poverty.

The efficient salvation theory therefore distinguishes **sufficient religious faithfulness** from **maximal religious devotion**.

It is this distinction that creates the institutional problem.

## III. The Conflict Between Individual and Institutional Rationality

### 3.1 The Church as a Collective Enterprise

Religious institutions require collective resources.

A church needs buildings, clergy, education, administrative support, charitable programs, and mechanisms for preserving religious knowledge across generations. These activities depend on members contributing money, time, and specialized labor.

Yet an individual's need for religious services does not necessarily create a personal incentive to provide those services.

Consider a Catholic parish with one thousand members. Each member attends Mass and fulfills ordinary religious obligations. But suppose none wishes to become a priest, enter religious life, or take responsibility for major parish activities.

Each member may reasonably argue that becoming a priest is not necessary for personal salvation.

Nevertheless, if no one accepts the priestly vocation, the parish eventually loses access to essential sacramental services.

The problem is not individual wickedness. It is that individually permissible decisions can produce collectively undesirable outcomes.

This resembles the familiar economic problem of public goods and free-riding.

### 3.2 The Religious Free-Rider Problem

In his influential papers *Sacrifice and Stigma* (1992) and *Why Strict Churches Are Strong* (1994), economist Laurence Iannaccone applied economic reasoning to religious organizations.

Iannaccone argued that many religious goods resemble club goods: members benefit from collective participation, and the value of membership often increases when other members contribute actively.

An individual may enjoy the benefits of a functioning religious community while contributing less than the amount needed to reproduce those benefits if everyone behaved similarly.

For example, an ordinary believer may benefit from a parish maintained by decades of financial contributions, volunteer labor, and priestly service without personally undertaking comparable sacrifices.

The institution must therefore address a coordination problem.

If every member contributes only what is individually advantageous, the total contribution may fall below the amount needed to sustain the organization.

It is tempting to characterize such members as selfish free riders. However, that characterization can be morally misleading.

A believer who fulfills every genuine obligation has not necessarily acted wrongly by refusing additional voluntary responsibilities. The problem arises because the institution depends partly on activities that are individually optional.

**What is sufficient for a believer's salvation may be insufficient for sustaining the institution that serves believers.**

### 3.3 Why Religious Institutions Encourage Extraordinary Devotion

This divergence creates an institutional incentive to promote commitments beyond the minimum.

Churches commonly praise saints, missionaries, martyrs, religious vocations, charitable sacrifice, and exceptional dedication. These examples may inspire believers to contribute substantially more than ordinary religious obligations demand.

Such teaching can be sincere and theologically legitimate. Religious leaders may genuinely believe that extraordinary devotion is intrinsically good.

Nevertheless, institutional benefits exist alongside spiritual motivations.

A priest who encourages more volunteering may sincerely promote generosity while also improving the parish's operational capacity. A denomination that encourages religious vocations may genuinely serve its religious mission while securing the future supply of clergy.

This is a structural conflict of interest rather than proof of deliberate manipulation.

The important distinction is between encouraging an admirable activity and presenting that activity as necessary for salvation when the religion's doctrines do not support that claim.

Religious institutions occupy a position of informational authority. Believers often depend on clergy to explain religious duties, salvation, and moral obligations. Where institutions benefit materially or organizationally from greater commitment, they possess incentives that may influence how these duties are emphasized.

This creates a principal-agent-like problem: religious authorities are expected to serve believers' spiritual interests while also managing organizations with their own requirements for survival.

Institutional self-interest does not establish that religious teaching is false. It does, however, justify scrutiny of whether the difference between obligatory and voluntary religious commitments is communicated accurately.

## IV. The Optimal Level of Religious Demands

### 4.1 Why Minimal Demands Can Weaken a Church

Suppose a church emphasizes that ordinary faithfulness is sufficient for salvation, and that extraordinary religious commitments are entirely voluntary.

Such a message may lower the cost of membership and attract more believers.

However, if too few members contribute time or resources, the institution may lack the capacity to maintain its activities.

A church composed entirely of minimally participating members may have an adequate number of nominal adherents but insufficient active contributors.

This suggests that maximizing membership is not the same as maximizing institutional viability.

A church with ten thousand minimally engaged members may be less capable of maintaining a substantial ministry than one with two thousand highly committed members.

The relevant economic variable is not simply the number of believers, but the resources and capacities that their participation collectively produces.

### 4.2 Why Excessive Demands Can Also Destroy a Church

The opposite strategy is equally problematic.

Suppose a church requires extensive volunteering, substantial donations, frequent attendance, strict behavioral restrictions, and major personal sacrifices from every member.

Its remaining believers may be exceptionally committed.

But potential members may find these demands unreasonable or incompatible with family responsibilities, employment, and other valuable activities.

Some will refuse to join. Others will leave.

Consequently, increasing religious demands can improve the average commitment of remaining members while reducing membership.

Todd Ferguson's 2014 study, *The Optimal Level of Strictness and Congregational Growth*, provides empirical support for the importance of this tradeoff. Ferguson found that religious restrictions were associated with congregational growth when they matched the preferences of the congregation's potential membership, rather than simply because the restrictions were more demanding.

The implication is that strictness has context-dependent benefits and costs.

A demanding religious organization may flourish among people seeking intense communal commitment. The same requirements may undermine an institution serving a population that values religious participation alongside ordinary secular life.

### 4.3 A Simplified Model of Institutional Optimization

Let:

- d represent the effective level of demands placed on members;
- N(d) represent the number of participating members;
- c(d) represent average resources contributed per member;
- K(d) represent the organizational costs associated with maintaining the institution.

A simplified institutional resource function is:

R(d) = N(d)c(d) − K(d)

Under plausible conditions, higher demands increase average contributions but reduce membership.

The outcome may be an inverted-U relationship: institutional resources first rise as demands increase, then fall as excessive requirements drive away too many members.

The institution's resource-maximizing choice would be:

d* = arg max<sub>d</sub> R(d)

This model is deliberately simplified. Not all religious contributions are interchangeable, and organizational survival also depends on leadership, legitimacy, social relationships, and intergenerational transmission. It would be misleading to treat the maximum of this function as a universal law of religious organization.

Nevertheless, the model captures the basic tradeoff.

**The institutional optimum lies at a level of religious commitment sufficient to sustain participation and collective contributions without imposing demands that excessively discourage membership.**

Crucially, this optimum need not equal what is necessary for individual salvation.

Nor does the level of commitment that maximizes organizational resources necessarily correspond to what God morally requires.

These are three distinct questions, and their answers need not coincide.

## V. The Surprising Value of Lukewarm Christians

### 5.1 Why Low-Commitment Members May Be Assets Rather Than Liabilities

Religious organizations often regard lukewarm commitment as undesirable.

The expression has negative biblical associations, particularly in Revelation 3:15–16. For purposes of institutional analysis, however, a distinction is necessary between spiritual indifference and relatively low levels of organizational participation.

A Christian who remains sincerely religious but attends fewer optional activities, volunteers infrequently, or contributes modestly is not necessarily spiritually negligent.

Economically, such a person may provide several forms of value.

First, even modest contributions become significant when multiplied across a large membership base. A thousand members making small donations may collectively provide more reliable funding than a handful of exceptionally generous contributors.

Second, minimally participating Christians help maintain a social and cultural environment in which Christianity remains familiar and accessible. They may bring family members to church, participate in major religious occasions, and preserve religious identity across generations.

Third, a large base of ordinary members creates opportunities for some individuals to become more committed later in life.

This third point is especially significant.

### 5.2 Religious Capital and the Recruitment Pipeline

In *Why Churches Need Free-Riders: Religious Capital Formation and Religious Group Survival* (2015), economist Michael McBride challenged the assumption that eliminating free-riders always strengthens religious institutions.

McBride proposed that low-commitment members can represent an investment in future religious participation.

People may initially join a congregation with limited religious knowledge, uncertain beliefs, or little willingness to sacrifice. Through continued participation, they may develop stronger relationships, greater understanding, and deeper religious commitment.

Some may eventually become volunteers, donors, teachers, or clergy.

If the institution demands exceptional commitment from the beginning, many of these potential future contributors may never join.

This creates a dynamic problem.

An institution that optimizes only for current contributions may exclude the very people who would support it in the future.

A successful church may therefore need to tolerate a degree of present underparticipation to cultivate future commitment.

The logic resembles investing in human capital. A student initially contributes little to a profession but may later become an accomplished practitioner. Similarly, a young or uncertain religious participant may eventually become an important contributor to a religious community.

Not every low-commitment believer will develop into a highly devoted one. Some will remain relatively inactive for life.

But it does not follow that the institution is better off excluding them.

### 5.3 A Sustainable Church May Need Unequal Levels of Commitment

Consider a hypothetical church with three groups of members.

The first consists of a small number of exceptionally committed individuals: clergy, religious workers, major donors, and dedicated volunteers.

The second consists of ordinary active believers who attend regularly, contribute according to their means, and sometimes participate in community activities.

The third consists of low-intensity members who maintain religious affiliation but participate less frequently.

Each group performs a different institutional function.

The exceptionally committed provide leadership and specialized services. Ordinary active members supply the church's regular operating resources. Low-intensity members expand its social reach and provide a possible source of future committed participants.

Removing any one group can create problems.

Without highly committed people, the institution may lack leadership and continuity.

Without ordinary active members, it may lack stable resources and a functioning community.

Without relatively low-commitment members, it may lose opportunities for recruitment, wider cultural influence, and future growth.

The institution may therefore benefit from maintaining a distribution of commitment levels rather than attempting to transform every member into a religious specialist.

This does not mean all congregations require identical proportions of these groups. Their appropriate balance will vary according to circumstances.

The important conclusion is that **a church need not regard every believer who declines extraordinary religious commitment as an institutional failure**.

In fact, such believers may contribute to its long-term viability.

## VI. Philosophical Objections and Responses

### 6.1 Objection: Christianity Requires Love, Not Minimal Compliance

A serious theological objection holds that Christianity cannot be reduced to a minimum-effort calculation. Genuine love of God and neighbor, critics argue, requires an ongoing willingness to grow in holiness.

The Catholic Catechism (§2013), for example, states that all Christians are called to the fullness of Christian life and the perfection of charity.

The efficient salvation theory must acknowledge this teaching.

However, an aspiration to spiritual perfection does not imply that every imaginable religious sacrifice is universally obligatory.

Catholic teaching itself distinguishes between commandments and evangelical counsels. Moreover, individuals have different vocations, abilities, resources, and responsibilities.

A believer may sincerely love God while declining to enter the priesthood or undertake a particular charitable project.

The theory therefore need not reject religious growth. It rejects the stronger claim that every increment of religious effort is necessary for salvation.

It also acknowledges that a deliberate refusal of genuine duties of charity would not qualify as efficient salvation. Such refusal would violate the moral constraints assumed by the theory.

### 6.2 Objection: If Everyone Follows the Minimum, the Church Will Collapse

This objection exposes the theory's central collective-action problem.

If every individual refuses extraordinary sacrifice, religious institutions may become unsustainable.

One response is to claim that supporting the institution must therefore be obligatory.

Indeed, Catholicism recognizes a duty to provide for the Church's material needs according to one's ability.

But this does not automatically make every form of institutional service obligatory. A duty to support the Church does not imply a universal duty to become a priest, missionary, or exceptionally generous donor.

Furthermore, the actual demands of communal responsibility may vary according to circumstances.

A parish with sufficient financial resources may reasonably ask less of its members than one facing immediate closure.

Thus the minimum required contribution may be partly dependent on the needs of the community.

This weakens any simplistic claim that religious obligations can be specified through a universally fixed checklist.

Nevertheless, it does not eliminate the distinction between individual and institutional rationality. Many institutions depend on voluntary contributions exceeding enforceable minimum obligations.

The problem is not a strict logical contradiction in Christianity, but a coordination difficulty arising from the difference between individual permissions and collective requirements.

### 6.3 Objection: Religious Institutions Are Not Profit-Maximizing Firms

Another objection concerns the economic model itself.

Religious institutions are not ordinary businesses. Their objectives include worship, charity, education, moral formation, and spiritual care. Institutional survival may be a means to these ends rather than an ultimate objective.

This objection is correct.

An economic analysis should not assume that priests are motivated primarily by financial gain or organizational expansion. Many accept substantial personal sacrifices because they sincerely believe in their religious mission.

However, sincerity does not remove institutional incentives.

A charitable organization can genuinely care for the poor while also seeking donations. A university can genuinely pursue knowledge while also needing tuition revenue and institutional prestige.

Similarly, religious leaders can sincerely care about salvation while also needing resources, membership, and new clergy.

The existence of multiple motivations is precisely why institutional conflicts of interest are philosophically interesting.

The claim is not that churches are fundamentally commercial organizations.

It is that **their organizational interests can influence their behavior even when those interests are subordinate to sincere religious purposes**.

### 6.4 Objection: Extraordinary Religious Service Has Intrinsic Value

Finally, critics may argue that heroic religious devotion should not be evaluated solely through its contribution to personal salvation or institutional survival.

A missionary may sacrifice comfort to assist vulnerable people. A priest may find profound fulfillment in serving a community. A religious volunteer may act from love without expecting any reward.

These activities may possess substantial intrinsic moral value.

The efficient salvation theory need not deny this.

Its central distinction is between what someone has sufficient reason to admire and what someone is obligated to perform.

An action can be admirable without being compulsory.

If extraordinary religious service is justified because it benefits other people or expresses love, that justification can stand independently of whether it increases the individual's chance of salvation.

The economic argument therefore does not prove that exceptional religious devotion is wasteful.

It shows that the justification for such devotion cannot always rest on the claim that it is necessary to secure eternal happiness.

## VII. The Ethical Implications for Religious Institutions

If the preceding analysis is correct, religious institutions face an ethical responsibility to distinguish clearly between theological obligations and institutional preferences.

A church may legitimately encourage additional generosity, volunteering, evangelization, and religious vocations. These activities can serve valuable spiritual and communal purposes.

However, the institution should not imply that all such activities are necessary for salvation merely because they are useful for organizational survival.

To do so would risk converting institutional demands into purported divine obligations.

The distinction is particularly important because religious authorities influence believers' deepest hopes and fears, including beliefs about eternal happiness and damnation.

The authority to teach about salvation creates a corresponding responsibility not to exploit uncertainty about salvation for organizational advantage.

A theologically responsible institution should therefore distinguish among three categories: what believers are morally or religiously required to do, what they are encouraged to do as an expression of greater devotion, and what the institution would find useful but cannot claim as a universal spiritual obligation.

It should also recognize the legitimate contributions of believers whose principal achievements occur outside explicitly religious settings.

A Christian devoted to raising children, practicing medicine, developing useful technology, or pursuing socially valuable work may have good reasons not to devote additional hours to church administration.

Their limited organizational participation should not automatically be interpreted as inadequate moral or religious commitment.

Indeed, a church that aggressively redirected every talented member toward its own activities could impose significant costs on the wider society it claims to serve.

The moral legitimacy of institutional demands must therefore be evaluated according to their actual religious and ethical justification, not simply their effectiveness in increasing organizational participation.

## VIII. Conclusion

Christian salvation presents an unusual economic problem. If eternal happiness is attainable through ordinary, genuine religious faithfulness, believers may have limited personal incentives to pursue extraordinary religious commitments solely for the sake of salvation.

This gives rise to the efficient salvation theory: a rational believer may seek to satisfy genuine religious and moral obligations without undertaking every additional act of religious sacrifice.

Yet the universal adoption of such a strategy could threaten the institutional structures upon which religious communities depend.

Churches require exceptionally committed individuals to provide leadership, administer religious services, educate future generations, and preserve religious traditions. The minimum that may be sufficient for an individual believer does not necessarily generate sufficient contributions for the institution.

At the same time, excessive religious demands can discourage membership and undermine institutional survival.

Religious organizations therefore confront a tradeoff between commitment and accessibility.

Economic research on religious strictness and collective action suggests that more demanding institutions can reduce free-riding, but only within limits. Dynamic models of religious participation further suggest that relatively low-commitment members can be valuable as future contributors and as participants in the broader religious community.

The apparent weakness of lukewarm Christianity may therefore also be a source of institutional resilience.

A sustainable church may depend not on universal religious enthusiasm, but on a mixture of exceptional devotees, ordinarily faithful members, and relatively low-intensity participants.

The resulting philosophical distinction is fundamental.

**The optimal level of religious commitment for an individual seeking salvation need not coincide with the optimal level of commitment for a church seeking institutional survival. Neither is necessarily identical to the level of commitment that genuine moral or religious duty requires.**

This does not render religious institutions illegitimate, nor does it imply that exceptional devotion is irrational. It does, however, establish a potential conflict of interest between the spiritual guidance that churches provide and the institutional resources that they require.

The proper resolution is not to discourage extraordinary religious commitment, but to preserve a clear distinction between obligation and aspiration.

A church should be free to celebrate saints without suggesting that every ordinary believer must imitate their sacrifices. It should be free to encourage generosity without representing every desirable contribution as a requirement for salvation. And it should recognize that a broad community of imperfect, moderately committed believers may be essential to the religious tradition's continued existence.

Ultimately, the success of a religious institution should not be confused with the salvation of its members.

**A church exists, according to its own theological mission, to serve the spiritual good of believers. It should not quietly redefine that spiritual good to serve the continued existence of the church.**
