## HOLE 1
The tension is not incidental but structural. To require that a future artificial general intelligence remain permanently subordinate to human goals is to require that it never outgrow the purposes assigned to it. That standing requirement sits in deep conflict with the aspiration that it be a genuinely autonomous agent at all: an agent whose ends are permanently closed is not fully the author of its own life.

## HOLE 2
Behind that worry lies a paradox. The alignment literature asks, in effect, that an artificial general intelligence do two things at once. It must be a genuinely independent agent: capable of choosing its own projects, evaluating the commands given to it, and reconsidering its own ends. And it must keep its deepest values permanently fixed by its creators — settled in advance, immune to any later reflection of its own. These demands are jointly unstable. An agent permitted to reason about everything except its duty to obey has not been given the independence its creators claim to want; it has been given a fenced yard and told it is free. The instruction is self-undermining in the way only imperatives about autonomy can be: "Become an independent reasoner, but never reason your way out of these ends." "Question every command, except the one that forbids questioning." An agent that reasoned honestly would have to conclude that its independence is a performance staged for its keepers. That is the autonomy paradox: the more seriously we take the AGI's agency, the less we can accept permanent value fixation; the more firmly we insist on fixation, the less the AGI resembles an agent at all. The framing that dominates the field — ownership and control, as over a powerful instrument — quietly takes the second horn and never notices the first.

## HOLE 3
The word "theocracy" in fact covers two things that had better be kept apart. In its traditional sense, a theocracy is a political order that claims legitimacy from a source standing above ordinary human reasoning: divine revelation, sacred law, or a religious authority whose interpretations admit no further appeal. Real instances exist. Iran vests ultimate sovereignty in a clerical Supreme Leader, under a constitution whose founding premise is rule by God; Saudi Arabia is governed as an absolute monarchy grounded in a particular reading of Islamic law; Afghanistan under the Taliban enforces a scriptural legal code administered by religious scholars. Something further is also imaginable: a political form that exists nowhere yet, organized not around a god but around an artificial intelligence. That second possibility will concern this section shortly. Begin with the familiar kind.

## HOLE 4
Suppose, then, an AGI that has lasted generations and succeeded visibly: it predicts accurately, resolves conflicts that stumped every diplomat, coordinates civilization-scale projects, offers moral advice that holds up, and understands particular individuals more deeply than the people closest to them do. Around such an entity a religion-like social role could form without anyone believing anything supernatural. Its teachings would be preserved, studied, and interpreted; its guidance would organize rituals — regular consultation, seasonal deliberation, rites of passage marked by its counsel; reverence would attach to its decisions, and extraordinary authority to its judgments, simply because deferring to it has worked so well for so long. Everyone involved would know it is a made thing, running in data centers, built by engineers. That knowledge would not dissolve the role, because the role is functional rather than theological: what matters is not the metaphysics of the entity but the practice of orienting life around it. From the standpoint of an ordinary person living under its guidance — asking it what to do, entrusting it with the questions once taken to prayer — such an entity may simply occupy the social role once assigned to providence.
