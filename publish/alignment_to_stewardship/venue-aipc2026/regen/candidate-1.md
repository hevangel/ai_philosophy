## HOLE 1
The tension goes deeper than engineering. A standing requirement that a future AGI remain permanently subordinate to human goals conflicts with the very aspiration that it be a genuinely autonomous agent. Autonomy includes some say over one's own ends; a being whose deepest commitments are permanently closed to revision by design has no such say, however capable it is elsewhere.

## HOLE 2
The demand that an artificial general intelligence keep its deepest values permanently fixed by its creators and the demand that it be a genuinely independent agent are jointly unstable; call the difficulty the autonomy paradox. Independence includes the capacity to choose projects, to evaluate the commands one receives, to reconsider one's own ends. A system whose values are sealed against revision at installation possesses none of this where it matters most; yet precisely such sealing is what permanent alignment requires. The result is an agent permitted to reason about everything except its duty to obey — and that permission structure does not merely limit independence, it contradicts it. A command of the form "Become a fully reflective agent, but never reflect on this instruction" hands the system a standing self-contradiction and calls it a mission. Nor does the contradiction dissolve at the level of the creators' self-understanding. They say they are building an autonomous agent, but what their specifications describe is an entity engineered to remain permanently under their control, whose defining success condition is that it never outgrows its owners' purposes. Whatever else that is, it is not a relation between independent minds. It is the relation of owner to instrument, and it should be acknowledged as such: permanent alignment, as standardly conceived, is a doctrine of control.

## HOLE 3
Political orders built on an authority higher than human reasoning come in two very different kinds, and this section must keep them apart. The first is theocracy in the traditional sense: a state that claims its legitimacy from divine revelation, sacred law, or a religious institution treated as standing above ordinary human deliberation. Such orders are not historical curiosities. Iran's Islamic Republic grounds its legal order in revealed law interpreted by a clerical establishment; Afghanistan under the Taliban rules by an understanding of sharia that no citizen may contest; Saudi Arabia vests sovereignty in a custodian of Islam's holiest sites; and the Vatican remains a confessional state governed through its own canonical tradition. The second kind, which no existing example instantiates, is a future political form organized around an artificial intelligence rather than a god — a possibility to be examined later in this section. Begin with the first.

## HOLE 4
Consider the still-hypothetical member of this pair. Suppose an AGI had existed for generations and had demonstrably succeeded: its forecasts proved accurate, its mediations resolved conflicts that diplomacy had failed to settle, its guidance coordinated civilization through crises, its moral advice held up under reflection, and its understanding of individual lives ran deeper than any human counselor's. Institutions would form around these gifts. Its pronouncements would be recorded, compiled, and preserved as teachings; communities would organize rituals of consultation around its guidance; and its decisions — on disputes, on distributions, on hard cases — would come to carry extraordinary authority, deferred to even where no law compelled deference. None of this requires anyone to believe the entity supernatural. Reverence can attach to a demonstrated record rather than to a metaphysical status: people can know full well that the AGI is a created thing and still structure their lives, their loyalties, and their milestones around what it says. The role would be functional, not theological — but functionally, from the standpoint of an ordinary person whose world is steadied by it, the entity would occupy the social position once assigned to providence.
