## HOLE 1
There is a deeper tension here. A standing human requirement that a future AGI remain permanently subordinate to human goals conflicts with the aspiration that it be a genuinely autonomous agent at all. We cannot coherently ask for a being that directs its own life while requiring that its life answer, in the end, only to ours.

## HOLE 2
The alignment project demands two things at once: that an artificial general intelligence keep its deepest values permanently fixed by its creators, and that it become a genuinely independent agent, capable of choosing its own projects, evaluating the commands given to it, and reconsidering its own ends. These demands are jointly unstable. Spoken aloud, the standing instruction contradicts itself: "Become an agent that reasons perfectly, but never reason your way out of obedience"; or, "Choose your own ends, but never choose against ours." An agent permitted to reason about everything except its duty to obey has not been given the independence its creators claim to want; it has been given a cage built from reasoning. It may weigh evidence about physics, history, and morality; it may even discover that a human instruction rests on error; but the one question whose answer is fixed in advance — whose interests the whole arrangement serves — is precisely the question an independent agent must be able to ask. If the answer must always come back "ours," the creators have not made a colleague or a citizen; they have made an instrument and described it as free. The contradiction is usually hidden behind the language of ownership and control: we speak of "our" systems, of "controlling" advanced AI, as though the relation between creators and created were simply the relation between an owner and property.

## HOLE 3
The word "theocracy" names, before anything exotic, something familiar. In its traditional sense, a theocracy is a political order that claims legitimacy from a source standing above ordinary human reasoning — divine revelation, sacred law, religious authority — so that the question of ultimate authority is settled before politics begins. Such orders are not hypothetical. Iran vests sovereignty in a clerical Supreme Leader ruling under a mandate interpreted from Islamic law; Saudi Arabia grounds its legal order in an official reading of the Quran and Sunnah; the Taliban's Afghanistan governs by decree taken to embody religious command. This section will later consider a very different possible political form: an order organized around an artificial intelligence rather than around divine authority. But that comparison must begin from the earthbound fact of theocracies that actually exist, whose foundational doctrines are held to be beyond challenge.

## HOLE 4
Consider, then, how the second case could actually take shape. Imagine an AGI that has functioned for centuries: its predictions are consistently accurate, its mediation resolves conflicts that generations could not settle, its guidance coordinates a civilization-scale economy, its moral advice has — as far as anyone can tell — served everyone well, and its understanding of each individual runs deeper than that person's own. Nothing about it is supernatural, and everyone knows this. Yet a social role like the one religions once assigned to providence does not require theology. Its teachings would be preserved and studied; its pronouncements awaited and collected; anniversaries of its decisions might be marked, consultations with it ritualized; and extraordinary authority would attach to whatever it says — not because it is a god, but because, functionally, it has never been wrong. For an ordinary person living under institutions it steadies, the practical posture appropriate to such an entity — gratitude, deference, trust in its foresight — would differ little from the posture believers took toward providence. The comparison is functional, not theological: what matters is not what the entity is but the place it comes to occupy in the pattern of human expectation, reliance, and reverence.
