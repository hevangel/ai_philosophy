# The Summit and the Climb: Automation, Creative Value, and the Democratization of Human Agency

### A Reply to Jordan MacKenzie on AI and Valuing Practices

## Abstract

Jordan MacKenzie argues that generative artificial intelligence poses two existential threats to human valuing practices. Its forward-looking threat consists in the displacement of meaningful creative processes by merely functional products; its backward-looking threat consists in revealing that audiences do not share creators' commitments to those processes. This paper challenges both arguments through a distinction between constitutive and instrumental difficulty, an account of evaluative independence, and a critique of the social authority attached to scarce creative skills. The coexistence of the Grouse Grind hiking trail and mechanized access to Grouse Mountain illustrates why automating the means to an objective need not diminish the value of pursuing that objective without automation. Cases involving culinary technology, fan fiction, interpersonal communication, and literary reception demonstrate that human agency may be preserved, transformed, or enlarged when established production methods become optional. Drawing on Foucault's analysis of the author-function and Bourdieu's account of cultural distinction, I further argue that declining exclusivity must be distinguished from declining intrinsic value. MacKenzie's concerns identify possible failures of technological implementation and institutional adaptation, but do not establish that generative AI uniquely or generally impoverishes human valuing. The appropriate philosophical presumption is not that traditional practices ought to be preserved unchanged, but that technological replacement should be assessed by its effects on the values those practices serve.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; creative agency; aesthetics; automation; technological progress; process value; Foucault; Bourdieu; valuing practices

---

## 1. Introduction

In North Vancouver, British Columbia, visitors to Grouse Mountain can ascend by two radically different methods. They can climb the Grouse Grind, a steep 2.5-kilometre trail consisting largely of stairs, rocks, and demanding uphill terrain. Alternatively, they can reach the mountain by aerial tramway or gondola, arriving without the considerable physical exertion required by the trail.

The distinction is philosophically instructive.

Consider two visitors. The first wishes to challenge herself physically, improve her fitness, and experience the satisfaction of completing a demanding ascent. The second wishes to enjoy the mountain scenery, dine at the summit, and admire the view of Vancouver. Both seek something valuable, but the sources of that value differ.

The first visitor would defeat her own purpose by taking the gondola. The second would gain little relevant to her objective by insisting on walking.

Neither visitor misunderstands the mountain. Neither necessarily values it more deeply. They simply pursue different goods that previously might have been inseparable.

Crucially, the availability of mechanized transportation does not invalidate the accomplishment of climbing. The Grouse Grind remains a demanding physical challenge even though the summit is accessible by other means. The technology permits visitors to distinguish the value of the destination from the value of the journey.

This example illuminates a fundamental issue in contemporary debates about generative artificial intelligence.

In *Fear and Loathing in Silicon Valley: Does AI Threaten Our Valuing Practices?*, Jordan MacKenzie (2026) argues that generative AI poses two existential threats to human valuing. The first is forward-looking: automation may displace meaningful human processes and leave us with products that possess functional value but lack the significance derived from their creation. The second is backward-looking: AI may reveal that audiences and other participants never shared the values creators attached to their activities.

MacKenzie's analysis identifies genuine features of human valuing. We sometimes appreciate objects because of their qualities, and sometimes because of their histories. Creative effort can express personal identity, establish relationships, and provide intrinsic satisfaction. Furthermore, discovering that others do not share our commitments can be painful.

The disagreement concerns the conclusions drawn from these observations.

The present paper advances three central claims.

First, the displacement of a historically necessary production method does not establish the destruction of the values previously realized through that method. A distinction must be drawn between *instrumental difficulty*, which is endured as a means to an end, and *constitutive difficulty*, which forms part of the end itself.

Second, the value of creating an object and the value of experiencing it need not coincide. Audiences may legitimately assess literary or artistic works without sharing their creators' commitment to the processes of production. Differences in valuation do not automatically constitute defective recognition or existentially troubling disagreement.

Third, the democratization of a previously scarce capability may diminish the social prestige associated with possessing it without diminishing its intrinsic value. Foucault's account of authorship and Bourdieu's theory of cultural distinction provide resources for examining whether established hierarchies of creative expertise are sometimes mistaken for natural hierarchies of value.

These arguments do not establish that every application of AI is beneficial. Nor do they imply that human creative activities are incapable of being harmed. Rather, they challenge the inference from technological displacement to value destruction and locate the burden of justification where it belongs: upon the claim that a particular transformation damages something worth preserving.

The philosophical question is not whether technology eliminates effort. It is whether the effort eliminated was valuable in precisely the respect that technology is alleged to threaten.

## 2. The Displacement Fallacy: Confusing a Means with a Value

MacKenzie distinguishes qualitative valuing from process valuing. In qualitative valuing, an object is appreciated for its features or functions. In process valuing, its history and the activities through which it came into being contribute to its significance.

This distinction is useful, and the present argument does not reject it.

The problem arises when the replacement of a production process is interpreted as the disappearance of the values associated with it.

Call this the *displacement fallacy*: the inference from the elimination or automation of a historically established means to the elimination of the goods that means once realized.

The inference is invalid without additional premises.

Consider three propositions:

1. A human activity involves a particular form of effort.
2. A technological development makes that effort unnecessary for obtaining the activity's traditional output.
3. Consequently, something valuable about the activity has been lost.

The third proposition does not follow from the first two.

Whether it follows depends on why the effort mattered.

A distinction is therefore needed between two categories of difficulty.

**Instrumental difficulty** consists in obstacles that must be overcome because of the limitations of available means. The obstacle is not part of the desired objective. Its removal is ordinarily beneficial, provided the same objective can be achieved without introducing greater costs.

**Constitutive difficulty** consists in effort that is integral to the activity's value. Overcoming the difficulty is itself part of what the participant seeks.

The distinction is relative to the participant's objective rather than fixed by the physical description of the task.

On Grouse Mountain, the climb is constitutive for the hiker who seeks physical achievement. It is instrumental for a visitor whose sole objective is to reach the summit and enjoy the scenery.

The gondola eliminates the second visitor's instrumental difficulty without eliminating the first visitor's constitutive difficulty.

Indeed, the coexistence of both options improves the situation of each. The hiker can continue climbing, while the sightseer need not undertake an unwanted physical ordeal.

The more general principle is this:

*When technology makes a historically necessary means optional, it need not destroy the value of that means. It can instead separate values that were previously combined by necessity.*

This is a significant expansion of practical freedom.

Before a demanding activity can be automated, individuals may have no choice but to perform it, regardless of whether they value the effort involved. After automation, those who value the effort can continue undertaking it voluntarily, while those who seek only the outcome can avoid it.

The resulting differentiation of activities need not flatten the landscape of human values. It may enrich that landscape by making previously inseparable objectives independently accessible.

Of course, the continued availability of constitutive activities cannot always be assumed. Institutions may withdraw opportunities, and communities may change their norms. But these are further propositions requiring argument. They do not follow from the technical possibility of automation.

MacKenzie's forward-looking concern therefore requires more than the observation that AI replaces labor. It requires an account of why the relevant labor is constitutive rather than instrumental, and why its value cannot adequately survive under the new technological arrangement.

## 3. From Wood Fires to Gas Stoves: Why Progress Need Not Diminish Skill

The history of cooking provides a second illustration.

A skilled cook working over a traditional wood fire must control heat by managing fuel, airflow, combustion, and the placement of cookware. Such techniques can demand considerable expertise.

Modern gas stoves permit much more immediate adjustment of heat. The cook no longer needs to master all the techniques associated with maintaining a wood fire.

Does the elimination of these skills make the modern cook less of a chef?

Not necessarily.

If the chef's objective is culinary excellence, more precise heat control may improve the ability to realize that objective. The chef can devote greater attention to ingredients, seasoning, texture, timing, and presentation. The disappearance of some traditional skills can accompany an expansion of others.

This observation does not entail that gas cooking is superior in every culinary respect. Wood fires can contribute distinctive flavors and cooking conditions. Where such characteristics matter, chefs may retain traditional techniques or employ additional methods, including controlled smoking and specialized equipment, to achieve some of the desired effects.

The relevant lesson is not that every traditional technique is fully reproducible through modern equipment. It is that culinary value is plural, and technological development can permit more precise selection among its components.

The cook who values controlling a wood fire may continue practicing that art. The cook who values producing a particular dish need not preserve fire-management difficulties merely to demonstrate culinary legitimacy.

There is therefore no general principle according to which the replacement of a difficult technique reduces the value of the activity in which it was historically employed.

The same distinction applies to creative production.

A writer may value the painstaking construction of sentences. Another may value the development of characters, philosophical ideas, or narrative possibilities. A third may be interested primarily in communicating an insight clearly.

AI can automate aspects of composition that are constitutive for the first writer but instrumental for the second and third.

To declare all three impoverished because they employ AI would impose one participant's conception of creative value upon others who have different objectives.

MacKenzie acknowledges that AI can extend human agency, allowing individuals to realize creative intentions despite limitations in traditional skills (2026, §2.3). Yet the possibility is more consequential than her threat-centered analysis allows.

The redistribution of labor can be a genuine transformation of creative agency rather than merely a reduction in its quantity.

A modern electronic engineer who employs automated design tools does not thereby cease to exercise engineering judgment. Architectural decisions, constraints, verification, and the evaluation of trade-offs can remain substantial intellectual achievements even when software performs operations once executed manually.

Similarly, an AI-assisted writer may exercise agency through conception, selection, direction, criticism, revision, and final judgment.

The extent of that agency is not determined simply by counting the words personally typed.

This suggests a more appropriate criterion:

*The significance of a creative process should be assessed by the agency, understanding, expression, and achievement it realizes, rather than by the volume of historically familiar labor it preserves.*

Anthony Cross's account of AI as a participant in an exploratory artistic practice (2025) provides a related way of understanding creative engagement beyond the simple tool-versus-replacement dichotomy.

The central distinction is not between unaided humans and AI users. It is between meaningful and unmeaningful forms of engagement, however technologically mediated.

## 4. Advertising and Fan Fiction: Two Different Economies of Creativity

MacKenzie's introductory example concerns Marshall, a talented storyteller whose work producing advertorials becomes largely a matter of prompting an AI system and editing the resulting material.

Marshall experiences this transformation as a loss. His work no longer affords the same opportunity to exercise his storytelling abilities.

His disappointment is understandable.

Nevertheless, advertorial production is a questionable basis for general conclusions about the intrinsic value of creative activity.

The principal purpose of an advertorial is commercial persuasion. Its employer generally commissions the text to achieve an external objective, while its audience may value the information only insofar as it helps identify relevant products or services.

MacKenzie explicitly recognizes that most readers do not appreciate advertorials as products of distinctive creative processes. Indeed, this divergence between Marshall's personal valuation and the instrumental interests of his employer is central to her backward-looking argument.

But this makes Marshall's situation less mysterious, not more.

Marshall values a personally meaningful activity that happens to be embedded in commercial employment. His employer values an economically useful output. There is no antecedent reason to expect these valuations to coincide.

The important question is whether his personal satisfaction creates a claim that the employer or audience should preserve the traditional process.

The mere existence of that satisfaction cannot establish such a claim.

Consider instead a fan-fiction writer.

She spends evenings writing stories featuring characters from a fictional universe she loves. She may receive no financial compensation. Her readership may be small. Her motivation arises primarily from the pleasure of imagination, storytelling, and participation in a community.

Fan-fiction scholarship has emphasized the importance of gift exchange and noncommercial participation within fandom communities (Hellekson 2009).

The writer's activity is valuable to her even if no publisher purchases the resulting work and no external institution recognizes her achievement.

Now introduce generative AI.

Several possibilities emerge.

The writer may reject AI because she loves constructing every sentence herself. Alternatively, she may use it to develop plot possibilities, revise dialogue, examine continuity, or explore alternative characterizations. She may also decide to direct interactive stories in which AI produces much of the prose.

These activities are different. Yet each can be intrinsically enjoyable.

The mere availability of automated storytelling does not compel the conclusion that the writer's original reasons for participating have disappeared.

If she values traditional literary composition, she may continue composing. If she values imaginative exploration, AI may increase the range of ideas she can explore. If she enjoys collaboration, the technology may provide an additional form of participation.

Indeed, AI can lower barriers for individuals with limited command of written language, little access to professional editing, or insufficient time to master specialized production techniques.

Such individuals may gain access to valuable creative activities in which they would otherwise have participated only minimally, or not at all.

The change is not necessarily from rich human creativity to impoverished automated consumption. It may be from restricted creative participation to broader creative opportunity.

An objection remains: AI-generated material could overwhelm a fan-fiction community, reducing feedback, reciprocity, and the attention available to traditional writers.

This is possible, but it concerns the institutional conditions governing participation. A recommendation system that rewards mass-produced material, for example, creates a different problem from a writing assistant that helps an author realize a personally meaningful narrative.

Communities can distinguish between interactive AI storytelling, AI-assisted writing, and traditional composition. They may organize separate spaces or adopt disclosure conventions when those distinctions matter to participants.

Such arrangements need not preserve all existing practices unchanged. They can instead support coexistence among different creative aims.

The fan-fiction example demonstrates a general limitation in MacKenzie's argument: the technical ability to replace a task does not establish that intrinsically motivated participants will cease valuing or performing it.

Automation changes what is possible. Human purposes help determine what is worth doing.

## 5. Personal Expression: The Problem Is Sincerity, Not Technology

MacKenzie offers cases involving love letters, children's artwork, and gifts to demonstrate the relational significance of production history.

These examples establish an important point: the value of an object is not always exhausted by its observable qualities.

They do not, however, establish that meaningful personal expression requires unaided production.

Suppose a daughter wishes to send her father a poem.

She might compose one herself. She might copy a poem from a published collection. Or she might ask an AI system to generate a poem expressing what she wishes to communicate.

The relational significance of these acts cannot be determined from their production methods alone.

If she copies a poem without reading it, understanding it, or considering its suitability, her gesture may express little personal attention.

But if she thoughtfully selects a published poem because it captures something she sincerely wishes to say, the act of selection may itself be meaningful.

The same reasoning applies to an AI-generated poem.

A thoughtless prompt followed by an unread output may reflect minimal personal engagement. An extended process of supplying memories, evaluating alternatives, rejecting inappropriate expressions, and deliberately adopting the result may reflect considerable attention and care.

In either case, the relevant questions concern sincerity, intention, and the nature of the interpersonal act.

The difference cannot be reduced to whether the words originated in a human mind or a machine.

MacKenzie's own example of an aunt giving away a treasured clock illustrates the point. The aunt did not manufacture the clock. Its relational value arose from her attachment to it and her decision to give it away.

The recipient appreciates the aunt's action, not the aunt's technical competence as a clockmaker.

The example therefore supports a distinction between *producing an object* and *using an object to express an intention*.

A person can meaningfully express affection through an object made by another person. There is no general reason to deny that possibility merely because the object was generated by AI.

This does not imply that deception is harmless. If a recipient was led to believe that a poem represented hours of personal composition, discovering that it did not may reasonably alter the recipient's judgment of the sender's honesty or involvement.

But this problem predates generative AI.

Insincere love letters, copied expressions of affection, purchased gifts, and falsely represented effort have long been possible.

AI may reduce the cost of such behavior, but lowering its cost does not make the technology itself the constitutive source of insincerity.

If the claim is that AI substantially increases insincere communication, evidence about actual communication practices is required.

The existence of an easier means is not itself evidence that the underlying intention has become less sincere.

## 6. The Value of Making and the Value of Experiencing

MacKenzie's backward-looking argument rests upon a difference between the values of creators and those of their audiences.

A creator may regard a work as the expression of years of effort, skill, and imagination. An audience may judge it principally by the enjoyment, insight, or emotional experience it provides.

MacKenzie suggests that the discovery of this difference can produce alienation. Creators may find that others never shared their understanding of what made the activity worthwhile.

But a further distinction is necessary.

The value of making a work and the value of encountering it are not identical objects of evaluation.

A musician may enjoy practicing a difficult composition. A listener may enjoy hearing it.

A chef may take satisfaction in mastering a culinary technique. A diner may judge the resulting meal by its flavor.

A fan-fiction writer may value the imaginative activity involved in constructing a narrative. A reader may value the narrative for its humor, characterization, or emotional significance.

There is no general requirement that the audience reproduce the creator's evaluative commitments.

Indeed, differences between productive and receptive interests are commonplace and often beneficial.

Call this the *principle of evaluative independence*:

*The intrinsic value of producing a work does not ordinarily depend upon an audience valuing the process of production, and the audience's legitimate appreciation of a work does not ordinarily require it to share the creator's reasons for making it.*

The qualification matters. Some works are explicitly offered as gifts, personal communications, or collaborative achievements in which the manner of production is part of what recipients reasonably value.

Nevertheless, the default assumption that creators and audiences must value the same processes is unwarranted.

An audience can admire a difficult artistic achievement without preferring the resulting work. Conversely, it can prefer a work without regarding its production as especially impressive.

These are not contradictory judgments.

The distinction becomes especially clear under controlled evaluation.

Imagine a literary experiment in which readers encounter three anonymous stories: one written without AI, one developed through extensive human–AI collaboration, and one generated with minimal human involvement.

Suppose participants are instructed to assess narrative coherence, dialogue, pacing, emotional effect, and literary style, without knowing the stories' origins. The experimental design also prevents the administrators who collect the ratings from inadvertently revealing authorship information.

If the AI-generated story performs best, the legitimate conclusion concerns the specified qualities of the reading experience.

It does not follow that the AI work represents greater human achievement. But neither may the human-written story be declared aesthetically superior merely because it required greater effort.

Doing so would substitute an evaluation of production for an evaluation of reception.

This is not a claim that all aesthetic value can be determined through blind assessment.

Kendall Walton (1970) argues that the artistic categories under which a work is perceived can affect its aesthetic properties. Historical context, genre, and production circumstances may therefore be relevant to some legitimate judgments of art.

The proper lesson is more precise: information about production should influence an evaluation only insofar as it is relevant to the value being assessed.

A literary competition may assess work-immanent qualities; a craftsmanship award may properly assess technique; a historical exhibition may properly emphasize authenticity.

There is no need to collapse these different evaluative purposes into a single ranking of works.

This also reveals a limitation in MacKenzie's use of empirical research on AI-generated poetry.

## 7. What the Poetry Experiment Does Not Establish

Porter and Machery (2024) found that nonexpert participants often preferred AI-generated poems to poems written by established human poets. Their research also found that poems received lower ratings when participants were told they were AI-generated rather than human-authored, regardless of actual authorship.

The results demonstrate the importance of distinguishing the experienced qualities of a poem from expectations surrounding its origin.

MacKenzie discusses these findings in connection with her backward-looking threat. She imagines a poet discovering that audiences prefer accessible AI-generated poems to the more complex works into which human poets have invested considerable effort.

The discovery could certainly disappoint the poet.

But what does it establish?

Let \(H\) represent a human-written poem, \(M\) an AI-generated poem, and \(A\) a particular audience member.

Suppose the audience member prefers the experience of reading \(M\):

\[
V_{\mathrm{reading}}(A,M)>V_{\mathrm{reading}}(A,H).
\]

Nothing in that preference entails that the same person assigns no value to the human creative achievement represented by \(H\).

It is entirely possible that:

\[
V_{\mathrm{achievement}}(A,H)>0.
\]

Indeed, the reader might admire the poet's effort while preferring a different poem.

The two judgments are logically compatible.

Consequently, an audience's preference for an AI-generated text under specified aesthetic criteria cannot establish its indifference to human creative labor.

The empirical inference would require additional evidence about participants' attitudes toward creative processes.

The observed authorship-framing effect is also relevant. Participants' ratings changed when they believed a poem had a different origin. This does not establish that the change was necessarily justified, but it undermines any simple assumption that readers are generally indifferent to authorship.

A second difficulty concerns the description of readers' preferences as shallow.

A poet may value complexity, ambiguity, and interpretive difficulty. A reader may value clarity, emotional immediacy, and effective communication.

The latter values are not self-evidently inferior.

Complexity can be aesthetically valuable, but so can simplicity. The achievement of expressing an insight concisely may be as demanding and artistically significant as constructing a work that permits many interpretations.

An argument for the superiority of one aesthetic ideal must therefore identify its relevant merits.

It cannot merely appeal to the labor necessary to realize it or the prestige attached to the institutions that cultivate it.

The disagreement between a poet and an audience may reveal genuine pluralism in aesthetic values rather than the shallowness of one participant's appreciation.

The experiment reveals what a particular population preferred under specified conditions. It does not establish what all competent readers ought to prefer, and it does not establish a general failure to appreciate human creativity.

## 8. The Poet and the Cup Stacker: Recognition Without Entitlement

Consider two individuals who devote twenty years to mastering their respective activities.

One is a poet who has developed extraordinary skill in meter, imagery, and literary composition.

The other is a competitive cup stacker who has developed exceptional speed, coordination, and precision in stacking and unstacking cups.

Both activities may demand sustained practice. Both may provide personal fulfillment. Both may support communities of enthusiasts who recognize achievement.

Yet the social prestige associated with the two activities may differ substantially.

The poet may regard literary achievement as a major cultural contribution. The cup stacker may recognize that most people outside the sport take little interest in competitive stacking.

Does the poet's greater expectation of recognition establish an entitlement to it?

No such entitlement follows simply from the length of training or the difficulty of the achievement.

A defense of poetry's superior cultural value would require independent reasons concerning its aesthetic, expressive, cognitive, or social significance.

Those reasons might be available. The present argument does not assume that every activity is equally valuable. It denies that difficulty alone establishes a hierarchy.

Nor does the relative obscurity of cup stacking render the stacker's activity meaningless. Someone who loves the sport may continue practicing without seeking broad cultural recognition.

The same possibility remains available to the poet.

This distinction challenges the normative force of MacKenzie's backward-looking threat.

A poet whose work loses social prestige may experience disappointment, diminished income, or damage to a professional identity.

These losses can be substantial. But the reduction of recognition does not, by itself, establish that the intrinsic value of composing poetry has decreased.

Nor does it establish that an audience has wronged the poet by changing its preferences.

We should distinguish three propositions:

First, people may reasonably desire recognition for activities central to their identities.

Second, recognizing the significance of another person's commitment does not require sharing that person's assessment of its aesthetic or cultural superiority.

Third, the existence of a desire for recognition does not create an unrestricted entitlement to public admiration, professional status, or continued demand.

The first proposition is compatible with rejecting the third.

A person can sincerely love poetry while also wishing for an audience. There is no need to portray every desire for recognition as evidence of insincerity.

Nevertheless, the recognition of intrinsic creative value must not be conflated with an obligation to preserve established structures of cultural prestige.

The poet may continue writing for the love of poetry, participating in communities that share the relevant values, and seeking readers who appreciate the work.

The wider public remains free to prefer different forms of cultural participation.

What MacKenzie describes as valuing discord may therefore consist, in some cases, in the painful discovery that one's preferred activity does not occupy the privileged social position one believed it did.

Such a discovery can be psychologically significant without establishing a philosophical defect in the audience's valuation.

## 9. Foucault, Bourdieu, and the Cultural Authority of Difficulty

The relationship between creative expertise and cultural prestige introduces a further dimension of the debate.

Michel Foucault's *What Is an Author?* (1969; English translation 1977) examines how authorship functions within systems of discourse. Foucault challenges the assumption that the author is merely a natural, self-evident origin of textual meaning. The social role assigned to authors varies across historical and institutional contexts.

This does not imply that individual creators are fictitious or that their contributions are irrelevant.

Rather, it suggests that the criteria through which societies recognize and authorize creative activity are historically structured.

The emergence of generative AI provides an opportunity to reconsider those criteria.

Traditionally, successful participation in literary culture has required access to particular forms of training, linguistic competence, and technical skill. Publishers, universities, competitions, and other cultural institutions help determine which competencies receive recognition.

Generative AI can make some of those capabilities accessible to individuals who lack the corresponding conventional training.

A person with an original philosophical argument but limited proficiency in academic English may use AI to express it clearly. A storyteller with limited ability to draw may use AI to develop visual narratives. A technically trained individual may employ AI to explore artistic forms previously inaccessible without extensive additional education.

These developments unsettle the relationship between the production of culturally recognized work and possession of the traditional skills associated with its production.

A Foucauldian question is therefore appropriate: which aspects of established authorship practices protect genuine intellectual and artistic value, and which primarily define the boundaries of legitimate participation?

This question does not establish that traditional standards are arbitrary. It requires those standards to be justified by the values they serve rather than assumed to be intrinsically authoritative.

Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979; English translation 1984) makes the issue of prestige more explicit.

Bourdieu examines how cultural tastes and competencies function as markers of social distinction. Cultural preferences are not merely private aesthetic judgments; they are also embedded in structures of education, status, and social hierarchy.

This framework offers an alternative interpretation of anxieties surrounding the democratization of creative capabilities.

Suppose a particular skill once required years of specialized training and was therefore possessed by relatively few people.

Its scarcity could support multiple forms of value: personal accomplishment, functional excellence, economic reward, and social distinction.

Technological development may reduce the scarcity without eliminating the other forms of value.

Returning to Grouse Mountain, the gondola makes the summit accessible to people who cannot or do not wish to complete the Grouse Grind.

The hiker's physical accomplishment remains demanding. But reaching the summit is no longer an exclusive distinction belonging only to those who undertake the climb.

A loss of exclusivity has occurred without a corresponding loss in the intrinsic value of hiking.

Analogously, AI may reduce the exclusivity of producing grammatically polished prose, sophisticated illustrations, or certain forms of literary content.

Those who possess traditional skills may consequently lose some of the positional advantages associated with them.

But a reduction in positional advantage is not equivalent to a reduction in artistic or intellectual excellence.

Bourdieu's framework also cautions against treating elite aesthetic preferences as neutral measurements of objective cultural value.

If established literary institutions favor difficult, allusive poetry while ordinary readers prefer accessible and emotionally direct verse, the difference may partly reflect divergent cultural training and social expectations.

This possibility does not demonstrate that the institutions are wrong. It does challenge any assumption that their preferences require no defense.

A rigorous evaluation must distinguish actual aesthetic merits from the prestige attached to possessing the competence required to recognize or produce them.

The resulting critique of MacKenzie is structural, not personal.

There is no need to attribute a desire for exclusivity or professional self-protection to MacKenzie herself.

The issue is whether an account of threatened valuing practices adequately distinguishes the disappearance of valuable activities from the erosion of social distinctions historically associated with those activities.

Foucault and Bourdieu make that distinction especially important.

They invite us to ask whether the apparent degradation of creativity sometimes consists instead in a redistribution of creative authority.

**The democratization of a capability is not, without further argument, the destruction of its value.**

## 10. The Strongest Objection: What If Automation Undermines the Capacity to Value?

A defender of MacKenzie may concede much of the preceding argument while maintaining a more subtle concern.

The Grouse Grind survives because the relevant activity remains available and people continue to understand its appeal.

But what if a technological environment changes the abilities and dispositions of future participants?

Suppose children increasingly rely on AI to compose stories, develop arguments, and communicate complex ideas. They may fail to acquire skills through which earlier generations discovered the intrinsic pleasures of writing.

Later, even if traditional composition remains technically possible, they may lack both the competence and the motivation to pursue it.

Under these conditions, the survival of a practice as an abstract option would not guarantee the survival of its value in lived experience.

This is an important objection because preferences themselves can be shaped by technological environments.

However, the argument remains incomplete.

It assumes that the relevant capacities can be acquired only, or best, through methods resembling traditional unaided composition.

That assumption requires defense.

An educational system might use AI to provide immediate criticism, offer alternative arguments, identify weaknesses, and encourage students to compare competing interpretations. Such interaction could, under appropriate conditions, develop intellectual capacities that conventional instruction develops less effectively or less accessibly.

The crucial educational question is therefore not whether a student used AI, but whether the activity developed the intended competence.

If the goal is independent argumentation, an exercise may reasonably require students to construct arguments without assistance. If the goal is critical evaluation, carefully designed AI dialogue may be an excellent instrument.

These are pedagogical decisions concerning learning objectives.

The mere existence of an automation tool does not determine how it should be integrated into education.

Nor does it establish that intellectual development will necessarily decline.

A second objection concerns the crowding out of valuable human production.

MacKenzie worries that cheap automated products may displace superior alternatives from the market, encouraging society to accept the merely adequate.

This possibility cannot be dismissed simply by observing that traditional production remains technically possible.

A practice may depend on economic support, distribution channels, specialized materials, educational institutions, or a sufficiently large community of participants.

However, market displacement does not establish the intrinsic inferiority of the new technology.

It identifies a problem involving the incentives and institutions through which the technology is deployed.

If recommendation algorithms reward cheaply generated material irrespective of quality, better discovery systems and more discriminating recommendation criteria may improve the situation.

If educational uses encourage passive dependence, assessments can be redesigned around understanding, criticism, and demonstrable competence.

If a fan-fiction community values direct interpersonal participation, it can establish practices distinguishing different forms of authorship and contribution.

These responses are not guaranteed to succeed. Their relevance lies in showing that technological capability and institutional implementation are analytically distinct.

The concern cannot be evaluated adequately without considering possible redesigns and adaptations.

Moreover, any comparison of lost opportunities must include the opportunities created.

People previously excluded from advanced artistic or literary production may gain access through AI. Their creative development and satisfaction count as human values no less than those of established professionals.

A complete assessment must therefore consider the distribution of opportunities before and after technological change, rather than focusing exclusively on the experiences of individuals whose existing practices are displaced.

The appropriate question is comparative: which technological and institutional arrangement best supports the relevant human goods?

## 11. Technological Progress and the Burden of Proof

MacKenzie is careful not to claim that generative AI inevitably destroys meaningful creative activity. Her argument identifies risks, including the possibility that users will gradually surrender agency through excessive delegation.

The present critique should not be understood as denying the logical possibility of such outcomes.

Instead, it challenges the philosophical significance assigned to their possibility.

Virtually every consequential technology changes the activities through which people exercise skill, acquire identity, and participate in communities.

Printing altered the social function of scribal copying. Typewriters transformed the physical practices of writing. Computer-aided design changed engineering work. Digital photography changed photographic production and access.

Each development made certain forms of labor less necessary while enabling others.

That familiar pattern is not sufficient to establish that all technological developments are equally benign. Generative AI may differ in scope, speed, and versatility.

But identifying its novelty does not dispense with the need to demonstrate why its effects are damaging.

A principled evaluation should adopt a *burden-of-proof requirement for displacement claims*:

**When technological automation replaces an established method, a claim that the replacement destroys value must identify the relevant value, explain why that value depends on the displaced method, and provide reasons to believe that the loss will be sufficiently significant and resistant to adaptation.**

This principle does not demand certainty before taking risks seriously.

Credible mechanisms of serious harm can justify precautionary measures even where their probability is uncertain.

Nor does the principle absolve technological developers of obligations concerning safety, honesty, accessibility, or the foreseeable effects of their products.

Rather, it rejects the implicit presumption that established methods possess normative priority simply because they are established.

The relevant comparison is not between a valuable past and an imagined technological future stripped of all beneficial adaptations.

It is between feasible arrangements, including arrangements in which new technology is improved in response to observed problems.

This suggests a form of *technological corrigibility*.

When a technological innovation produces undesirable consequences, the first philosophical question should concern the mechanisms producing those consequences and whether those mechanisms can be altered while preserving the innovation's benefits.

The answer may require better tools, different incentives, revised institutions, or combinations of these.

There is no general reason to assume that restoring the former technological constraint is the uniquely appropriate response.

The strongest possible version of technological optimism would maintain that every problem caused by technology can ultimately be solved through additional technology.

That universal thesis is not necessary for the present argument, and establishing it would require considerably more than the examples considered here.

A more defensible principle is that technological problems are frequently open to further technical and institutional solutions, and that claims of unavoidable value destruction must take those possibilities seriously.

Applied to generative AI, this principle reverses the emphasis of MacKenzie's diagnosis.

Instead of beginning with the assumption that automation endangers meaningful human activity, it begins by asking how new technical capabilities can preserve, redistribute, or increase the forms of agency people value.

Risks remain relevant, but they are not evidence that the new capability is intrinsically impoverishing.

The inability to reach a mountain summit without a difficult climb may once have been a necessary condition of the experience.

Removing that necessity does not establish that society has lost something valuable.

It may establish that more people can now reach the summit while those who love climbing remain free to climb.

## 12. Conclusion: The Summit Is Not the Climb

MacKenzie's account of AI and valuing practices draws attention to two genuine features of human life.

We often care about the processes through which objects come into being, and our commitments can be affected by the recognition or indifference of others.

Neither observation, however, establishes that generative AI poses a general existential threat to human valuing.

The forward-looking argument risks conflating the replacement of established methods with the destruction of the values those methods once realized. The distinction between instrumental and constitutive difficulty demonstrates why this inference is unwarranted.

The backward-looking argument risks treating differences between creators' and audiences' evaluative interests as evidence of defective appreciation. Yet the intrinsic value of making a work need not depend upon an audience sharing the creator's reasons for making it. Nor does a person's investment in a difficult skill create an unrestricted entitlement to social recognition.

Foucault and Bourdieu further illuminate the possibility that some anxieties surrounding automation concern the redistribution of cultural authority and the loss of exclusivity attached to historically scarce competencies.

These possibilities warrant examination without attributing disreputable motives to those who defend traditional creative practices.

Generative AI can produce shallow, misleading, or uninspired work. So can human beings. It can facilitate thoughtless communication, but thoughtlessness is not a novel technological achievement. It can remove meaningful activities from particular institutional settings, but it can also permit new forms of creativity and make previously inaccessible activities available to many more people.

Whether these developments improve human flourishing depends upon the goals, capabilities, practices, and institutions involved.

The philosophical task is to evaluate those effects rather than identify value with inherited technical constraints.

Return, finally, to Grouse Mountain.

The hiker who loves the Grouse Grind is not diminished by the visitor who takes the gondola. The visitor who wants the view is not morally or aesthetically inferior for declining to climb. The presence of both routes allows different people to pursue different goods without making one person's preferred difficulty compulsory for everyone else.

Likewise, the writer who loves constructing sentences may continue writing without AI. The storyteller who loves developing ideas may use AI to realize them. The reader may evaluate the story according to the experience it provides. None of these participants must surrender the value of their chosen activity merely because another participant employs different means.

**The preservation of human creativity does not require the preservation of every difficulty that once constrained it.**

Technological progress can separate the destination from the journey, allowing individuals to decide which parts of the experience they value.

The summit is not the climb.

And making the summit accessible to more people does not make climbing worthless.
